\begin{remindbox}
\small In Lesson 1.1 an \textbf{expression} was a recipe. In Lesson 1.2 an \textbf{equation} was a
\emph{question} with one, no, or infinitely many answers. A \textbf{function} is neither: it is a
\emph{machine} that takes an input and returns an output --- and its promise is that every input
gets \textbf{exactly one} output. Two inputs may share an output; one input may never have two. That
single sentence is the whole definition, and the \textbf{vertical line test} is that sentence drawn.
\\[3pt]
Function notation is how you speak to the machine. ``$f(3)=0$'' and ``the point $(3,0)$ is on the
graph'' say the same thing. Going \emph{forward} --- given $x$, find $f(x)$ --- is arithmetic. Going
\emph{backward} --- given $f(x)$, find $x$ --- is solving an equation, which is all of Lesson 1.2.
So the \textbf{zero} of a function, the solution of $f(x)=0$, and the \textbf{$x$-intercept} of its
graph are three names for one thing.
\\[3pt]
Today you meet three families and ask each the same six questions: domain, range, zeros,
intercepts, rate of change, increasing or decreasing. \textbf{Linear} functions add the same amount
every step. \textbf{Quadratic} functions turn around at a vertex, so their range stops somewhere.
\textbf{Exponential} functions multiply by the same amount every step --- which is why they have no
zeros at all, and why they eventually beat everything else no matter how far behind they start.
\end{remindbox}
