\begin{headlinebox}{forestbg}
\small\textbf{One In, One Out --- Three Families, One Set of Questions.} Every item on this page
asks one of the same six questions: \emph{is it a function? what is the domain? the range? the
zeros? the intercepts? how fast is it changing?} With your partner, say out loud which question you
are being asked \emph{before} you start computing. Argue about the ones you disagree on first.
\end{headlinebox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching Proficiency},

\textbf{Part 1 --- Write the line. Nobody is telling you which method.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=6pt]
  \item Through $(-2,7)$ and $(2,-1)$. \qquad $m=$ \ans{$-2$} \qquad Equation: \ans{$y=-2x+3$}
  \item Slope $\tfrac23$, through $(3,-4)$. Give it in \emph{point-slope} form first, then
        slope-intercept.
        \\[2pt]
        Point-slope: \ans{$y+4=\tfrac23(x-3)$} \qquad Slope-intercept: \ans{$y=\tfrac23x-6$}
  \item Through $(2,1)$, \textbf{parallel} to $3x+y=8$. \; \emph{(Rewrite the given line first.)}
        \\[2pt]
        Given line's slope: \ans{$-3$} \qquad Equation: \ans{$y=-3x+7$}
  \item Through $(2,1)$, \textbf{perpendicular} to $3x+y=8$. \qquad Equation:
        \ans{$y=\tfrac13x+\tfrac13$}
  \item Which of items 1--4 describes a line that is \emph{not} a function? \ans{none of them}
        \; (Careful --- this one is a trap.)
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Part 2 --- A quadratic doing a job.} A rectangular vegetable bed is built against a barn
wall, so only three sides are fenced. With $x$ metres of width, the area in square metres is
$A(x)=x(6-x)$.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}c l@{}}
\ctxgrid{\draw[forest, very thick, domain=0:6, smooth, variable=\x] plot ({\x},{\x*(6-\x)});
         \fill[keyred] (3,9) circle (2.2pt);
         \fill[keyred] (0,0) circle (2.2pt);
         \fill[keyred] (6,0) circle (2.2pt);}
&
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.54\linewidth}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\hline
Zeros of $A$ & \ans{$x=0$ and $x=6$} \\
Vertex & \ans{$(3,9)$} \\
Max or min? & \ans{maximum} \\
Domain \emph{here} & \ans{$0<x<6$} \\
Range \emph{here} & \ans{$0<A\le 9$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{minipage}
\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
What width gives the largest bed, and how large is it? Answer in a sentence with units.
\ansline{A width of $3$ metres gives the largest bed, $9$ square metres. (The vertex input answers
\emph{where}; the vertex output answers \emph{how much}.)}
\\[2pt]
The zeros are $x=0$ and $x=6$. Explain what each one \emph{means about the bed} --- why is neither
a useful answer?
\ansline{At $x=0$ the bed has no width and at $x=6$ it has no depth --- in both cases the area is
zero, so there is no bed at all. They are honest solutions of $A(x)=0$ and useless as designs, which
is why the domain in context is the \emph{open} interval $0<x<6$.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Part 3 --- An exponential doing a job.} A rumour spreads: $3$ people know it at
$8{:}00$~a.m., and the number \emph{triples} every hour. Let $N(t)=3\cdot 3^{\,t}$, with $t$ in hours
after $8{:}00$.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|c|c|c|c@{}}
\hline
$t$ & $0$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$ \\
\hline
$N(t)$ & \ans{$3$} & \ans{$9$} & \ans{$27$} & \ans{$81$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
In $N(t)=ab^{\,t}$: \; $a=$ \ans{$3$} and it means \ans{how many knew at $8{:}00$}; \;
$b=$ \ans{$3$} and it means \ans{the number triples each hour}
\\[2pt]
At what time do $243$ people know? \ans{$12{:}00$ noon ($t=4$)} \\
Is there any time at which \emph{nobody} knows the rumour, according to this model? \ans{No} \;
Explain.
\ansline{$N(t)=0$ has no solution: tripling a positive number never gives zero, and running the
model backwards divides by $3$ forever ($1$, $\frac13$, $\frac19$, \dots) --- shrinking toward zero
without arriving. The model says the rumour always existed, which is exactly where the model stops
being realistic.}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier E --- Extension},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{Part 1 --- Build a function from conditions only.} You are told just this: $f$ is
\textbf{linear}, $f(2)=7$, and $f(5)=16$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item Find the slope. \ans{$3$} \; What two \emph{points} did you use? \ans{$(2,7)$ and $(5,16)$}
  \item Write the rule $f(x)=$ \ans{$3x+1$}
  \item Find $f(0)$. \ans{$1$} \quad Find $x$ with $f(x)=100$. \ans{$33$}
  \item Why was the word \textbf{linear} essential? Give a different function through the same two
        points to prove the rule would not be determined without it.
        \ansline{Infinitely many functions pass through two given points --- ``linear'' is what
        pins it down to exactly one. For instance $g(x)=x^2-4x+11$ also has $g(2)=7$ and $g(5)=16$,
        but $g(0)=11$, not $1$. Two points determine a \emph{line}; on their own they determine
        nothing.}
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Part 3 --- The overtake.} Extend the Hook's race.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.45}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|c|c|c|c|c|c|c@{}}
\hline
$x$ & $0$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$ & $4$ & $5$ & $6$ \\
\hline
$x^2$ & $0$ & $1$ & $4$ & \ans{$9$} & \ans{$16$} & \ans{$25$} & \ans{$36$} \\
$2^x$ & $1$ & $2$ & $4$ & \ans{$8$} & \ans{$16$} & \ans{$32$} & \ans{$64$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item At which two inputs are they \emph{equal}? \ans{$x=2$ and $x=4$}
  \item Between those two, which one is ahead? \ans{$x^2$ (at $x=3$: $9>8$)} \; After the second one?
        \ans{$2^x$, permanently}
  \item Each step, $x^2$ gains a growing amount and $2^x$ \emph{doubles}. Explain in two sentences
        why doubling must eventually win, no matter how far behind it starts.
        \ansline{Going from $x$ to $x+1$ adds $2x+1$ to $x^2$ --- an amount that grows, but only by
        $2$ each step. Doubling instead adds the \emph{entire current value}, which is itself
        growing without bound, so once $2^x$ catches up its head start compounds and the gap only
        widens.}
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 4 --- Two tests, two questions.} The vertical line test decides whether a graph is a
function. Now consider a \textbf{horizontal} line test.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item The graph of $y=x^2$ is crossed \emph{twice} by the horizontal line $y=4$. Is $y=x^2$ still
        a function? \ans{Yes} \; Why does that not break the promise?
        \ansline{Two crossings of a horizontal line means two \emph{inputs} ($-2$ and $2$) share one
        \emph{output}, which was never forbidden. The promise is that one input has one output.}
  \item The graph of $x=3$ is crossed \emph{once} by every horizontal line, and it still is not a
        function. What exactly does each test measure?
        \ansline{The vertical line test asks whether any one \emph{input} has more than one output
        --- that is the definition of a function, so it is the only test that decides the question.
        The horizontal line test asks whether any one \emph{output} comes from more than one input;
        it says nothing about being a function, and answering ``no'' to it is a stronger property
        with its own name, which you will meet in Unit 6 when functions get undone.}
\end{enumerate}
