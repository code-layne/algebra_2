\begin{notesbox}{Practice}
Every answer with exponents uses \textbf{positive} exponents only. Every radical is in
\textbf{simplest form}. Every factorization is \textbf{complete}.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=11pt]
  \item \textbf{Translate.} Let $n$ be the number.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X l@{}}
    \hline
    (a) \; Twelve less than a number & \ans{$n-12$} \\
    (b) \; The product of 8 and the quantity of a number plus 3 & \ans{$8(n+3)$} \\
    (c) \; The sum of a number and its square & \ans{$n+n^2$} \\
    (d) \; Six less than the quotient of a number and 5 & \ans{$\frac{n}{5}-6$} \\
    \hline
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}

  \item \textbf{Evaluate.} Substitute first. Absolute value bars and radical signs are grouping
        symbols --- finish the inside before you do anything else.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X l@{}}
    \hline
    \textbf{Expression} & \textbf{Substitute and simplify} & \textbf{Value} \\
    \hline
    (a) \; $x^2+6x-4$ at $x=-3$ & \ans{$(-3)^2+6(-3)-4=9-18-4$} & \ans{$-13$} \\
    (b) \; $|4x-9|$ at $x=\tfrac12$ & \ans{$|2-9|=|{-7}|$} & \ans{$7$} \\
    (c) \; $\sqrt{2x+7}$ at $x=\tfrac92$ & \ans{$\sqrt{9+7}=\sqrt{16}$} & \ans{$4$} \\
    (d) \; $\sqrt[3]{x}-2x$ at $x=-27$ & \ans{$\sqrt[3]{-27}-2(-27)=-3+54$} & \ans{$51$} \\
    \hline
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}

  \item \textbf{Laws of exponents.}
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}l l@{}}
    (a) \; $(4x^3)(3x^5)=$ \ans{$12x^8$} &
    (b) \; $(2a^4b)^3=$ \ans{$8a^{12}b^3$} \\
    (c) \; \TallMath{\frac{x^7}{x^{-2}}} $=$ \ans{$x^9$} &
    (d) \; $(5m^0n^2)^2=$ \ans{$25n^4$} \\
    (e) \; \TallMath{\frac{24a^3b^{-4}}{8a^{-1}b^2}} $=$ \ans{$\dfrac{3a^4}{b^6}$} &
    (f) \; \TallMath{\frac{10x^{-2}y^5}{15x^3y^{-1}}} $=$ \ans{$\dfrac{2y^6}{3x^5}$} \\
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}

  \item \textbf{Polynomial operations.}
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=7pt]
      \item $(2x^2+7x-5)+(4x^2-3x+8)=$ \ans{$6x^2+4x+3$}
      \item $(x^2-4x+9)-(5x^2-x-3)=$ \ans{$-4x^2-3x+12$}
      \item $(x-7)(x+7)=$ \ans{$x^2-49$} \quad Which pattern is this?
            \ans{difference of squares}
      \item $(3x+2)(x^2-4x+5)=$ \ans{$3x^3-10x^2+7x+10$}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Factor completely.} Four of these six have a GCF to remove first --- and every one
        of those four still factors after the GCF comes out.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}l l@{}}
    (a) \; $x^2+11x+24=$ \ans{$(x+3)(x+8)$} &
    (b) \; $4x^2-100=$ \ans{$4(x+5)(x-5)$} \\
    (c) \; $x^2-3x-40=$ \ans{$(x-8)(x+5)$} &
    (d) \; $2x^3-8x=$ \ans{$2x(x+2)(x-2)$} \\
    (e) \; $5x^2+35x+50=$ \ans{$5(x+2)(x+5)$} &
    (f) \; $3x^2-6x-24=$ \ans{$3(x-4)(x+2)$} \\
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    Pick any \emph{one} of the six and check it by expanding. Which did you pick, and does it check?
    \ans{Answers vary --- e.g.\ (b): $4(x^2-25)=4x^2-100$ \; \checkmark}

  \item \textbf{Divide.}
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.9}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}l l@{}}
    (a) \; \TallMath{\frac{18x^5-12x^3+6x^2}{6x^2}} $=$ \ans{$3x^3-2x+1$} &
    (b) \; \TallMath{\frac{x^2+2x-35}{x+7}} $=$ \ans{$x-5$}, \; $x\ne$ \ans{$-7$} \\
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}

  \item \textbf{Radicals and rational exponents.} Simplify (a)--(d). For (e) and (f), rewrite the
        expression in the \emph{other} notation.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
    (a) \; $\sqrt{50}=$ \ans{$5\sqrt2$} &
    (b) \; $\sqrt[3]{24}=$ \ans{$2\sqrt[3]{3}$} &
    (c) \; $4\sqrt3+7\sqrt3-\sqrt3=$ \ans{$10\sqrt3$} \\
    (d) \; $\sqrt{20}+\sqrt{45}=$ \ans{$5\sqrt5$} &
    (e) \; $\sqrt{13}=$ \ans{$13^{1/2}$} &
    (f) \; $x^{1/3}=$ \ans{$\sqrt[3]{x}$} \\
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    Item (d) looked impossible at first, then worked. Why?
    \ans{After simplifying, $\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt5$ and $\sqrt{45}=3\sqrt5$ --- they became like radicals.}

  \item \textbf{Explain.} A classmate claims two expressions are equivalent ``because I checked, and
        they both give the same thing at $x=0$.'' Explain why that is not a proof --- and describe
        what \emph{would} settle the question. Then give one pair of expressions that agree at
        $x=0$ but are \emph{not} equivalent.
        \ansline{Equivalence is a claim about \emph{every} legal value of $x$, and $x=0$ is one
        value out of infinitely many. Agreement there rules out nothing.}
        \ansline{What settles it is algebra: rewrite one expression, using rules that hold for an
        unspecified $x$, until it becomes the other. That covers every value at once.}
        \ansline{Sample pair: $x^2+5$ and $5-x^2$ --- both give $5$ at $x=0$, but at $x=2$ they give
        $9$ and $1$. (Also fine: $(x+1)^2$ and $x^2+1$, both $1$ at $x=0$.)}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{extensionbox}
\textbf{The equivalence audit.} For each pair, decide. If they \textbf{are} equivalent, prove it
with algebra --- rewrite one side until it becomes the other. If they are \textbf{not}, kill it with
a single value of $x$ and show the arithmetic. One row is trickier than it looks.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.7}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}c l l X@{}}
\hline
 & \textbf{First} & \textbf{Second} & \textbf{Proof, or one disproving value} \\
\hline
(a) & $(x-4)^2$ & $x^2-16$ & \ans{No: $(x-4)^2=x^2-8x+16$. At $x=1$, $9\ne-15$.} \\
(b) & $(x+2)(x-5)$ & $x^2-3x-10$ & \ans{Yes: expanding gives $x^2-5x+2x-10=x^2-3x-10$.} \\
(c) & $\sqrt{x^2}$ & $x$ & \ans{No: at $x=-3$, $\sqrt{9}=3$ but $x=-3$.} \\
(d) & \TallMath{\frac{2x+6}{2}} & $x+3$ & \ans{Yes: split the fraction, $\frac{2x}{2}+\frac{6}{2}=x+3$.} \\
(e) & \TallMath{\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}} & $x+3$ & \ans{Equal for every $x\ne3$; at $x=3$ the first is undefined.} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Row (c) fails for \emph{every} negative $x$. Write the rule that actually describes
        $\sqrt{x^2}$ for all real $x$. \ans{$\sqrt{x^2}=|x|$}
  \item Row (e) is the tricky one: the two agree everywhere \emph{except} at one value of $x$, where
        one of them does not exist at all. Which value, and which expression breaks?
        \ansline{At $x=3$. The first expression breaks --- its denominator is $0$, so it is
        undefined there, while $x+3$ happily gives $6$.}
  \item So ``equivalent'' has a hidden requirement you have been using all along without saying it.
        State it.
        \ansline{The two expressions must agree at every value that is \emph{legal for both} --- so
        they must have the same domain. Cancelling a factor can quietly enlarge the domain, which is
        exactly what happened in (e).}
\end{enumerate}
\end{extensionbox}

\begin{spiralbox}
\textbf{Coming next --- Lesson 1.2: Equations and Inequalities.} The second Algebra 1 domain area,
and every rewriting rule from today goes straight to work there. Distributing clears the
parentheses; combining like terms collapses the sides; the \emph{zero product property} from Lesson
1.0 turns your factoring into a way to \textbf{solve} quadratics --- which is exactly why
``factored'' is not good enough and ``factored \emph{completely}'' is. And when the quadratic
formula appears, the radical it hands you will be one you now know how to simplify.
\end{spiralbox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Grade items 5 and 8 first.} They are the two that predict Lesson 1.2. In item 5, watch for
answers that are factored but not \emph{completely} --- $4(x^2-25)$, $2x(x^2-4)$ --- and mark them
partially correct with that phrase written out, because in 1.2 the same stop costs two solutions
rather than one step. In item 8, a student who can articulate ``one value cannot establish every
value'' has the intellectual content of the lesson even if the computation is shaky.

\textbf{Item 4(b) is the leading-minus item.} The wrong answer to look for is $-4x^2-5x+6$ or
$-4x^2-3x+6$ --- a minus distributed to some but not all of the second polynomial. Do not accept
``I forgot''; have them rewrite it as $+(-1)(5x^2-x-3)$ once, out loud.

\textbf{Item 3(e)--(f) are the two negative-exponent monomial ratios (A.EO.3b).} Both require
subtracting a negative, which is where the errors live: in (e), $3-(-1)=4$ and $-4-2=-6$; in (f),
$-2-3=-5$ and $5-(-1)=6$. Have students who miss these write the factor-counting expansion out once.

\textbf{The Extension is where the real Algebra 2 lives.} Rows (c) and (e) are both previews. Row
(c), $\sqrt{x^2}=|x|$, is the Unit 6 radical subtlety in embryo --- do not let a strong class talk
you out of the absolute value. Row (e) introduces \emph{domain} as part of what ``equivalent''
means, which is the idea Unit 5 (rational functions) is built on; it is genuinely hard, and a
student who only notices that something goes wrong at $x=3$ has done well.
\end{teachernote}
