\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=forestbg, colframe=forest, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students can \emph{translate} a verbal situation into an algebraic
    expression and \emph{evaluate} it --- including absolute values, square roots, and cube roots ---
    for given replacement values; apply the \emph{laws of exponents} to simplify multivariable
    expressions and monomial ratios with integer exponents; add, subtract, multiply, \emph{factor},
    and divide polynomial expressions in one variable; and simplify \emph{radical} expressions,
    converting freely between radicals and rational exponents. Throughout, students justify that two
    differently written expressions are \textbf{equivalent} --- and know that a single replacement
    value can prove they are not.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Standards (2023 VA SOL --- Algebra 1, reviewed):} this lesson reviews the entire
    \textbf{Expressions and Operations} domain. \textbf{A.EO.1} --- translate between verbal
    situations and algebraic expressions (a), and evaluate expressions containing absolute value,
    square roots, and cube roots at rational replacement values (b). \textbf{A.EO.2} --- sums and
    differences (a) and products (b) of polynomials in one variable, complete factoring of first-
    and second-degree polynomials with integral coefficients, GCF first (c), quotients using
    monomial, binomial, or completely factored divisors (d), and demonstrating equality of quadratic
    expressions written in different forms (e). \textbf{A.EO.3} --- deriving and applying the laws of
    exponents (a) and simplifying multivariable expressions and monomial ratios with integer
    exponents (b). \textbf{A.EO.4} --- simplifying square roots of whole numbers (a) and cube roots
    of integers (b), adding, subtracting, and multiplying numeric radicals (c), and rational
    exponents of $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac13$ (d).
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Prerequisite:} Lesson 1.0. The distributive property is the engine of every product in
    Section~3, and the inverse properties are what the laws of exponents in Section~2 actually
    \emph{are}. Students should still be naming their justifications.
\end{tcolorbox}

\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Ideas \& Skills} & \textbf{Key Understandings (the ``why'')} \\[2pt]
\hline
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item \textbf{Translate} a verbal quantitative situation into an algebraic expression, and read one back into words.
  \item \textbf{Evaluate} an expression at given replacement values --- including $|\,\cdot\,|$, $\sqrt{\ }$, and $\sqrt[3]{\ }$, with rational inputs.
  \item Apply the \textbf{laws of exponents} (product, quotient, power, zero, negative) to multivariable expressions and monomial ratios.
  \item \textbf{Add, subtract, and multiply} polynomials in one variable; recognize the difference of squares and perfect-square patterns.
  \item \textbf{Factor completely} --- GCF \emph{first}, then trinomial or difference-of-squares.
  \item \textbf{Divide} polynomials by a monomial or a factored divisor.
  \item \textbf{Simplify radicals} and convert between $\sqrt[n]{a}$ and $a^{1/n}$.
  \item \textbf{Justify equivalence} --- and disprove it with one replacement value.
\end{itemize}
&
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item \emph{An expression is a recipe, not a question.} It has no solution --- it has a \emph{value}, and only once you supply the inputs.
  \item Order of operations is not etiquette; it is what makes an expression name \emph{one} number instead of several.
  \item The exponent laws are not memorized facts. Every one of them is what happens when you \emph{write out the factors and count}.
  \item Factoring and multiplying are the same trip in opposite directions --- which is why you can always check a factorization by expanding it.
  \item ``Completely'' is the whole content of the factoring standard: GCF first, then keep going until nothing factors further.
  \item A radical and a fractional exponent are two spellings of one idea, and the exponent spelling is the one that makes the laws apply.
  \item Equivalence is a claim about \emph{every} input. Testing one value can \emph{disprove} it outright, but confirming one value proves nothing.
\end{itemize}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{sky}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\textbf{Expression} & A combination of numbers, variables, and operations that names a number once values are supplied. It has no equals sign and is never ``solved.'' \\
\textbf{Term / coefficient} & A term is a piece separated by $+$ or $-$; its coefficient is the numerical factor. \\
\textbf{Evaluate} & Replace each variable with a given value and simplify to a single number. \\
\textbf{Polynomial} & A sum of terms of the form $ax^n$ with $n$ a whole number. Named by degree (linear, quadratic, cubic) and by term count (monomial, binomial, trinomial). \\
\textbf{Degree} & The largest exponent on the variable. It determines the shape of everything in Units 2--4. \\
\textbf{Laws of exponents} & \TallMath{a^m a^n=a^{m+n};\quad \frac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n};\quad (a^m)^n=a^{mn};\quad a^0=1;\quad a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}} \\
\textbf{Difference of squares} & $a^2-b^2=(a+b)(a-b)$ --- the single most useful factoring pattern in the course. \\
\textbf{Perfect square trinomial} & $a^2\pm 2ab+b^2=(a\pm b)^2$. Recognizing it is what makes completing the square possible in Unit 3. \\
\textbf{GCF} & The greatest common factor of every term; the \emph{first} thing to remove when factoring. \\
\textbf{Factor completely} & Keep factoring until no factor can be factored further over the integers. \\
\textbf{Radical / radicand} & In $\sqrt[n]{a}$, the index is $n$ and the radicand is $a$. \\
\textbf{Simplest radical form} & No perfect-power factor remains under the radical (and, later, no radical in a denominator). \\
\textbf{Rational exponent} & $a^{1/2}=\sqrt{a}$ and $a^{1/3}=\sqrt[3]{a}$ --- the same number, spelled so the exponent laws apply. \\
\textbf{Equivalent expressions} & Two expressions that produce the same value for \emph{every} legal replacement value. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{fixedskillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{forestbg}
    The Warm-Up rehearses the three things this lesson is built on. \textbf{(1)} Two order-of-
    operations evaluations, one containing $|{-}6|$ and one containing $\sqrt{16}$ and $\sqrt[3]{27}$,
    with a negative substituted into $x^2$ so the room can argue about $-3^2$ versus $(-3)^2$ ---
    that argument is the debrief. \textbf{(2)} An exponent pattern table ($2^5\cdot2^3$ written out
    as factors and counted) that has students \emph{re-derive} the product law rather than recall it,
    exactly as \textbf{A.EO.3a} asks. \textbf{(3)} One distribution, $4(2x-5)$, and one area model
    for $(x+3)(x+2)$ --- the same property doing the work in both, which is the bridge from Lesson
    1.0 to today's polynomial products. Debrief item~2 by asking ``where did the $+$ in the exponent
    come from?'' and do not accept ``that's the rule.''
\end{fixedskillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{forestbg}
    Write four pairs on the board and ask a single question: \textbf{``Equivalent, or not?''}
    \emph{(a)} $2^3\cdot 2^4$ and $2^{12}$; \emph{(b)} $(x+3)(x-3)$ and $x^2-9$; \emph{(c)}
    $(x+5)^2$ and $x^2+25$; \emph{(d)} $\sqrt{9+16}$ and $\sqrt9+\sqrt{16}$. Give the room ninety
    seconds and no instructions. Students will reach for \emph{testing a value}, which is the habit
    from 1.0 --- so let them, then use what they find. Pairs (a), (c), and (d) each die to a single
    number ($2^3\cdot2^4=128\ne 4096$; at $x=1$, $36\ne 26$; $\sqrt{25}=5\ne 3+4$). Pair (b)
    survives every test they try. Now land the asymmetry, which is the intellectual content of the
    day: \textbf{``One bad value \emph{disproves} equivalence forever. But no number of good values
    ever \emph{proves} it --- for that you need algebra.''} Multiply $(x+3)(x-3)$ out on the spot to
    show what proof looks like. Then frame the lesson: \emph{everything today is a rule for
    rewriting an expression into an equivalent one --- and the reason we can trust those rules is
    that each was proved once, so we never have to test again.} Note that (c) and (d) are the two
    most common errors of the entire year, and both are the same mistake: assuming an operation
    distributes when it does not.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{forestbg}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. Translating and evaluating.} Start with translation, because it is the standard students
have practiced least: build a small two-way table (\emph{seven less than twice a number} $\to 2n-7$;
\emph{the quotient of a number and 4, increased by 9} $\to \frac n4+9$), and insist on reading the
finished expression back into words. Flag the two order traps --- ``less than'' reverses the
subtraction, and ``the quantity'' signals parentheses. Then evaluate. Do $3x^2-4x+1$ at $x=-2$
slowly, and stop the room on the difference between $-3^2=-9$ and $(-3)^2=9$: the exponent binds
only to what it touches, and the parentheses are what change that. Then evaluate an expression with
$|2x-7|$, one with $\sqrt{x+9}$, and one with $\sqrt[3]{x}$, at rational values, since
\textbf{A.EO.1b} names all three. Emphasize: absolute value and radicals are \emph{grouping
symbols} --- finish the inside first.

\textbf{2. The laws of exponents --- derived, not memorized.} Return to Warm-Up item~2 and derive
each law by writing out factors and counting. $a^3a^2$ has five $a$'s, so exponents \emph{add};
$\frac{a^5}{a^2}$ cancels two, so exponents \emph{subtract}; $(a^3)^2$ is three $a$'s twice, so
exponents \emph{multiply}. Then get $a^0=1$ and $a^{-n}=\frac1{a^n}$ the honest way --- by
continuing the quotient law past where it ``should'' stop: $\frac{a^3}{a^3}=a^0$ and also $=1$;
$\frac{a^2}{a^5}=a^{-3}$ and also $=\frac{1}{a^3}$. Never present these as arbitrary conventions.
Then apply to multivariable expressions and monomial ratios, e.g. $\frac{12x^5y^{-2}}{3x^2y^4}$,
per \textbf{A.EO.3b}, and require positive exponents in the final answer.

\columnbreak

\textbf{3. Polynomial operations and factoring.} Add and subtract by combining like terms, with the
whole difficulty concentrated in \emph{one} place: distributing the minus sign across every term of
the second polynomial. Multiply with the distributive property (area model on the board for
$(x+3)(x+2)$), then do $(2x-1)(3x^2+x-4)$ so students see the method scale past two terms.
Highlight the two patterns worth recognizing on sight --- difference of squares and perfect-square
trinomial --- and connect them back to Hook items (b) and (c). Then reverse the arrow: factoring is
multiplying backwards, so \emph{every factorization is checkable by expanding}. Insist on the order:
\textbf{GCF first, always}, then the pattern or trinomial method, then check whether any factor can
still be factored --- that is what ``completely'' means in \textbf{A.EO.2c}. Finish with division by
a monomial (split the fraction, one term at a time) and by a factored divisor (cancel common
factors), and note the restriction that cancelling assumes the divisor is nonzero.

\textbf{4. Radicals and rational exponents.} Simplify $\sqrt{72}$ by pulling out the largest perfect
square, and $\sqrt[3]{54}$ by pulling out the largest perfect cube --- the method is identical, only
the index changes. Add and subtract only \emph{like} radicals ($3\sqrt5+2\sqrt5$ works;
$3\sqrt5+2\sqrt3$ does not, and this is the same idea as combining like terms). Multiply with
$\sqrt a\cdot\sqrt b=\sqrt{ab}$. Close by connecting the two notations: $a^{1/2}=\sqrt a$ and
$a^{1/3}=\sqrt[3]{a}$, so $\sqrt[3]{8}=8^{1/3}=(2^3)^{1/3}=2$ --- the exponent law from Section~2
doing the work. State plainly why this matters: the rational-exponent spelling is what lets the
exponent laws reach radicals at all, and it is the notation Unit 6 is built on.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
Circulate during the notes practice and Tier work. Watch for and correct:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep]
  \item evaluating $-x^2$ at $x=-3$ as $9$ --- the exponent binds only to $x$, so the answer is $-9$; this is the most common evaluation error in the course and it recurs in every unit;
  \item translating ``seven less than twice a number'' as $7-2n$ --- ``less than'' reverses the order;
  \item subtracting polynomials without distributing the minus past the \emph{first} term of the second polynomial;
  \item adding exponents when multiplying \emph{coefficients} too ($3x^2\cdot 4x^3 = 12x^5$, not $7x^5$ or $12x^6$);
  \item writing $(x+5)^2=x^2+25$ --- Hook item (c); squaring does not distribute over addition, and the missing $10x$ is the whole point;
  \item writing $\sqrt{a+b}=\sqrt a+\sqrt b$ --- Hook item (d), the same error in a different costume;
  \item factoring but skipping the GCF, so $2x^2-18$ becomes $(\sqrt2 x-\ldots)$ nonsense instead of $2(x+3)(x-3)$ --- and stopping at $2(x^2-9)$, which is factored but not \emph{completely};
  \item leaving a negative exponent in a final answer, or treating $a^{-2}$ as a negative number;
  \item adding unlike radicals, $3\sqrt5+2\sqrt3=5\sqrt8$.
\end{itemize}
Cold-call prompts: ``Read that expression back to me in words.'' ``Where did the exponents' $+$
come from --- show me the factors.'' ``Is it factored, or factored \emph{completely}? How do you
know?'' ``Check it: multiply your factors back out.'' ``Give me one value of $x$ that proves those
two are not equivalent.''
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\textbf{Rewriting Without Changing the Value} activity (tiered; mirrors the notes).
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate.} Four straightforward blocks, one per notes section: translate four
phrases; evaluate four expressions at given values (one with $|\,\cdot\,|$, one with $\sqrt{\ }$,
one with a negative substituted into a squared term); apply one exponent law per item; and one
short factoring set in which every problem has a GCF to remove first. Closes with an
expand-to-check step, so students verify their own factoring instead of asking. Send here any
student who wrote $(x+5)^2=x^2+25$.

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching.} Mixed and unlabelled, which is the real difficulty: a set where
students must decide \emph{which} tool applies before using it. Includes a polynomial subtraction
with a leading minus, a monomial ratio with negative exponents, a trinomial and a
difference-of-squares to factor completely (one of each requiring a GCF first), a division by a
factored divisor, and $\sqrt{72}$ and $\sqrt[3]{54}$ side by side so the identical method is
visible. Ends with two justification items: show two given expressions \emph{are} equivalent by
algebra, and show two others are \emph{not} with one value.

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension.} Structure work. A rectangle of area $x^2+7x+12$ and one dimension
$x+3$: find the other by dividing, then state what $x$ cannot be and why. A ``spot the error'' set
of four student solutions --- one per classic misconception from Active Monitoring --- where the
error must be named, not just fixed. Then the strongest item on the page: given
$\frac{4^{x+1}}{2^{2x}}$, simplify to a single power of $2$, which forces students to notice
$4=2^2$ before any law applies. Closes by asking why one confirming value never proves equivalence
while one contradicting value always disproves it.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (independent, no notes): three items. \textbf{(1)} Evaluate $2x^2-5x+|x-8|$ at
$x=-3$ --- one item that catches the $-x^2$ sign error, the order-of-operations habit, and absolute
value as a grouping symbol all at once. \textbf{(2)} Factor $3x^2-27$ completely and check by
expanding; the GCF is required, and stopping at $3(x^2-9)$ is the diagnostic. \textbf{(3)} An
\textbf{SOL-style multiple-choice} item: simplify $\frac{15x^4y^{-1}}{5x^2y^3}$ (answer
$\frac{3x^2}{y^4}$), with distractors that each break exactly one law --- subtract exponents the
wrong way / divide the exponents / leave the negative exponent in the numerator. Collect and sort
into ``got it / sign-and-order slips / factoring not complete / exponent laws.'' The last two piles
matter most: Lesson 1.2 solves quadratics by factoring, so incomplete factoring becomes a wrong
\emph{answer} there rather than a lost point, and Units 3--6 lean on the exponent laws continuously.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} four translation items and four evaluations (covering absolute value, square
root, and cube root at rational replacement values); an exponent-law set including two monomial
ratios with negative exponents; a polynomial set with one addition, one subtraction with a leading
minus, and two products (one a difference-of-squares pattern, one a binomial times a trinomial); a
factor-completely set of six in which four require a GCF first; two divisions; a radical set
including $\sqrt{50}$, $\sqrt[3]{24}$, a sum of like radicals, and two rational-exponent
conversions; and one written item asking students to explain why testing $x=0$ cannot prove two
expressions equivalent. \textbf{Extension:} the equivalence audit --- five pairs of expressions
where students must either prove equivalence algebraically or produce a single disproving value,
including $(x-4)^2$ vs.\ $x^2-16$ and $\sqrt{x^2}$ vs.\ $x$ (which fails for every negative $x$ and
previews the Unit 6 absolute-value subtlety). \textbf{Preview:} Lesson 1.2, \emph{Equations and
Inequalities} --- the second Algebra 1 domain area, where every rewriting rule from today gets put
to work: distributing to clear parentheses, factoring to solve quadratics via the zero product
property from 1.0, and the quadratic formula, whose radical you will now be able to simplify.
\end{skillbox}
