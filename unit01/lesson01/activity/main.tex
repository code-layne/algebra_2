\begin{headlinebox}{forestbg}
\small\textbf{Rewriting Without Changing the Value.} Every move today turns an expression into a
\emph{different-looking} expression with the \emph{same} value. With your partner, do the work ---
then check it the way this lesson lets you: multiply a factorization back out, or test a value to
kill a claim. Argue about the ones you disagree on before you write anything down.
\end{headlinebox}

\vspace{0.06in}

Two of those four have a trap in the \emph{order}. Which two, and what is the trap?

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 2 --- Evaluate.} Substitute first, then follow the order of operations.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X l@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Expression} & \textbf{Substitute and simplify} & \textbf{Value} \\
\hline
(a) \; $4x-7$ at $x=3$ & \blank{5.6cm} & \blank{1.4cm} \\
(b) \; $-x^2+2x$ at $x=-5$ & \blank{5.6cm} & \blank{1.4cm} \\
(c) \; $|3x+1|$ at $x=-4$ & \blank{5.6cm} & \blank{1.4cm} \\
(d) \; $\sqrt{x}+\sqrt[3]{x}$ at $x=64$ & \blank{5.6cm} & \blank{1.4cm} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
In (b), is the answer positive or negative? \blank{2.4cm} \; Say why in one sentence.
\writeline

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 3 --- One law at a time.} Each item below needs exactly \emph{one} law of exponents.
Be ready to name the one you used.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
(a) \; $x^5\cdot x^7=$ \blank{2.0cm} &
(b) \; $\dfrac{y^9}{y^4}=$ \blank{2.0cm} &
(c) \; $(m^3)^5=$ \blank{2.0cm} \\[8pt]
(d) \; $(3k)^2=$ \blank{2.0cm} &
(e) \; $7^0=$ \blank{2.0cm} &
(f) \; $x^{-3}=$ \blank{2.0cm} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Which item did you have to think hardest about, and why? \blank{7.0cm}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 4 --- Factor completely. Every one of these has a GCF to pull out first.}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l@{}}
(a) \; $4x^2+10x=$ \blank{4.0cm} &
(b) \; $3x^2-12=$ \blank{4.0cm} \\[8pt]
(c) \; $2x^2+10x+12=$ \blank{4.0cm} &
(d) \; $5x^3-20x=$ \blank{4.0cm} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textbf{Check yourself.} Multiply your answer to (b) back out. Do you land on $3x^2-12$?
\blank{5.0cm} \\
That check works on \emph{every} factoring problem --- which is why you never have to ask whether a
factorization is right.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching Proficiency},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{Part 1 --- Mixed, and nobody is telling you which tool to use.} That is the whole
difficulty. Decide first, then work. Every answer with radicals must be in simplest form, and every
answer with exponents must use \textbf{positive} exponents only.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=9pt]
  \item $(4x^2-x+6)-(2x^2+5x-3)=$ \blank{6.0cm}
  \item \TallMath{\frac{18a^{-2}b^5}{6a^3b^{-1}}} $=$ \blank{4.4cm}
  \item Factor completely: \; $4x^2-36=$ \blank{5.0cm}
  \item Factor completely: \; $2x^2-2x-24=$ \blank{5.0cm}
  \item \TallMath{\frac{x^2-x-20}{x-5}} $=$ \blank{3.6cm} \quad and $x\ne$ \blank{1.2cm}
  \item Side by side --- \emph{same method, different index.} \\[3pt]
        $\sqrt{80}=$ \blank{3.0cm} \hfill $\sqrt[3]{40}=$ \blank{3.0cm} \\[3pt]
        What was identical about the two? \blank{6.4cm}
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Part 2 --- Prove it, or kill it.} These two items are answered in \emph{different ways} on
purpose.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=7pt, start=7]
  \item Show by \textbf{algebra} that $(2x+3)(2x-3)$ and $4x^2-9$ are equivalent. Testing values is
        not enough here --- multiply.
        \writelines{2}
  \item Show that $\sqrt{x^2+9}$ and $x+3$ are \textbf{not} equivalent, using exactly \emph{one}
        value of $x$. Show the arithmetic for both expressions at your value.
        \writelines{2}
\end{enumerate}
Why did item 7 need algebra while item 8 needed only one number? \writeline
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier E --- Extension},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{Part 1 --- A rectangle you are handed backwards.} A rectangle has area $x^2+9x+20$ square
units, and one dimension measures $x+4$ units.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Find the other dimension. \blank{4.0cm} \quad What operation did that require?
        \blank{4.0cm}
  \item What value can $x$ \emph{not} be, and why does the algebra forbid it? \blank{5.0cm}
  \item This is a real rectangle, so both dimensions must be positive lengths. What does that
        restrict $x$ to --- and why is that a \emph{stronger} restriction than your answer to (b)?
        \writeline
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 2 --- Spot the error.} Each student below made \textbf{exactly one} mistake. Circle it,
\textbf{name} what went wrong, and write the correct answer. Naming it is the part that counts.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}c l X l@{}}
\hline
 & \textbf{Student's work} & \textbf{What went wrong (name it)} & \textbf{Correct} \\
\hline

    & $\qquad =3x^2-2x+3$ & & \\

is the mistake they share? \writeline

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 3 --- The one that needs a first move nobody tells you to make.} Simplify
\TallMath{\frac{4^{x+1}}{2^{2x}}} to a single power of $2$, then to a number.
\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.94\linewidth}{\centering \emph{Hint you should not need: no exponent law applies
until the two bases are the same.}}}
\end{center}
\writelines{3}
Answer: \blank{3.0cm}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Part 4 --- The asymmetry, stated in your own words.} One \emph{contradicting} value always
disproves equivalence, but one --- or a hundred --- \emph{confirming} values never proves it.
Explain why the two directions are not symmetric. Use the word \textbf{every}.
\writelines{3}
