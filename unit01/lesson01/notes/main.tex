\begin{objectivebox}
\textbf{By the end of this lesson, I will be able to\dots}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item \emph{translate} a verbal situation into an algebraic expression and \emph{evaluate} it at given values --- including absolute values, square roots, and cube roots;
  \item apply the \emph{laws of exponents} to simplify multivariable expressions and monomial ratios, and explain where each law comes from;
  \item add, subtract, multiply, \emph{factor completely}, and divide polynomials in one variable;
  \item simplify \emph{radicals} and convert between radical and rational-exponent notation;
  \item \emph{justify} that two expressions are equivalent --- or disprove it with a single value.
\end{itemize}
\end{objectivebox}

\begin{hookbox}
\textbf{Equivalent, or not?} No rules yet --- just decide, and be ready to defend it.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}c l l X@{}}
\hline
 & \textbf{First} & \textbf{Second} & \textbf{Equivalent? How do you know?} \\
\hline
(a) & $2^3\cdot 2^4$ & $2^{12}$ & \blank{5.6cm} \\
(b) & $(x+3)(x-3)$ & $x^2-9$ & \blank{5.6cm} \\
(c) & $(x+5)^2$ & $x^2+25$ & \blank{5.6cm} \\
(d) & $\sqrt{9+16}$ & $\sqrt9+\sqrt{16}$ & \blank{5.6cm} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Three of those four die to a \emph{single} number. One survives every value you try.
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item Which one survived? \blank{2.0cm}
  \item You tested it at several values and it worked every time. Does that \textbf{prove} the two
        are equivalent? \blank{1.6cm} \; Why or why not? \writeline
\end{itemize}
\textbf{The asymmetry to remember all year:} one bad value \emph{disproves} equivalence forever, but
no number of good values ever \emph{proves} it. For proof you need algebra --- and every rule in
today's lesson is a rewriting move that was proved once, so you never have to test again.
\end{hookbox}

\begin{notesbox}{1. Translating and Evaluating}
An \textbf{expression} is a recipe for a number. It has no equals sign, so it is never
\emph{solved} --- it is \blank{2.4cm} once you supply values.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Translate.} Watch the two traps: ``less than'' \blank{2.6cm} the order, and ``the quantity''
signals \blank{2.4cm}.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X l@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Words} & \textbf{Expression} \\
\hline
Seven less than twice a number & \blank{3.0cm} \\
The quotient of a number and 4, increased by 9 & \blank{3.0cm} \\
Five times the quantity of a number decreased by 2 & \blank{3.0cm} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Now read this one \emph{back} into words: \; $3n+8$ \; is \blank{7.0cm}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Evaluate.} Replace, then follow the order of operations. Remember: absolute value bars and
radical signs are \textbf{grouping symbols} --- finish the inside first.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X l@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Expression} & \textbf{Substitute and simplify} & \textbf{Value} \\
\hline
$3x^2-4x+1$ at $x=-2$ & \blank{6.0cm} & \blank{1.4cm} \\
$|2x-7|$ at $x=1.5$ & \blank{6.0cm} & \blank{1.4cm} \\
$\sqrt{x+9}$ at $x=-\tfrac{11}{4}$ & \blank{6.0cm} & \blank{1.4cm} \\
$\sqrt[3]{x}+x$ at $x=-8$ & \blank{6.0cm} & \blank{1.4cm} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\textbf{The sign trap from the Warm-Up.} At $x=-3$: \quad $(-x)^2=$ \blank{1.4cm} \quad but
$-x^2=$ \blank{1.4cm}. \\
An exponent binds only to \blank{4.0cm}, and \blank{2.4cm} are what change that.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{2. The Laws of Exponents --- Derived, Not Memorized}
Every law below is just \textbf{counting factors}. Fill in the reason, not only the rule.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Law} & \textbf{Rule} & \textbf{Why (count the factors)} \\
\hline
Product & $a^m\cdot a^n=a^{\blank{1.0cm}}$ & the two piles of factors are put together, so the counts \blank{1.8cm} \\
Quotient & $\dfrac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{\blank{1.0cm}}$ & factors cancel in pairs, so the counts \blank{1.8cm} \\
Power of a power & $(a^m)^n=a^{\blank{1.0cm}}$ & a pile of $m$ factors, repeated $n$ times \\
Power of a product & $(ab)^n=$ \blank{2.0cm} & every factor inside gets the exponent \\
Zero & $a^0=$ \blank{1.0cm} & because $\dfrac{a^3}{a^3}$ is both $a^0$ and \blank{1.0cm} \\
Negative & $a^{-n}=$ \blank{1.6cm} & because $\dfrac{a^2}{a^5}$ is both $a^{-3}$ and \blank{1.6cm} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\emph{Notice:} the last two are not conventions somebody invented. They are what the quotient law
\textbf{forces} if we want it to keep working past where it ``should'' stop.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Apply them.} Write every answer with \textbf{positive} exponents only.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
(a) \; $3x^2\cdot 4x^3=$ \blank{2.4cm} &
(b) \; $(2x^3y)^4=$ \blank{2.6cm} &
(c) \; $\dfrac{12x^5y^{-2}}{3x^2y^4}=$ \blank{2.6cm} \\[10pt]
(d) \; $\left(\dfrac{x^4}{x^{-1}}\right)^2=$ \blank{2.4cm} &
(e) \; $(5a^0b^3)(2b^{-5})=$ \blank{2.6cm} &
(f) \; $\dfrac{(2m^2)^3}{4m^5}=$ \blank{2.6cm} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\emph{Careful on (a):} the coefficients are \blank{2.6cm}, but the exponents are \blank{2.0cm}.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{3. Polynomial Operations --- and Factoring, Which Is the Same Trip Backwards}
A \textbf{polynomial} is a sum of terms $ax^n$ where every $n$ is a \blank{2.4cm} number. Its
\textbf{degree} is the \blank{2.6cm} exponent.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Add and subtract} by combining like terms. All the difficulty is in one place.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\hline
(a) \; $(3x^2-5x+2)+(x^2+4x-9)=$ & \blank{6.4cm} \\
(b) \; $(3x^2-5x+2)-(x^2+4x-9)=$ & \blank{6.4cm} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
In (b), the minus sign must be distributed to \blank{4.4cm} of the second polynomial --- not just
the first one. That single slip is the most common polynomial error there is.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Multiply} with the distributive property. Two patterns are worth recognizing on sight.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l@{}}
(c) \; $(x+3)(x-3)=$ \blank{3.0cm} &
(d) \; $(x+5)^2=$ \blank{3.4cm} \\[6pt]
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l@{}}{(e) \; $(2x-1)(3x^2+x-4)=$ \blank{6.0cm}} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textbf{Difference of squares:} $a^2-b^2=$ \blank{3.0cm} \qquad
\textbf{Perfect square trinomial:} $(a+b)^2=$ \blank{3.4cm} \\
Hook item (c) is now settled: $(x+5)^2$ is \emph{not} $x^2+25$, because the middle term
\blank{1.6cm} is missing.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Factor completely.} Factoring is multiplying \emph{backwards}, so you can always check by
\blank{2.6cm}. Follow the order every time:
\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.94\linewidth}{\centering \textbf{(1)} Pull out the \blank{1.6cm} first, always.
\quad \textbf{(2)} Then use a pattern or the trinomial method. \\
\textbf{(3)} Then check whether any factor \emph{still} factors. That is what
``\blank{2.4cm}'' means.}}
\end{center}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
(f) \; $x^2+7x+12=$ \blank{2.3cm} &
(g) \; $x^2-64=$ \blank{2.3cm} &
(h) \; $6x^2-15x=$ \blank{2.3cm} \\[6pt]
(i) \; $2x^2-18=$ \blank{2.3cm} &
(j) \; $3x^2+15x+18=$ \blank{2.3cm} &
(k) \; $x^2-5x-14=$ \blank{2.3cm} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\emph{Watch (i).} If you stop at $2(x^2-9)$ you have factored --- but not \blank{2.6cm}, because
$x^2-9$ is still a \blank{4.0cm}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Divide} by splitting the fraction (monomial divisor) or cancelling common factors.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.7}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l@{}}
(l) \; $\dfrac{12x^4-8x^3+4x^2}{4x^2}=$ \blank{3.6cm} &
(m) \; $\dfrac{x^2+7x+12}{x+3}=$ \blank{3.0cm} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In (m), cancelling assumes $x+3\ne 0$, so $x\ne$ \blank{1.2cm}.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{4. Radicals and Rational Exponents}
\textbf{Simplify} by pulling out the largest perfect power. The method is identical for square and
cube roots --- only the \blank{1.6cm} changes.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.7}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X l@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Radical} & \textbf{Largest perfect square / cube factor} & \textbf{Simplest form} \\
\hline
$\sqrt{72}$ & \blank{4.6cm} & \blank{2.0cm} \\
$\sqrt{50}$ & \blank{4.6cm} & \blank{2.0cm} \\
$\sqrt[3]{54}$ & \blank{4.6cm} & \blank{2.0cm} \\
$\sqrt[3]{-24}$ & \blank{4.6cm} & \blank{2.0cm} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Add and subtract} only \emph{like} radicals --- same index, same radicand. This is exactly
the like-terms rule wearing a different costume.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
(a) \; $3\sqrt5+2\sqrt5=$ \blank{2.0cm} &
(b) \; $3\sqrt5+2\sqrt3=$ \blank{2.6cm} &
(c) \; $\sqrt{12}+\sqrt{27}=$ \blank{2.4cm} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\emph{Why does (c) work when the radicands started different?} Because after simplifying, they
became \blank{2.6cm}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Multiply} with $\sqrt a\cdot\sqrt b=\sqrt{ab}$:
\quad (d) \; $\sqrt6\cdot\sqrt{10}=$ \blank{2.6cm} \quad
(e) \; $\sqrt3(\sqrt3+\sqrt5)=$ \blank{3.0cm}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Two spellings of one idea.} A radical \emph{is} a fractional exponent:
\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.90\linewidth}{\centering
$a^{1/2}=\blank{1.6cm}$ \qquad and \qquad $a^{1/3}=\blank{1.6cm}$}}
\end{center}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
(f) \; $\sqrt{7}=7^{\blank{0.8cm}}$ &
(g) \; $25^{1/2}=$ \blank{1.4cm} &
(h) \; $\sqrt[3]{8}=8^{1/3}=(2^3)^{1/3}=$ \blank{1.4cm} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Look at (h): the \blank{5.0cm} law from Section~2 did the work. \emph{That} is why this notation
matters --- the exponent spelling is what lets the exponent laws reach radicals at all, and it is
the notation all of Unit 6 is built on.
\end{notesbox}
