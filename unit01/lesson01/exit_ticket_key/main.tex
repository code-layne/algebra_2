No notes. Show enough work that a wrong answer can still earn a right idea.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=8pt]
  \item \textbf{Evaluate} $2x^2-5x+|x-8|$ at $x=-3$. Substitute first, then work outward from the
        grouping symbols.

        \vspace{2pt}
        Substitute: \ans{$2(-3)^2-5(-3)+|{-3}-8| = 2(9)+15+|{-11}| = 18+15+11$}

        \vspace{2pt}
        Value: \ans{$44$}

  \item \textbf{Factor completely:} $3x^2-27$.

        \vspace{2pt}
        Step 1 --- pull out the GCF: \ans{$3(x^2-9)$}

        \vspace{2pt}
        Step 2 --- keep going: \ans{$3(x+3)(x-3)$}

        \vspace{2pt}
        \textbf{Check by expanding.} Multiply your factors back out and show you land on $3x^2-27$.
        \ansline{$(x+3)(x-3)=x^2-9$, and $3(x^2-9)=3x^2-27$.}

  \item \textbf{SOL-style multiple choice.} Which expression is equivalent to
        \TallMath{\frac{15x^4y^{-1}}{5x^2y^3}} \; ?
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\Alph*., leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=2pt]
          \item $3x^2y^4$
          \item \TallMath{\frac{3x^2}{y^4}} \quad \textcolor{keyred}{\textbf{$\leftarrow$ correct}}
          \item \TallMath{\frac{3x^2}{y^2}}
          \item \TallMath{\frac{3x^6}{y^4}}
        \end{enumerate}

        \vspace{2pt}
        In one sentence, what did the \emph{wrong} answer you were most tempted by get wrong?
        \ansline{Answers vary --- A subtracts the $y$ exponents backwards, C loses the negative sign
        on $y^{-1}$, D adds the $x$ exponents instead of subtracting.}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Answer 3: B.} $\frac{15}{5}=3$; $x^{4-2}=x^2$; $y^{-1-3}=y^{-4}=\frac{1}{y^4}$. Each
distractor breaks exactly one law. \textbf{A} subtracts in the wrong order ($3-(-1)=4$) and so gets
a positive $y$ exponent. \textbf{C} treats $y^{-1}$ as $y^{1}$ ($1-3=-2$) --- the negative sign is
simply dropped. \textbf{D} adds the $x$ exponents ($4+2$) instead of subtracting.

\textbf{Sort the collected tickets into four piles:} \emph{got it} / \emph{sign-and-order slips}
(item 1: $2(-3)^2$ evaluated as $-18$, or the absolute value dropped) / \emph{factoring not
complete} (item 2 stopped at $3(x^2-9)$) / \emph{exponent laws} (item 3 wrong).

\textbf{Act on the last two piles.} Incomplete factoring is the one that costs the most soonest:
Lesson 1.2 uses factoring to \emph{solve} quadratics, so a student who stops at $3(x^2-9)$ there
loses two solutions rather than a step. If that pile is large, open 1.2 by re-running item 2 with
new numbers before starting anything new. The exponent pile is slower-burning but wider --- Units 3
through 6 lean on those laws continuously --- so plan a five-minute derivation warm-up rather than a
reteach.

\textbf{Item 1 is the sign diagnostic.} A student who writes $-18$ for $2(-3)^2$ has the $-x^2$
error from the Warm-Up still intact; name it that way when you hand the ticket back, because they
have now seen it three times and the name is what makes it stick.
\end{teachernote}
