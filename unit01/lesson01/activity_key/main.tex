\begin{headlinebox}{forestbg}
\small\textbf{Rewriting Without Changing the Value.} Every move today turns an expression into a
\emph{different-looking} expression with the \emph{same} value. With your partner, do the work ---
then check it the way this lesson lets you: multiply a factorization back out, or test a value to
kill a claim. Argue about the ones you disagree on before you write anything down.
\end{headlinebox}

\vspace{0.06in}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{Part 1 --- Translate.} Let $n$ be the number.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X l@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Words} & \textbf{Expression} \\
\hline
(a) \; Nine more than a number & \ans{$n+9$} \\
(b) \; Four less than three times a number & \ans{$3n-4$} \\
(c) \; The quotient of a number and 6 & \ans{$\frac{n}{6}$} \\
(d) \; Twice the quantity of a number increased by 5 & \ans{$2(n+5)$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Two of those four have a trap in the \emph{order}. Which two, and what is the trap?
\ansline{(b) and (d). ``Less than'' reverses the subtraction --- it is $3n-4$, never $4-3n$. ``The
quantity'' means the whole sum is doubled, so the parentheses are required: $2(n+5)$, not $2n+5$.}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 2 --- Evaluate.} Substitute first, then follow the order of operations.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X l@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Expression} & \textbf{Substitute and simplify} & \textbf{Value} \\
\hline
(a) \; $4x-7$ at $x=3$ & \ans{$4(3)-7=12-7$} & \ans{$5$} \\
(b) \; $-x^2+2x$ at $x=-5$ & \ans{$-(-5)^2+2(-5)=-25-10$} & \ans{$-35$} \\
(c) \; $|3x+1|$ at $x=-4$ & \ans{$|3(-4)+1|=|{-11}|$} & \ans{$11$} \\
(d) \; $\sqrt{x}+\sqrt[3]{x}$ at $x=64$ & \ans{$\sqrt{64}+\sqrt[3]{64}=8+4$} & \ans{$12$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
In (b), is the answer positive or negative? \ans{negative} \; Say why in one sentence.
\ansline{The exponent belongs only to $x$, so $-x^2$ squares first ($25$) and \emph{then} takes the
opposite ($-25$); adding $2(-5)=-10$ makes it more negative still.}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 3 --- One law at a time.} Each item below needs exactly \emph{one} law of exponents.
Be ready to name the one you used. \; \ans{(a) product \; (b) quotient \; (c) power of a power \;
(d) power of a product \; (e) zero \; (f) negative}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
(a) \; $x^5\cdot x^7=$ \ans{$x^{12}$} &
(b) \; $\dfrac{y^9}{y^4}=$ \ans{$y^5$} &
(c) \; $(m^3)^5=$ \ans{$m^{15}$} \\[8pt]
(d) \; $(3k)^2=$ \ans{$9k^2$} &
(e) \; $7^0=$ \ans{$1$} &
(f) \; $x^{-3}=$ \ans{$\dfrac{1}{x^3}$} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Which item did you have to think hardest about, and why?
\ansline{Answers vary; (d) is the usual one --- the exponent reaches the $3$ as well as the $k$, so
it is $9k^2$ and not $3k^2$. (f) is the runner-up: $x^{-3}$ is a \emph{reciprocal}, not a negative
number.}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 4 --- Factor completely. Every one of these has a GCF to pull out first.}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l@{}}
(a) \; $4x^2+10x=$ \ans{$2x(2x+5)$} &
(b) \; $3x^2-12=$ \ans{$3(x+2)(x-2)$} \\[8pt]
(c) \; $2x^2+10x+12=$ \ans{$2(x+2)(x+3)$} &
(d) \; $5x^3-20x=$ \ans{$5x(x+2)(x-2)$} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textbf{Check yourself.} Multiply your answer to (b) back out. Do you land on $3x^2-12$?
\ans{Yes: $(x+2)(x-2)=x^2-4$, and $3(x^2-4)=3x^2-12$.} \\
That check works on \emph{every} factoring problem --- which is why you never have to ask whether a
factorization is right.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching Proficiency},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{Part 1 --- Mixed, and nobody is telling you which tool to use.} That is the whole
difficulty. Decide first, then work. Every answer with radicals must be in simplest form, and every
answer with exponents must use \textbf{positive} exponents only.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=9pt]
  \item $(4x^2-x+6)-(2x^2+5x-3)=$ \ans{$2x^2-6x+9$}
  \item \TallMath{\frac{18a^{-2}b^5}{6a^3b^{-1}}} $=$ \ans{$\dfrac{3b^6}{a^5}$}
  \item Factor completely: \; $4x^2-36=$ \ans{$4(x+3)(x-3)$}
  \item Factor completely: \; $2x^2-2x-24=$ \ans{$2(x-4)(x+3)$}
  \item \TallMath{\frac{x^2-x-20}{x-5}} $=$ \ans{$x+4$} \quad and $x\ne$ \ans{$5$}
  \item Side by side --- \emph{same method, different index.} \\[3pt]
        $\sqrt{80}=$ \ans{$4\sqrt5$} \hfill $\sqrt[3]{40}=$ \ans{$2\sqrt[3]{5}$} \\[3pt]
        What was identical about the two?
        \ans{Both pulled out the largest perfect power: $80=16\cdot5$, $40=8\cdot5$. Only the index changed.}
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Part 2 --- Prove it, or kill it.} These two items are answered in \emph{different ways} on
purpose.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=7pt, start=7]
  \item Show by \textbf{algebra} that $(2x+3)(2x-3)$ and $4x^2-9$ are equivalent. Testing values is
        not enough here --- multiply.
        \ansline{$(2x+3)(2x-3)=4x^2-6x+6x-9$. The middle terms are opposites and cancel, leaving
        $4x^2-9$. This is the difference-of-squares pattern with $a=2x$, $b=3$.}
  \item Show that $\sqrt{x^2+9}$ and $x+3$ are \textbf{not} equivalent, using exactly \emph{one}
        value of $x$. Show the arithmetic for both expressions at your value.
        \ansline{At $x=4$: $\sqrt{4^2+9}=\sqrt{25}=5$, but $4+3=7$. Since $5\ne7$, they are not
        equivalent. (Any $x\ne0$ works.)}
\end{enumerate}
Why did item 7 need algebra while item 8 needed only one number?
\ansline{Equivalence is a claim about \emph{every} value, so proving it means covering all values at
once --- only algebra does that. Disproving it needs just one value where the claim fails.}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier E --- Extension},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{Part 1 --- A rectangle you are handed backwards.} A rectangle has area $x^2+9x+20$ square
units, and one dimension measures $x+4$ units.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Find the other dimension. \ans{$x+5$} \quad What operation did that require?
        \ans{division (or factoring, which is the same trip backwards)}
  \item What value can $x$ \emph{not} be, and why does the algebra forbid it?
        \ans{$x\ne-4$ --- cancelling $x+4$ assumes it is not zero.}
  \item This is a real rectangle, so both dimensions must be positive lengths. What does that
        restrict $x$ to --- and why is that a \emph{stronger} restriction than your answer to (b)?
        \ansline{Both $x+4>0$ and $x+5>0$ force $x>-4$. That rules out an entire interval of values,
        while (b) ruled out only the single point $x=-4$.}
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 2 --- Spot the error.} Each student below made \textbf{exactly one} mistake. Circle it,
\textbf{name} what went wrong, and write the correct answer. Naming it is the part that counts.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}c l X l@{}}
\hline
 & \textbf{Student's work} & \textbf{What went wrong (name it)} & \textbf{Correct} \\
\hline
(a) & $(x+6)^2=x^2+36$ & \ans{squared each term separately --- squaring does not distribute over addition} & \ans{$x^2+12x+36$} \\
(b) & $(5x^2-3x+7)-(2x^2+x-4)$ & \ans{the minus was distributed only to the first term of the second polynomial} & \ans{$3x^2-4x+11$} \\
    & $\qquad =3x^2-2x+3$ & & \\
(c) & \TallMath{\frac{8x^6}{2x^2}=4x^3} & \ans{divided the exponents instead of subtracting them} & \ans{$4x^4$} \\
(d) & $2\sqrt5+3\sqrt7=5\sqrt{12}$ & \ans{added unlike radicals --- and added the radicands, as if $\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}=\sqrt{a+b}$} & \ans{cannot be combined} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Two of those four are the \emph{same} underlying mistake in different costumes. Which two, and what
is the mistake they share?
\ansline{(a) and (d). Both assume an operation distributes across addition when it does not:
$(x+6)^2\ne x^2+6^2$ for the same reason $\sqrt{5}+\sqrt{7}\ne\sqrt{12}$. Only multiplication
distributes over addition.}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 3 --- The one that needs a first move nobody tells you to make.} Simplify
\TallMath{\frac{4^{x+1}}{2^{2x}}} to a single power of $2$, then to a number.
\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.94\linewidth}{\centering \emph{Hint you should not need: no exponent law applies
until the two bases are the same.}}}
\end{center}
\ansline{Rewrite $4$ as $2^2$: \; $\dfrac{4^{x+1}}{2^{2x}}=\dfrac{(2^2)^{x+1}}{2^{2x}}
=\dfrac{2^{2x+2}}{2^{2x}}=2^{(2x+2)-2x}=2^2$.}
\ansline{The power-of-a-power law made the numerator $2^{2x+2}$; the quotient law then subtracted
the exponents, and the $2x$ cancelled --- so the answer does not depend on $x$ at all.}
Answer: \ans{$2^2=4$}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Part 4 --- The asymmetry, stated in your own words.} One \emph{contradicting} value always
disproves equivalence, but one --- or a hundred --- \emph{confirming} values never proves it.
Explain why the two directions are not symmetric. Use the word \textbf{every}.
\ansline{``Equivalent'' means the two expressions agree at \textbf{every} legal value. A single
disagreement is enough to make that false forever, because ``every'' has already been broken.}
\ansline{But agreeing at a hundred values leaves infinitely many untested, so it never establishes
``every.'' Only an algebraic rewriting --- one that holds for an unspecified $x$ --- covers them all
at once.}
\end{tcolorbox}
