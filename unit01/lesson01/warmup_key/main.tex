\begin{notesbox}{Getting Ready: Skills We'll Use Today}
\small These three skills power today's review of expressions. Work quickly.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{1. Order of operations.} Evaluate each one.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
(a) \; $12-3(5-7)=$ \ans{18} &
(b) \; $|{-6}|+\sqrt{16}-\sqrt[3]{27}=$ \ans{7} &
(c) \; $\dfrac{4+2^3}{6}=$ \ans{2} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Now the one that always starts an argument. Let $x=-3$.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l@{}}
(d) \; $x^2=$ \ans{9} &
(e) \; $-x^2=$ \ans{$-9$} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Those two are \emph{not} the same. What exactly is the exponent attached to in each one?
\ansline{In (d) the variable itself is squared: $x^2=(-3)^2=9$. In (e) the exponent is attached
\emph{only} to $x$, and the negative sign is applied afterward: $-x^2=-(-3)^2=-(9)=-9$. An exponent
binds only to what it touches --- parentheses are what change that.}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{2. Exponents --- write them out and count.} Do \emph{not} use a rule. Expand first.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X l@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Expression} & \textbf{Written out as factors} & \textbf{Single power} \\
\hline
$2^5\cdot 2^3$ & \ans{$(2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2)(2\cdot2\cdot2)$ --- eight $2$'s} & \ans{$2^8$} \\
$\dfrac{a^6}{a^2}$ & \ans{$\frac{a\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a}{a\cdot a}$ --- two cancel, four left} & \ans{$a^4$} \\
$(a^3)^2$ & \ans{$(a\cdot a\cdot a)(a\cdot a\cdot a)$ --- three $a$'s, twice} & \ans{$a^6$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Look at your three answers. Where did the exponents' $+$, $-$, and $\times$ \emph{come from}?
\ansline{From \emph{counting factors}, nothing else. Multiplying puts the two piles of factors
together, so you add the counts. Dividing cancels factors in pairs, so you subtract. A power of a
power repeats a pile of factors, so you multiply. The three laws are descriptions of counting, not
rules to memorize.}

\vspace{4pt}

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.44\linewidth}
\centering (a) \; $4(2x-5)=$ \ans{$8x-20$}
\vspace{0.35cm}
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.52\linewidth}
\centering
(b) \; Fill in the area model for $(x+3)(x+2)$.

\vspace{3pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
$\times$ & $x$ & $+3$ \\ \hline

\end{center}
Both parts used the \emph{same} property from Lesson 1.0. Name it. \ans{the distributive property}

\end{notesbox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Item 1(d)--(e) is the debrief worth having.} The $-x^2$ versus $(-x)^2$ distinction is the
most persistent evaluation error in the entire course --- it resurfaces in the quadratic formula
(Unit 3), in end behavior (Unit 4), and in every graphing lesson. Take the argument publicly and
resolve it with the phrase ``the exponent binds only to what it touches.''

\textbf{Item 2 is A.EO.3a in miniature} --- the standard says students \emph{derive} the laws
through exploring patterns, not recite them. Do not let anyone skip the middle column; the whole
value of the item is that the three laws come out of counting. Ask ``where did the $+$ come from?''
and refuse ``that's the rule.''

\textbf{Item 3} is the bridge from Lesson 1.0: the same distributive property that was a name to
recite yesterday is today's engine for every polynomial product. Say that out loud.
\end{teachernote}
