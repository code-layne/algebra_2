\begin{objectivebox}
\textbf{By the end of this lesson, I will be able to\dots}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item \emph{translate} a verbal situation into an algebraic expression and \emph{evaluate} it at given values --- including absolute values, square roots, and cube roots;
  \item apply the \emph{laws of exponents} to simplify multivariable expressions and monomial ratios, and explain where each law comes from;
  \item add, subtract, multiply, \emph{factor completely}, and divide polynomials in one variable;
  \item simplify \emph{radicals} and convert between radical and rational-exponent notation;
  \item \emph{justify} that two expressions are equivalent --- or disprove it with a single value.
\end{itemize}
\end{objectivebox}

\vspace{0.05in}

\begin{vocabbox}
\small
Fill in each term as we build it together.
\par\vspace{2pt}
\vocabans{Expression (vs.\ equation)}{An expression combines numbers, variables, and operations to name a number --- it has no equals sign and is never solved. An equation sets two expressions equal and \emph{is} solved.}
\vocabans{Evaluate}{Replace every variable with a given value, then follow the order of operations until one number is left.}
\vocabans{Polynomial / degree}{A sum of terms $ax^n$ with every $n$ a whole number; the degree is the largest exponent on the variable.}
\vocabans{Factor completely}{Pull out the GCF first, then keep factoring until no factor can be factored further over the integers.}
\vocabans{Simplest radical form}{No perfect-power factor is left under the radical (and no radical is left in a denominator).}
\vocabans{Rational exponent}{A fractional exponent that means a root: $a^{1/2}=\sqrt{a}$ and $a^{1/3}=\sqrt[3]{a}$.}
\vocabans{Equivalent expressions}{Two expressions that give the same value for \emph{every} legal replacement value --- not just for the ones you happened to test.}
\end{vocabbox}

\boxguard
\begin{hookbox}
\textbf{Equivalent, or not?} No rules yet --- just decide, and be ready to defend it.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}c l l X@{}}
\hline
 & \textbf{First} & \textbf{Second} & \textbf{Equivalent? How do you know?} \\
\hline
(a) & $2^3\cdot 2^4$ & $2^{12}$ & \ans{No --- $2^3\cdot2^4=2^7=128$, not $4096$.} \\
(b) & $(x+3)(x-3)$ & $x^2-9$ & \ans{Yes --- multiplying out gives $x^2-9$.} \\
(c) & $(x+5)^2$ & $x^2+25$ & \ans{No --- at $x=1$, $36\ne 26$.} \\
(d) & $\sqrt{9+16}$ & $\sqrt9+\sqrt{16}$ & \ans{No --- $\sqrt{25}=5$, but $3+4=7$.} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Three of those four die to a \emph{single} number. One survives every value you try.
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item Which one survived? \ans{(b)}
  \item You tested it at several values and it worked every time. Does that \textbf{prove} the two
        are equivalent? \ans{No.} \; Why or why not?
        \ansline{There are infinitely many values left untested, so passing a few proves nothing.
        Only algebra --- multiplying $(x+3)(x-3)$ out --- settles \emph{every} value at once.}
\end{itemize}
\textbf{The asymmetry to remember all year:} one bad value \emph{disproves} equivalence forever, but
no number of good values ever \emph{proves} it. For proof you need algebra --- and every rule in
today's lesson is a rewriting move that was proved once, so you never have to test again.
\end{hookbox}

\begin{notesbox}{1. Translating and Evaluating}
An \textbf{expression} is a recipe for a number. It has no equals sign, so it is never
\emph{solved} --- it is \ans{evaluated} once you supply values.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Translate.} Watch the two traps: ``less than'' \ans{reverses} the order, and ``the quantity''
signals \ans{parentheses}.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X l@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Words} & \textbf{Expression} \\
\hline
Seven less than twice a number & \ans{$2n-7$} \\
The quotient of a number and 4, increased by 9 & \ans{$\frac{n}{4}+9$} \\
Five times the quantity of a number decreased by 2 & \ans{$5(n-2)$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Now read this one \emph{back} into words: \; $3n+8$ \; is
\ans{eight more than three times a number}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Evaluate.} Replace, then follow the order of operations. Remember: absolute value bars and
radical signs are \textbf{grouping symbols} --- finish the inside first.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X l@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Expression} & \textbf{Substitute and simplify} & \textbf{Value} \\
\hline
$3x^2-4x+1$ at $x=-2$ & \ans{$3(-2)^2-4(-2)+1=12+8+1$} & \ans{$21$} \\
$|2x-7|$ at $x=1.5$ & \ans{$|3-7|=|{-4}|$} & \ans{$4$} \\
$\sqrt{x+9}$ at $x=-\tfrac{11}{4}$ & \ans{$\sqrt{-\frac{11}{4}+9}=\sqrt{\frac{25}{4}}$} & \ans{$\frac52$} \\
$\sqrt[3]{x}+x$ at $x=-8$ & \ans{$\sqrt[3]{-8}+(-8)=-2-8$} & \ans{$-10$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\textbf{The sign trap from the Warm-Up.} At $x=-3$: \quad $(-x)^2=$ \ans{$9$} \quad but
$-x^2=$ \ans{$-9$}. \\
An exponent binds only to \ans{the base it touches}, and \ans{parentheses} are what change that.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{2. The Laws of Exponents --- Derived, Not Memorized}
Every law below is just \textbf{counting factors}. Fill in the reason, not only the rule.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Law} & \textbf{Rule} & \textbf{Why (count the factors)} \\
\hline
Product & $a^m\cdot a^n=$ \ans{$a^{m+n}$} & the two piles of factors are put together, so the counts \ans{add} \\
Quotient & $\dfrac{a^m}{a^n}=$ \ans{$a^{m-n}$} & factors cancel in pairs, so the counts \ans{subtract} \\
Power of a power & $(a^m)^n=$ \ans{$a^{mn}$} & a pile of $m$ factors, repeated $n$ times \\
Power of a product & $(ab)^n=$ \ans{$a^nb^n$} & every factor inside gets the exponent \\
Zero & $a^0=$ \ans{$1$} & because $\dfrac{a^3}{a^3}$ is both $a^0$ and \ans{$1$} \\
Negative & $a^{-n}=$ \ans{$\dfrac{1}{a^n}$} & because $\dfrac{a^2}{a^5}$ is both $a^{-3}$ and \ans{$\dfrac{1}{a^3}$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\emph{Notice:} the last two are not conventions somebody invented. They are what the quotient law
\textbf{forces} if we want it to keep working past where it ``should'' stop.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Apply them.} Write every answer with \textbf{positive} exponents only.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
(a) \; $3x^2\cdot 4x^3=$ \ans{$12x^5$} &
(b) \; $(2x^3y)^4=$ \ans{$16x^{12}y^4$} &
(c) \; $\dfrac{12x^5y^{-2}}{3x^2y^4}=$ \ans{$\dfrac{4x^3}{y^6}$} \\[10pt]
(d) \; $\left(\dfrac{x^4}{x^{-1}}\right)^2=$ \ans{$x^{10}$} &
(e) \; $(5a^0b^3)(2b^{-5})=$ \ans{$\dfrac{10}{b^2}$} &
(f) \; $\dfrac{(2m^2)^3}{4m^5}=$ \ans{$2m$} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\emph{Careful on (a):} the coefficients are \ans{multiplied}, but the exponents are \ans{added}.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{3. Polynomial Operations --- and Factoring, Which Is the Same Trip Backwards}
A \textbf{polynomial} is a sum of terms $ax^n$ where every $n$ is a \ans{whole} number. Its
\textbf{degree} is the \ans{largest} exponent.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Add and subtract} by combining like terms. All the difficulty is in one place.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\hline
(a) \; $(3x^2-5x+2)+(x^2+4x-9)=$ & \ans{$4x^2-x-7$} \\
(b) \; $(3x^2-5x+2)-(x^2+4x-9)=$ & \ans{$2x^2-9x+11$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
In (b), the minus sign must be distributed to \ans{every term} of the second polynomial --- not just
the first one. That single slip is the most common polynomial error there is.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Multiply} with the distributive property. Two patterns are worth recognizing on sight.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l@{}}
(c) \; $(x+3)(x-3)=$ \ans{$x^2-9$} &
(d) \; $(x+5)^2=$ \ans{$x^2+10x+25$} \\[6pt]
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l@{}}{(e) \; $(2x-1)(3x^2+x-4)=$ \ans{$6x^3-x^2-9x+4$}} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textbf{Difference of squares:} $a^2-b^2=$ \ans{$(a+b)(a-b)$} \qquad
\textbf{Perfect square trinomial:} $(a+b)^2=$ \ans{$a^2+2ab+b^2$} \\
Hook item (c) is now settled: $(x+5)^2$ is \emph{not} $x^2+25$, because the middle term
\ans{$10x$} is missing.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Factor completely.} Factoring is multiplying \emph{backwards}, so you can always check by
\ans{expanding}. Follow the order every time:
\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.94\linewidth}{\centering \textbf{(1)} Pull out the \ans{GCF} first, always.
\quad \textbf{(2)} Then use a pattern or the trinomial method. \\
\textbf{(3)} Then check whether any factor \emph{still} factors. That is what
``\ans{completely}'' means.}}
\end{center}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{2pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
(f) \; $x^2+7x+12=$ \ans{$(x+3)(x+4)$} &
(g) \; $x^2-64=$ \ans{$(x+8)(x-8)$} &
(h) \; $6x^2-15x=$ \ans{$3x(2x-5)$} \\[6pt]
(i) \; $2x^2-18=$ \ans{$2(x+3)(x-3)$} &
(j) \; $3x^2+15x+18=$ \ans{$3(x+2)(x+3)$} &
(k) \; $x^2-5x-14=$ \ans{$(x-7)(x+2)$} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\emph{Watch (i).} If you stop at $2(x^2-9)$ you have factored --- but not \ans{completely}, because
$x^2-9$ is still a \ans{difference of squares}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Divide} by splitting the fraction (monomial divisor) or cancelling common factors.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.7}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l@{}}
(l) \; $\dfrac{12x^4-8x^3+4x^2}{4x^2}=$ \ans{$3x^2-2x+1$} &
(m) \; $\dfrac{x^2+7x+12}{x+3}=$ \ans{$x+4$} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In (m), cancelling assumes $x+3\ne 0$, so $x\ne$ \ans{$-3$}.
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[12]
\begin{notesbox}{4. Radicals and Rational Exponents}
\textbf{Simplify} by pulling out the largest perfect power. The method is identical for square and
cube roots --- only the \ans{index} changes.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.7}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X l@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Radical} & \textbf{Largest perfect square / cube factor} & \textbf{Simplest form} \\
\hline
$\sqrt{72}$ & \ans{$72=36\cdot 2$} & \ans{$6\sqrt2$} \\
$\sqrt{50}$ & \ans{$50=25\cdot 2$} & \ans{$5\sqrt2$} \\
$\sqrt[3]{54}$ & \ans{$54=27\cdot 2$} & \ans{$3\sqrt[3]{2}$} \\
$\sqrt[3]{-24}$ & \ans{$-24=-8\cdot 3$} & \ans{$-2\sqrt[3]{3}$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Add and subtract} only \emph{like} radicals --- same index, same radicand. This is exactly
the like-terms rule wearing a different costume.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
(a) \; $3\sqrt5+2\sqrt5=$ \ans{$5\sqrt5$} &
(b) \; $3\sqrt5+2\sqrt3=$ \ans{cannot combine} &
(c) \; $\sqrt{12}+\sqrt{27}=$ \ans{$5\sqrt3$} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\emph{Why does (c) work when the radicands started different?} Because after simplifying, they
became \ans{like radicals} \ans{($2\sqrt3+3\sqrt3$)}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Multiply} with $\sqrt a\cdot\sqrt b=\sqrt{ab}$:
\quad (d) \; $\sqrt6\cdot\sqrt{10}=$ \ans{$2\sqrt{15}$} \quad
(e) \; $\sqrt3(\sqrt3+\sqrt5)=$ \ans{$3+\sqrt{15}$}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Two spellings of one idea.} A radical \emph{is} a fractional exponent:
\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.90\linewidth}{\centering
$a^{1/2}=$ \ans{$\sqrt{a}$} \qquad and \qquad $a^{1/3}=$ \ans{$\sqrt[3]{a}$}}}
\end{center}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
(f) \; $\sqrt{7}=$ \ans{$7^{1/2}$} &
(g) \; $25^{1/2}=$ \ans{$5$} &
(h) \; $\sqrt[3]{8}=8^{1/3}=(2^3)^{1/3}=$ \ans{$2$} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Look at (h): the \ans{power of a power} law from Section~2 did the work. \emph{That} is why this
notation matters --- the exponent spelling is what lets the exponent laws reach radicals at all, and
it is the notation all of Unit 6 is built on.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{practicebox}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item Evaluate $x^2-3x+|x+2|$ at $x=-4$.
\begin{work}
  x^2-3x+|x+2| &= (-4)^2-3(-4)+|{-4}+2| \\
               &= 16+12+2               \\
               &= 30
\end{work}
  \item Simplify $\dfrac{20a^6b^{-3}}{5a^2b}$ with positive exponents. \ans{$\dfrac{4a^4}{b^4}$}
  \item Factor completely: $5x^2-45$. \ans{$5(x+3)(x-3)$} \; Check by expanding:
        \ans{$5(x^2-9)=5x^2-45$ \checkmark}
  \item Simplify $\sqrt{98}$. \ans{$7\sqrt2$} \quad Write $\sqrt[3]{11}$ with a rational exponent.
        \ans{$11^{1/3}$}
  \item Are $(x-4)^2$ and $x^2-16$ equivalent? Answer with \emph{one} value of $x$ if they are not.
        \ansline{No. $(x-4)^2=x^2-8x+16$, and the $-8x$ never disappears. At $x=1$:
        $(1-4)^2=9$ but $1-16=-15$.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{practicebox}
