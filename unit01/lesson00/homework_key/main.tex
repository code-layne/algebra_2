    (b) \; $-9(x-4)=-9x+36$ & \ans{distributive property} \\
    (c) \; $\dfrac{7}{2}\cdot\dfrac{2}{7}=1$ & \ans{multiplicative inverse property} \\
    (d) \; if $x=7$ and $7=y$, then $x=y$ & \ans{transitive property of equality} \\
    (e) \; $x-4=10 \;\Rightarrow\; x-4+4=10+4$ & \ans{addition property of equality} \\

    (h) \; if $3x<12$ then $x<4$ & \ans{division property of inequality (positive divisor --- no reversal)} \\

    \ans{Yes: $-3(0-2)=6<15$, and $0>-3$.}

  \item \textbf{Explain.} Using the words \textbf{one side} and \textbf{both sides}, explain in your
        own words the difference between a \emph{property of real numbers} and a \emph{property of
        equality}. Give one example of each that does not appear anywhere above.
        \ansline{A property of real numbers rewrites \textbf{one side} --- it changes how a single
        expression is written without changing its value, and no equation is needed for it to make
        sense. A property of equality acts on \textbf{both sides} of an existing equation at once,
        producing a new equation with exactly the same solutions.\\[3pt]
        Sample answers (accept any correct originals): real numbers --- $2(y+6)=2y+12$
        (distributive). Equality --- if $x+11=20$ then $x+11-11=20-11$ (subtraction property of
        equality).}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{extensionbox}
\textbf{The closure investigation.} A set is \textbf{closed} under an operation when the operation
on members of the set always lands back \emph{inside} the set. Write \textbf{yes} or \textbf{no} in
each box --- and every ``no'' must be justified with a \textbf{counterexample} written underneath.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l Y Y Y Y@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Set} & \textbf{Addition} & \textbf{Subtraction} & \textbf{Multiplication} & \textbf{Division (nonzero)} \\
\hline
Natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$ & \ans{yes} & \ans{no} & \ans{yes} & \ans{no} \\
Integers $\mathbb{Z}$ & \ans{yes} & \ans{yes} & \ans{yes} & \ans{no} \\
Rationals $\mathbb{Q}$ & \ans{yes} & \ans{yes} & \ans{yes} & \ans{yes} \\
Irrationals & \ans{no} & \ans{no} & \ans{no} & \ans{no} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\ansline{Counterexamples --- $\mathbb{N}$ subtraction: $3-8=-5$, not natural. $\mathbb{N}$ division:
$3\div 8=\tfrac38$, not natural. $\mathbb{Z}$ division: $3\div 5=\tfrac35$, not an integer.
Irrationals, all four: $\sqrt2+(-\sqrt2)=0$; $\sqrt2-\sqrt2=0$; $\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2=2$;
$\sqrt2\div\sqrt2=1$ --- every result is rational.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item Which single ``no'' box explains why mathematicians had to invent the \textbf{integers}?
  \item Which single ``no'' box explains why they then had to invent the \textbf{rationals}?
  \item One row is ``yes'' in all four columns. Which one, and why does that make it special?
\end{enumerate}
\ansline{(a) Natural numbers under \emph{subtraction} --- there was no way to answer $3-8$ until the
negatives existed. (b) Integers under \emph{division} --- there was no way to answer $3\div 5$ until
ratios were allowed. (c) The \textbf{rationals}: closed under all four operations, so you can add,
subtract, multiply, and divide (by nonzero) forever and never leave the set. That self-containment
is what makes $\mathbb{Q}$ a complete arithmetic system on its own --- and note that the irrationals,
by contrast, are closed under \emph{nothing}.}
\end{extensionbox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Item 1} is graded on \emph{completeness}, not just correctness: a student who checks one box
per row has the classic misconception that the sets are mutually exclusive. Note that $0.\overline{6}$
is deliberately included --- a repeating decimal is rational ($=\tfrac23$), and some students will
call it irrational because it ``goes on forever.''

\textbf{Item 2} rows (h) and (k) are a matched pair: same property, opposite outcomes, because one
divisor is positive and the other negative. If a student names both identically without mentioning
the sign, that is the reversal-rule gap the exit ticket was screening for.

\textbf{The extension's irrationals row} is the payoff of the whole page --- ``closed under nothing''
surprises nearly everyone and is the cleanest possible argument for why the rationals, not the
irrationals, are the workhorse set. Accept any valid counterexample; students often find
$\pi + (1-\pi) = 1$ on their own, which is worth reading aloud.
\end{teachernote}
