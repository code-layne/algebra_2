\begin{headlinebox}{forestbg}
\small\textbf{Name That Property.} For the rest of this course, ``because I moved it over'' is not
an answer --- every legal move in algebra has a \emph{name}. With your partner, name the property
behind each step, and when a claim is false, kill it with a single \textbf{counterexample}. Argue
about the ones you disagree on before you write anything down.
\end{headlinebox}

Which two were disguised, and what were they really?
\ansline{$\sqrt{25}=5$ (a natural number, not irrational) and $-\tfrac{12}{4}=-3$ (an integer, not
merely a fraction). The way a number is written is not what decides --- the value is.}

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Part 2 --- Name the property.} Use the word bank. Each name is used at least once.
\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.96\linewidth}{\small\centering
commutative \quad$\cdot$\quad associative \quad$\cdot$\quad distributive \quad$\cdot$\quad
additive identity \quad$\cdot$\quad multiplicative identity \\
additive inverse \quad$\cdot$\quad multiplicative inverse \quad$\cdot$\quad
addition property of equality \quad$\cdot$\quad division property of equality}}
\end{center}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=7pt]
  \item $6(x+2)=6x+12$ \hfill \ans{distributive}
  \item $9+(-9)=0$ \hfill \ans{additive inverse}
  \item $(4\cdot 7)\cdot 3=4\cdot(7\cdot 3)$ \hfill \ans{associative ($\times$)}
  \item $x+5=12 \;\Rightarrow\; x+5-5=12-5$ \hfill \ans{addition property of equality (adding $-5$)}
  \item $-8\cdot 1=-8$ \hfill \ans{multiplicative identity}
  \item $2y+7=7+2y$ \hfill \ans{commutative ($+$)}
  \item $5x=35 \;\Rightarrow\; \dfrac{5x}{5}=\dfrac{35}{5}$ \hfill \ans{division property of equality}
  \item $\dfrac{2}{5}\cdot\dfrac{5}{2}=1$ \hfill \ans{multiplicative inverse}
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 2 --- Now an inequality.} One line here does something none of the lines above did.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Step} & \textbf{Property that allowed it} \\
\hline
$-4(x+1)\ge 8$ & Given \\
$-4x-4\ge 8$ & \ans{distributive property} \\
$-4x\ge 12$ & \ans{addition property of inequality (added 4 to both sides)} \\
$x\le -3$ & \ans{division property of inequality --- divided by $-4$, so the symbol reversed} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
The symbol reversed on the last line but \emph{not} on the line above it, even though both lines
involved negative numbers. Explain exactly what makes the difference.
\ansline{What matters is not whether a negative number is \emph{present} but whether you multiplied
or divided \emph{by} one. Line 3 added $4$ to both sides --- addition never disturbs the order, so
the symbol stands. Line 4 divided both sides by $-4$, which reflects the whole number line through
zero and reverses order, so $\ge$ became $\le$.}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 3 --- Error hunt.} Each solution below has \textbf{exactly one} illegal step. Circle it,
then name the property that was \emph{violated}.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{(a)} & \textbf{(b)} & \textbf{(c)} \\
\hline
$3(x-5)=21$ & $-2x<10$ & $x^2=7x$ \\
$3x-5=21$ \ans{$\leftarrow$} & $x<-5$ \ans{$\leftarrow$} & $\dfrac{x^2}{x}=\dfrac{7x}{x}$ \ans{$\leftarrow$} \\
$3x=26$ & & $x=7$ \\
$x=\tfrac{26}{3}$ & & \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
(a) Property violated: \ans{distributive --- the $3$ was applied to $x$ but not to $-5$; it should give $3x-15=21$, so $x=12$.} \\[3pt]
(b) Property violated: \ans{division property of inequality --- divided by $-2$ without reversing; correct answer is $x>-5$.} \\[3pt]
(c) Property violated: \ans{division property of equality --- divided both sides by $x$, which may be $0$.} \\[3pt]
Problem (c) is the sneaky one --- the arithmetic is fine. What did that step quietly assume, and
what solution did it throw away?
\ansline{Dividing by $x$ assumes $x\ne0$, since division by zero is undefined --- and $x=0$ really
is a solution of $x^2=7x$. The legal route uses the zero product property instead: $x^2-7x=0$, so
$x(x-7)=0$, giving $x=0$ \emph{or} $x=7$. Dividing by a variable silently deletes a solution.}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier E --- Extension},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{Part 1 --- True or false?} A property is a claim about \emph{all} numbers. To defend a true
one, name it. To kill a false one, give a \textbf{counterexample} --- one is enough.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.7}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{0.44\linewidth} c X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Claim} & \textbf{T/F} & \textbf{Property name, or counterexample} \\
\hline
Subtraction is associative. & \ans{F} & \ans{$(9-4)-2=3$ but $9-(4-2)=7$} \\
The integers are closed under division. & \ans{F} & \ans{$3\div 5=\tfrac35$, not an integer} \\
The irrationals are closed under addition. & \ans{F} & \ans{$\sqrt2+(-\sqrt2)=0$, rational} \\
For all reals, $a(b-c)=ab-ac$. & \ans{T} & \ans{distributive property} \\
Every real number has a multiplicative inverse. & \ans{F} & \ans{$0$ has none --- no number times $0$ gives $1$} \\
If $ab=0$ then both $a=0$ and $b=0$. & \ans{F} & \ans{$a=3$, $b=0$: the property says \emph{or}, not \emph{and}} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 2 --- The one that surprises everybody.} Most groups guess wrong on the irrationals.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Find two irrational numbers whose \textbf{sum} is rational. \ans{$\sqrt2$ and $-\sqrt2$ (sum $0$); or $\pi$ and $1-\pi$}
  \item Find two irrational numbers whose \textbf{product} is rational. \ans{$\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2=2$; or $\sqrt3\cdot\sqrt{12}=6$}
  \item So the irrationals are \emph{not} closed under addition or multiplication. The rationals,
        however, \emph{are} closed under both. Why does that difference matter --- what would go
        wrong if we tried to build all of arithmetic out of the irrationals alone?
        \ansline{The irrationals are not a self-contained number system: ordinary addition and
        multiplication keep dropping you \emph{out} of the set, and the set has no additive identity
        ($0$ is rational) and no multiplicative identity ($1$ is rational), so there is nothing to
        build on. The rationals are closed under $+,-,\times$, and $\div$ (by nonzero), which is why
        they form a complete arithmetic on their own --- and why the irrationals are best understood
        as what \emph{fills the gaps} in the number line rather than as a system in their own right.}
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 3 --- Why is there no reversal rule for equations?} Solving $-3x=12$ gives $x=-4$; the
equals sign does not turn around. Solving $-3x>12$ gives $x<-4$; the symbol does. Using the number
line and the word \textbf{order}, explain why inequalities need a rule that equations do not.
\ansline{An equation claims only that two quantities are the \emph{same number} --- it says nothing
about position on the number line, so there is no order for anything to reverse. Multiplying both
sides by $-3$ relocates both, but they stay equal to each other.\\[3pt]
An inequality claims something stronger: that one number sits to the \emph{left} of the other.
Multiplying by a negative reflects the entire line through zero, and a reflection turns left into
right --- so the ordering claim must be restated backwards to stay true. Equality has no order to
disturb; inequality does. That is the whole difference.}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Tier placement.} Send to Tier~R anyone who wrote ``I moved it over'' in the notes, or who
checked exactly one box per row in the Warm-Up. Tier~A is the default. Tier~E is for groups who
finished the notes practice quickly and named all three properties correctly.

\textbf{Two items are worth a whole-class pause.} Tier~A Part~3(c) --- dividing by $x$ --- is the
single most useful error on the page: it will reappear in Unit 3 (solving quadratics) and again in
Unit 5 (rational equations), and this is the cheapest place to meet it. Tier~E Part~1 row~3 (the
irrationals) is where most groups confidently answer ``true''; if two or more groups reach it, stop
the room and take the counterexample publicly.

\textbf{Grading.} Names, not phrasing. ``Combine like terms'' for Tier~A Part~1 line~3 is fine;
``simplify'' alone is not, unless the student can say what was simplified and why that was legal.
\end{teachernote}
