\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=forestbg, colframe=forest, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students can write and solve multistep linear equations and
    inequalities in one variable, justifying each step by a \emph{property of equality} or
    \emph{property of inequality}; rearrange a literal equation for a specified variable; graph an
    inequality's solution set on a number line and interpret it in context; determine whether a
    linear equation has \emph{one}, \emph{no}, or \emph{infinitely many} solutions; and solve a
    quadratic equation over the real numbers by square roots, by factoring with the zero product
    property, and by the quadratic formula, determining and justifying the \emph{number of real
    solutions} before solving. Throughout, students \textbf{verify} every solution by substitution
    and read it back against the graph.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Standards (2023 VA SOL --- Algebra 1, reviewed):} this lesson reviews the
    \textbf{Equations and Inequalities} domain, minus systems. \textbf{A.EI.1} --- write a linear
    equation or inequality in one variable from a context (a); solve multistep linear equations by
    the properties of real numbers and of equality (b); solve multistep linear inequalities
    algebraically and graph the solution set on a number line (c); rearrange a formula or literal
    equation for a specified variable (d); determine whether a linear equation has one, no, or
    infinitely many solutions (e); and verify solutions algebraically, graphically, and with
    technology, explaining the method and interpreting the solution in context (f).
    \textbf{A.EI.3} --- solve a quadratic equation in one variable over the reals with rational or
    irrational solutions, including contextual ones (a); determine and justify whether it has no,
    one, or two real solutions (b); and verify solutions algebraically, graphically, and with
    technology (c).
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Deliberately omitted: A.EI.2} (systems of two linear equations, linear inequalities in
    two variables, systems of linear inequalities). Systems of \emph{linear} equations and linear
    programming are out of scope for this course, so reviewing them would set up an expectation the
    year never honors. Systems of \emph{quadratics} return in Unit 3.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Prerequisite:} Lessons 1.0 and 1.1. From 1.0: the properties of equality are the
    \emph{permissions} that make solving legal, and the zero product property is what converts a
    factorization into solutions. From 1.1: distributing clears parentheses, combining like terms
    collapses the sides, factoring \emph{completely} is what makes a quadratic solvable, and
    simplifying radicals is what makes the quadratic formula's answer presentable.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Ideas \& Skills} & \textbf{Key Understandings (the ``why'')} \\[2pt]
\hline
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item \textbf{Solve} a multistep linear equation in one variable and \emph{name the property} that licenses each step.
  \item \textbf{Classify} a linear equation as having one solution, no solution, or infinitely many.
  \item \textbf{Solve} a multistep linear inequality, \emph{reversing} the sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative --- and explain why.
  \item \textbf{Graph} an inequality's solution set on a number line; open vs.\ closed circle.
  \item \textbf{Rearrange} a literal equation or formula for a specified variable.
  \item \textbf{Write} an equation or inequality from a contextual situation, then interpret the solution back in context.
  \item \textbf{Solve} a quadratic by square roots, by factoring $+$ the zero product property, and by the quadratic formula.
  \item \textbf{Determine and justify} the number of real solutions from the discriminant --- \emph{before} solving.
  \item \textbf{Verify} every solution by substitution, and read it off the graph.
\end{itemize}
&
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item \emph{An expression is a recipe; an equation is a \textbf{question}.} The question is ``which values make this true?'' and the answer is a \textbf{solution set}.
  \item ``No solution'' and ``all real numbers'' are \emph{answers}, not failures. An empty set and an infinite set are both perfectly good sets.
  \item Every solving step replaces the equation with an \textbf{equivalent} one --- same question, easier wording. That is why the properties of equality matter: they are the only moves that preserve the answer.
  \item The inequality flip is not a rule to memorize. Multiplying by a negative \emph{reflects the number line across zero}, and reflection reverses order.
  \item A literal equation is solved with exactly the same moves as a numerical one; only the alphabet is scarier.
  \item Factoring stopped short of \emph{complete} loses solutions --- in 1.1 that cost a step, here it costs an answer.
  \item The discriminant tells you \emph{how many} answers exist before you find any of them --- and it is the same information as the number of $x$-intercepts.
  \item Substituting back is not optional politeness. It is the \emph{definition} of a solution.
\end{itemize}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{sky}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\textbf{Equation} & Two expressions joined by $=$. Unlike an expression, it asks a question and is \emph{solved}. \\
\textbf{Solution / solution set} & A value that makes the equation true; the set of all of them. The set may be one number, no numbers, or every real number. \\
\textbf{Equivalent equations} & Equations with the same solution set. Every legal solving step produces one. \\
\textbf{Properties of equality} & Add, subtract, multiply, or divide \emph{both sides} by the same quantity (never divide by $0$). These are what make solving legal. \\
\textbf{Identity} & An equation true for every value --- both sides simplify to the same expression, e.g.\ $3(x-2)=3x-6$. Solution set: all reals. \\
\textbf{Contradiction} & An equation true for no value --- the variable vanishes and a false statement remains, e.g.\ $-6=-5$. Solution set: empty. \\
\textbf{Literal equation} & An equation with several letters, solved for one specified variable, e.g.\ $A=\tfrac12 bh$ solved for $h$. \\
\textbf{Properties of inequality} & Same as equality with \emph{one} change: multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative \textbf{reverses} the inequality symbol. \\
\textbf{Solution set on a number line} & \emph{Closed} (filled) circle for $\le$ or $\ge$; \emph{open} circle for $<$ or $>$; shade every value that works. \\
\textbf{Quadratic equation} & An equation that can be written $ax^2+bx+c=0$ with $a\ne0$. \\
\textbf{Zero product property} & If $mn=0$, then $m=0$ or $n=0$. The bridge from a \emph{factorization} to \emph{solutions} --- and it works only against $0$. \\
\textbf{Quadratic formula} & \TallMath{x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}} --- solves \emph{every} quadratic, always. \\
\textbf{Discriminant} & $b^2-4ac$. Positive $\Rightarrow$ two real solutions; zero $\Rightarrow$ one; negative $\Rightarrow$ none real. \\
\textbf{Verify} & Substitute the answer back into the \emph{original} equation, or locate it as an $x$-intercept / intersection point on the graph. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{fixedskillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{forestbg}
    The Warm-Up rehearses the three things this lesson is built on, each from a named earlier
    lesson. \textbf{(1)} A four-step solve of $5(x-3)=2x+9$ with the \emph{work already done} and the
    \emph{justifications blank} --- students name the property behind each line, which is Lesson 1.0
    doing exactly the job it was written for. \textbf{(2)} Two factor-completely items from Lesson
    1.1 ($x^2-5x-14$ and $3x^2-27$), followed by one sentence to complete: ``if a product equals
    zero, then \dots'' --- so the zero product property arrives as a \emph{consequence} students
    state themselves, not as a new rule. \textbf{(3)} Two pre-drawn number lines to \emph{read}: an
    open circle at $-1$ shaded right, and a closed circle at $3$ shaded left. Students write the
    inequality. Debrief item~1 by refusing ``I moved it over'' --- the property has a name. Debrief
    item~3 by asking how many numbers are in each solution set; ``infinitely many'' is the answer
    that makes graphing the set necessary in the first place.
\end{fixedskillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{forestbg}
    Put three nearly identical equations on the board and say only: \textbf{``Solve all three.''}
    \emph{(a)} $3(x-2)=x-6$; \emph{(b)} $3(x-2)=3x-6$; \emph{(c)} $3(x-2)=3x-5$. Give four minutes.
    Everyone gets (a): $x=0$. Then the room stalls, because in (b) the variable disappears and
    leaves $-6=-6$, and in (c) it disappears and leaves $-6=-5$. Students will say they ``did it
    wrong.'' They did not. Land the point: \textbf{solving is not a hunt for the number --- it is
    asking which values make the statement true, and the honest answers are \emph{every} number,
    \emph{no} number, or \emph{one} number.} (b) is true for everything you substitute; (c) is true
    for nothing. Both are complete answers.
    \\[3pt]
    Now show the same trichotomy in a different family, which is the move that makes the day cohere:
    $x^2=9$ has two solutions, $x^2=0$ has one, and $x^2=-9$ has none over the reals. Ask what
    number of solutions a quadratic can have, and get \emph{two, one, or none} on the board next to
    \emph{one, none, or all}. Then frame the lesson: \emph{today we make the count something you can
    predict instead of discover} --- for a linear equation by watching whether the variable survives,
    and for a quadratic by one number, the discriminant, computed before you solve anything.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{forestbg}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. Linear equations, and what the answer can look like.} Open by naming the contrast with
1.1: an expression is a recipe, an equation is a \emph{question}. Solve $5(x-3)=2x+9$ on the board
and demand a property name for every line --- distributive, subtraction property of equality,
addition, division. Then insist on the \textbf{check}: substitute $x=8$ back into the
\emph{original}, because that is what ``solution'' means (\textbf{A.EI.1f}). Return to the Hook and
formalize: if the variable survives, one solution; if it vanishes leaving a true statement, the
equation is an \textbf{identity} and every real number works; if it vanishes leaving a false one, a
\textbf{contradiction} and nothing works (\textbf{A.EI.1e}). Say plainly that the variable
disappearing is \emph{information}, not an error. Close the section with one contextual setup
(\textbf{A.EI.1a}): a \$40 fee plus \$15 per hour reaching \$130 --- write it, solve it, and answer
in \emph{hours}, not in $x$.

\textbf{2. Literal equations.} Same moves, scarier alphabet. Do $A=\tfrac12bh$ for $h$, then
$P=2\ell+2w$ for $w$, then $y=mx+b$ for $x$ so students see that ``solve for'' is a request, not a
new skill (\textbf{A.EI.1d}). The one genuine difficulty is that you can no longer combine terms
just because they look combinable --- $2\ell$ and $2w$ are not like terms. Point out that Unit 2
will hand them $y=mx+b$ rearranged in three different ways and call them ``forms.''

\columnbreak

\textbf{3. Linear inequalities.} Solve exactly as before, then confront the one difference honestly
rather than announcing it. Write the true statement $2<6$, multiply both sides by $-1$, and ask
whether $-2<-6$. It is false --- and the reason is visible: multiplying by a negative
\textbf{reflects the number line across zero}, and reflection reverses order. \emph{Then} state the
rule (\textbf{A.EI.1c}). Solve $-3x+7>19$ to get $x<-4$, and graph the set: open circle at $-4$,
shaded left. Do a second with $\ge$ so the closed circle appears. Stress what the picture is
\emph{for}: the solution set has infinitely many members, so a picture is the only way to write it
down. Finish with a context --- ``at most \$60'' --- and translate ``at most'' to $\le$, then
interpret the answer as a sentence about the situation.

\textbf{4. Quadratic equations.} Three methods, chosen by what the equation looks like.
\emph{Square roots} when there is no linear term: $x^2=9$ gives $x=\pm3$, and the lost $-3$ is the
classic error. \emph{Factoring $+$ zero product property} when it factors: get one side to zero
first --- the property is about \emph{zero} and nothing else --- then factor \emph{completely} and
set each factor to $0$. Say out loud that stopping early here costs a \emph{solution}, which is why
1.1 pressed on ``completely.'' \emph{Quadratic formula} always. Do $x^2-4x-7=0$ so the answer is
irrational and 1.1's radical simplification is required (\textbf{A.EI.3a}). Then the discriminant
(\textbf{A.EI.3b}): compute $b^2-4ac$ first and predict the count. Tie it to the picture with the
three pre-drawn parabolas in the notes --- two $x$-intercepts, one, or none --- and say that the
discriminant and the graph are the same fact told twice (\textbf{A.EI.3c}).
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
Circulate during the notes practice and Tier work. Watch for and correct:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep]
  \item distributing over only the first term: $5(x-3)=5x-3$;
  \item treating a vanished variable as a mistake --- ``I got $-6=-5$ so I started over'' is the moment to intervene, not later;
  \item writing ``no solution'' for an identity and ``all reals'' for a contradiction (the two get swapped constantly); the test is whether the leftover statement is \emph{true};
  \item forgetting to reverse the inequality after dividing by a negative --- and, just as often, reversing it after \emph{subtracting} a negative, which is not a thing;
  \item open/closed circle mixed up, or the shading pointed at the wrong side because the answer was left as $-4>x$ rather than rewritten as $x<-4$;
  \item in literal equations, ``combining'' $2\ell+2w$ into $4\ell w$ or dividing only one term by the coefficient;
  \item applying the zero product property to a product that does \emph{not} equal zero: from $(x-2)(x+5)=8$ concluding $x-2=8$;
  \item solving $x^2=9$ as $x=3$ only --- half the solution set, silently;
  \item factoring incompletely, so $2x^2-18=0$ becomes $2(x^2-9)=0$ and the student stops, losing both roots;
  \item quadratic formula slips: $-b$ written as $b$ when $b$ is already negative, $2a$ dividing only the radical, or $b^2$ computed as a negative number;
  \item never checking. If nobody at a table has substituted anything back, stop the table.
\end{itemize}
Cold-call prompts: ``Which property let you do that line?'' ``The $x$ disappeared --- is that good
news or bad news? Why?'' ``How many numbers are in your solution set?'' ``Multiply my inequality by
$-1$ and tell me what happened to the order.'' ``Before you solve it: how many real solutions will
this have, and how do you know?'' ``Show me the check.''
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\textbf{How Many Answers Does This Question Have?} activity (tiered; mirrors the notes).
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate.} Four straightforward blocks, one per notes section. Solve four linear
equations with the property-name column left blank. Classify four already-solved equations as one /
none / infinitely many from the last line alone --- no solving, just reading the leftover statement.
Solve two inequalities and shade the two pre-drawn number lines beneath them. Then solve four
quadratics that all factor cleanly, each with a check row. Send here any student who called
$-6=-5$ a mistake.

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching.} Mixed and unlabelled --- students must decide what kind of question
they are looking at before touching it. Includes a multistep equation with fractions, a literal
equation solved for the variable that appears twice on one side, an inequality whose final step
divides by a negative, a quadratic requiring the equation be set to zero \emph{first}, and one
needing the quadratic formula and a simplified radical. Two contextual items: a cost inequality
answered as a sentence, and a projectile height set equal to a value. Ends with a
predict-then-solve pair: state the number of real solutions from the discriminant, \emph{then}
solve and confirm.

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension.} Structure work. Given $kx+6=3x+6$, find every $k$ making the equation
have one solution, no solution, infinitely many --- this forces the classification to become a
condition on a coefficient rather than an observation after the fact. A ``spot the error'' set of
four solutions, one per misconception from Active Monitoring, where the error must be
\textbf{named}. Then: for what values of $c$ does $x^2-6x+c=0$ have two real solutions, one, none?
--- the discriminant read as an inequality in $c$, which is the first time the two halves of the
lesson are used simultaneously. Closes by asking why the zero product property has no version for
$mn=8$.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (independent, no notes): three items. \textbf{(1)} Solve
$4(x-3)=2(2x-5)+2$ and classify the solution set --- both sides collapse to $4x$, leaving
$-12=-8$, so the answer is \emph{no solution}; a student who writes ``$x=0$'' or gives up mid-page
has not absorbed the Hook. \textbf{(2)} Solve $x^2-3x=10$ by factoring and verify \emph{one}
solution by substitution --- the diagnostic is whether the student sets the equation to zero before
factoring, since $x(x-3)=10$ leads nowhere. \textbf{(3)} An \textbf{SOL-style multiple-choice}
item: solve $-3x+7>19$ (answer $x<-4$), with distractors that each break exactly one thing --- the
flip omitted, the sign of the division lost, or both. Collect and sort into ``got it / classification
/ inequality direction / zero-first.'' The last pile matters most for Lesson 1.3, where every zero of
a function is found by solving exactly the equation these students could not set up.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} four multistep linear equations with a property-justification column; four
classification items answered from the final line; three literal equations including one where the
target variable appears twice; four inequalities with pre-drawn number lines to shade, one of which
requires the flip; two contextual items (write it, solve it, answer in a sentence with units); a
quadratic set of six --- two by square roots, two by factoring where one must first be set to zero,
two by the quadratic formula with irrational answers to simplify; a discriminant table filled in
\emph{before} solving; and one written item explaining why the zero product property needs the zero.
\textbf{Extension:} the solution-count audit --- given a parameter $k$ or $c$ inside the equation,
state the values that produce each possible number of solutions, for both a linear and a quadratic
case, and explain why a linear equation can have infinitely many solutions but a quadratic cannot.
\textbf{Preview:} Lesson 1.3, \emph{Functions} --- the third Algebra 1 domain area, where the
equations from today become the machinery for finding a function's \textbf{zeros}: every zero is
the solution of $f(x)=0$, so today's factoring and quadratic formula are how the $x$-intercepts get
found, and today's inequality is how domain restrictions get written.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
\small
\textbf{Item 1 is Lesson 1.0 being cashed in.} Refuse ``I moved it over'' out loud --- the whole
point of 1.0 was that each move has a name and the name is what makes it legal. If the room cannot
produce ``subtraction property of equality,'' spend two extra minutes here; the notes ask for the
same column three more times today.

\textbf{Item 2 delivers the zero product property without announcing it.} Students state it
themselves in the fill-in sentence, and the ``does it work for eight?'' question is the guard that
matters --- from $(x-2)(x+5)=8$ nothing follows, and that error appears every year. Say the reason:
zero is the only number with no way to be built from two nonzero factors.

\textbf{Item 3 motivates graphing.} Take the ``how many numbers'' answer publicly. Because the set
is infinite, no list can express it, so the picture is not decoration --- it is the only available
notation. Also flag the two circle conventions now: open for $<$ and $>$, closed for $\le$ and
$\ge$, since \textbf{A.EI.1c} grades that detail.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
\small
\textbf{The Hook is the lesson.} Do not rescue students from the vanishing variable in (b) and (c).
The entire day depends on them experiencing ``I got $-6=-5$, so I did it wrong'' and being told
\emph{no, you finished}. Write ``one / none / all'' on the board next to ``two / one / none'' and
leave both up for the whole period.

\textbf{Section 1's property column is graded work, not decoration.} \textbf{A.EI.1b} says students
solve \emph{by applying the properties}, so accepting ``I moved it over'' quietly de-implements the
standard. This is the third lesson in a row asking for the same column; by 1.3 it should be
automatic.

\textbf{Section 3: derive the flip, never assert it.} The $2<6$ / $-2>-6$ demonstration takes
ninety seconds and it is the difference between a rule students forget under pressure and a fact
they can rebuild. The reflection language pays off again in Unit 2, where $y=-x$ is the same
reflection acting on a whole graph.

\textbf{Section 4, the two errors that cost real points.} (i) Not setting the equation to zero
before factoring --- $x^2-3x=10$ becoming $x(x-3)=10$ and then ``$x=10$ or $x-3=10$'' is the single
most common quadratic error on the SOL. (ii) Dropping the negative root in Method 1. Both are
diagnosable from the notes table at a glance while circulating.

\textbf{The discriminant before the graphs, not after.} Have the room compute all three
discriminants first, predict the crossings out loud, \emph{then} look at the three parabolas. If the
figures are shown first, students read the answer off the picture and the predictive point --- the
whole content of \textbf{A.EI.3b} --- is lost.

\textbf{Where this goes next.} Lesson 1.3 defines the \emph{zeros} of a function as the solutions of
$f(x)=0$. Every technique in Section 4 is the machinery for finding $x$-intercepts, and the
inequality work in Section 3 is how domain restrictions get written. Say that explicitly at the
close so 1.3 opens as a continuation rather than a new topic.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
\small
\textbf{Tier R Part 2 is the highest-leverage block on the page.} Students classify without solving,
which strips the task down to the only thing that matters --- reading the leftover statement.
Anyone who called $0=7$ ``zero solutions'' but $5=5$ ``one solution'' has the idea half-formed;
that pair is the diagnostic.

\textbf{Tier A item 7 is worth taking to the whole room} even if only some students reach it. Two
solutions to a height equation are not an artifact --- the ball genuinely is at $48$ feet twice.
Students who have been trained that ``the second answer gets thrown out'' need to hear that the
throwing-out happens only when the context forbids it (negative time, negative lengths), not
because two answers are suspicious.

\textbf{Tier E Part 1 is the hardest thing on the page and the most worthwhile.} It converts the
classification from an observation into a condition on a coefficient --- which is how the idea will
be used from Unit 3 onward. Expect the ``no such $k$'' boxes to be left blank; the point is that
$kx+6=3x+6$ \emph{cannot} be a contradiction, because matching $x$ terms here also means matching
constants.

\textbf{Tier E Part 3 is the first time both halves of the lesson run at once} --- a quadratic
question answered with a linear inequality. If a group finishes early, ask what happens to
$x^2-6x+c=0$ as $c$ passes through $9$, and connect it to the parabola sliding up until it just
touches the $x$-axis and then clears it. That picture is Unit 3's opening move.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
\small
\textbf{Sort the tickets into four piles:} \emph{got it} / \emph{classification} (item 1 answered
``one solution,'' or abandoned mid-page because the $x$ disappeared) / \emph{inequality direction}
(item 3 --- A is the flip, C and D divide by $+3$) / \emph{zero-first} (item 2 attacked as
$x(x-3)=10$).

\textbf{The zero-first pile is the one to act on.} ``$x(x-3)=10$, so $x=10$ or $x-3=10$'' has the
zero product property without its precondition --- the most common quadratic error on the SOL, and
the one that matters most for Lesson 1.3, where every \emph{zero of a function} is found by solving
exactly this kind of equation. Re-run item 2 with new numbers at the top of 1.3 if the pile is large.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
\small
\textbf{Grade problem 2 first.} It takes thirty seconds per paper and it separates students who can
\emph{interpret} a result from students who can only compute one. A student who is fluent everywhere
else but writes ``one solution'' for $7=7$ needs one sentence of correction, not a reteach.

\textbf{Problem 6(d) is the one that carries.} $x^2+7x=-10$ is the only item on the page whose first
move is not the obvious one, and a student who attacks it as $x(x+7)=-10$ will produce confident
nonsense. Pair it with problem 8, which asks for the same idea in words --- if a student gets 6(d)
right but 8 wrong, they have the procedure without the reason and will lose it by Unit 3.

\textbf{Problem 5(b) rewards reading, not algebra.} $s\le31.875$ becomes ``$31$ shirts,'' and
rounding to $32$ blows the budget. \textbf{A.EI.1f} asks students to \emph{interpret} solutions in
context, and this is where that gets graded --- accept no answer that stops at the decimal.

\textbf{The Extension is optional but item (3) is the best question on the page.} It turns the
discriminant into a statement about a vertex sliding down through the $x$-axis, which is exactly the
Unit 3 picture. If any student writes that connection unprompted, put it on the board.
\end{teachernote}
