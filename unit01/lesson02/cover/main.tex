\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item \ldots{} \textbf{solve} a multistep linear equation and \textbf{name the property of equality} behind each step --- not just ``move it over'' --- then \textbf{verify} the answer in the original.
  \item \ldots{} decide whether a linear equation has \textbf{one solution, no solution, or infinitely many}, and explain how the last line of my work told me which.
  \item \ldots{} solve a multistep \textbf{inequality}, \textbf{reverse} the symbol when I multiply or divide by a negative --- and say why --- then \textbf{graph} the solution set on a number line with the right circle at the endpoint.
  \item \ldots{} rearrange a \textbf{literal equation} or formula to solve for a specified variable.
  \item \ldots{} \textbf{write} an equation or inequality from a described situation and interpret my answer as a sentence about that situation, with units.
  \item \ldots{} solve a \textbf{quadratic} three ways --- square roots, factoring with the zero product property, and the quadratic formula --- and use the \textbf{discriminant} to say how many real solutions exist \emph{before} solving, matching that count to the graph's $x$-intercepts.
\end{enumerate}
\end{learningtargetbox}

\begin{remindbox}
\small In Lesson 1.1 an \textbf{expression} was a recipe for a number. An \textbf{equation} is a
\textbf{question}: \emph{which values make this true?} The answer is a \textbf{solution set}, and it
need not hold exactly one number. A linear equation has \textbf{one} solution, \textbf{no} solution,
or \textbf{infinitely many}, and the last line tells you which: if the variable vanishes leaving
something \emph{true}, everything works; if it leaves something \emph{false}, nothing does. The
variable disappearing is information, not a mistake.
\\[3pt]
Every step must produce an \textbf{equivalent} equation --- same question, easier wording. That is
what the \textbf{properties of equality} are for, and why every line of your work should have a
name. Inequalities obey the same properties with \textbf{one} exception: multiplying or dividing by
a negative \textbf{reverses} the symbol. That is not a convention --- multiplying by a negative
reflects the number line across zero, and reflection reverses order: $2<6$, but $-2>-6$.
\\[3pt]
A quadratic has \textbf{two, one, or no} real solutions, and the \textbf{discriminant} $b^2-4ac$
tells you which before you solve --- the same fact the graph tells you by how often it crosses the
$x$-axis. To factor, first make one side \textbf{zero}: $mn=0$ forces $m=0$ or $n=0$, while $mn=8$
forces nothing at all. And in 1.1 stopping short of \emph{completely} cost you a step; here it costs
you a \textbf{solution}.
\end{remindbox}
