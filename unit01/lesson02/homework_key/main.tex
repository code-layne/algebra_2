\begin{notesbox}{Practice}
Every solution gets \textbf{checked}. Every inequality gets \textbf{graphed}. Every quadratic gets
one side set to \textbf{zero} before anything else happens.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=8pt]
  \item \textbf{Solve, and justify one step.} For each equation, give the solution and name one
        property of equality you used along the way.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
    \hline
    \textbf{Equation} & \textbf{Solution} & \textbf{One property you used} \\
    \hline
    (a) \; $4x-9=3x+2$ & $x=$ \ans{$11$} & \ans{subtraction property of equality (subtract $3x$)} \\
    (b) \; $6(x+2)=4x-2$ & $x=$ \ans{$-7$} & \ans{distributive property, then division by $2$} \\
    (c) \; $\dfrac{x}{3}-5=1$ & $x=$ \ans{$18$} & \ans{multiplication property of equality (times $3$)} \\
    (d) \; $3(2x-1)+4=5x+9$ & $x=$ \ans{$8$} & \ans{subtraction property of equality (subtract $5x$)} \\
    \hline
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}
    Check your answer to (d) in the original equation.
    \ans{$3(16-1)+4=45+4=49$ \; and \; $5(8)+9=49$}

  \item \textbf{How many solutions?} You are given only the \emph{last line} of each student's work.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}l l l l@{}}
    (a) \; $7=7$ \; $\to$ \ans{infinitely many} &
    (b) \; $x=-2$ \; $\to$ \ans{one} \\[6pt]
    (c) \; $4=9$ \; $\to$ \ans{none} &
    (d) \; $0=0$ \; $\to$ \ans{infinitely many} \\
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    Which of the four came from an \textbf{identity}? \ans{(a) and (d)} \quad From a
    \textbf{contradiction}? \ans{(c)}

  \item \textbf{Literal equations.} Solve each for the variable named.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
    \hline
    (a) \; $V=\ell wh$ & solve for $w$ & \ans{$w=\dfrac{V}{\ell h}$} \\
    (b) \; $2x+5y=20$ & solve for $y$ & \ans{$y=\dfrac{20-2x}{5}=4-\tfrac25 x$} \\
    (c) \; $cx-4x=d$ & solve for $x$ & \ans{$x=\dfrac{d}{c-4}$, \; $c\ne4$} \\
    \hline
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}
    Item (c) is different from the other two. What did you have to do first, and why?
    \ansline{Factor $x$ out: $x(c-4)=d$. The target variable appeared in two terms, and you cannot
    divide it away until it appears exactly once.}

  \item \textbf{Solve and graph.} Shade each solution set. Open $\circ$ for $<$ and $>$, closed
        $\bullet$ for $\le$ and $\ge$.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X c@{}}
    \hline
    (a) \; $3x-5\le 4$ \qquad $x$ \ans{$\le 3$} & \numline{\closedleft{3}} \\
    (b) \; $-2x+1>9$ \qquad $x$ \ans{$<-4$} & \numline{\openleft{-4}} \\
    (c) \; $5(x+1)\ge 3x+7$ \qquad $x$ \ans{$\ge 1$} & \numline{\closedright{1}} \\
    (d) \; $2(x-1)<x+3$ \qquad $x$ \ans{$<5$} & \numline{\openleft{5}} \\
    \hline
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}
    Exactly one of those four made you reverse the symbol. Which, and at which step?
    \ans{(b), when dividing both sides of $-2x>8$ by $-2$}

  \item \textbf{Write it, solve it, say it.} Answer each in a sentence with units --- not as
        ``$x=$ something.''
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item A gym charges a \$25 sign-up fee plus \$12 per month. Ana has paid \$121 in total.
            \\[2pt]
            Equation: \ans{$25+12m=121$} \quad Answer:
            \ansline{Ana has been a member for $8$ months.}
      \item A club orders T-shirts: a \$45 setup fee plus \$8 per shirt, and the budget is
            \textbf{at most} \$300.
            \\[2pt]
            Inequality: \ans{$45+8s\le 300$} \quad Answer:
            \ansline{$s\le 31.875$, so the club can order at most $31$ shirts --- shirts come in
            whole numbers, so the decimal has to be rounded \emph{down}, not to the nearest.}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Quadratics --- pick your method.} Two are square-root problems, two factor, and two
        need the formula (leave those in simplest radical form).
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.9}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}l l@{}}
    (a) \; $x^2=64$, \quad $x=$ \ans{$\pm 8$} &
    (b) \; $3x^2-27=0$, \quad $x=$ \ans{$\pm 3$} \\
    (c) \; $x^2-2x-24=0$, \quad $x=$ \ans{$6,\ -4$} &
    (d) \; $x^2+7x=-10$, \quad $x=$ \ans{$-5,\ -2$} \\
    (e) \; $x^2-6x+2=0$, \quad $x=$ \ans{$3\pm\sqrt7$} &
    (f) \; $2x^2+4x-1=0$, \quad $x=$ \ans{$\dfrac{-2\pm\sqrt6}{2}$} \\
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    Which item required a Step 0 the others did not, and what was it?
    \ans{(d) --- add $10$ to both sides first so one side is $0$} \\
    Verify one solution of (c) by substituting it back.
    \ans{At $x=6$: $36-12-24=0$. \; (Or at $x=-4$: $16+8-24=0$.)}

  \item \textbf{Count before you solve.} Fill the table in \emph{without} solving anything.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X l@{}}
    \hline
    \textbf{Equation} & $b^2-4ac$ & \textbf{Positive / zero / negative?} & \textbf{\# real solutions} \\
    \hline
    (a) \; $x^2+5x+6=0$ & \ans{$1$} & \ans{positive} & \ans{two} \\
    (b) \; $x^2+4x+4=0$ & \ans{$0$} & \ans{zero} & \ans{one} \\
    (c) \; $x^2+x+5=0$ & \ans{$-19$} & \ans{negative} & \ans{none} \\
    (d) \; $3x^2-2x-1=0$ & \ans{$16$} & \ans{positive} & \ans{two} \\
    \hline
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}
    One of those four has a graph that touches the $x$-axis without crossing it. Which?
    \ans{(b)}

  \item \textbf{Explain.} A classmate solves $(x+1)(x-4)=6$ by writing ``$x+1=6$ or $x-4=6$.''
        Explain what is wrong, why the zero product property needs the zero, and then solve the
        equation correctly.
        \ansline{The zero product property applies only when the product is \emph{zero}. Six can be
        written as a product of two nonzero numbers in infinitely many ways ($6\cdot1$, $3\cdot2$,
        $12\cdot\frac12$, \dots), so knowing the product is $6$ forces nothing about either factor.
        Zero is the only number that cannot be built from two nonzero factors --- that uniqueness is
        the whole property.\\[3pt]
        Correctly: expand to $x^2-3x-4=6$, then set one side to zero: $x^2-3x-10=0$, so
        $(x-5)(x+2)=0$ and $x=5$ or $x=-2$. Check: $(5+1)(5-4)=6$ \; and \; $(-2+1)(-2-4)=6$.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{extensionbox}
\textbf{The solution-count audit.} In each problem below, a letter sits \emph{inside} the equation.
Your job is to say which values of that letter produce each possible number of solutions --- so the
count becomes something you control rather than something you discover.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.7}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Number of solutions} & \textbf{For which $c$ does} $5x+c=5x+8$ ? & \textbf{For which $b$ does} $x^2+bx+9=0$ have that many \emph{real} solutions? \\
\hline
two & --- & \ans{$b<-6$ or $b>6$} \\
exactly one & \ans{no such $c$} & \ans{$b=6$ or $b=-6$} \\
none & \ans{every $c\ne8$} & \ans{$-6<b<6$} \\
infinitely many & \ans{$c=8$} & --- \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item One box in the left column is impossible to fill. Which, and why can that never happen for
        $5x+c=5x+8$?
        \ansline{``Exactly one.'' The $5x$ terms are identical on both sides, so they always cancel
        --- the variable can never survive, and a surviving variable is the only way to get exactly
        one solution.}
  \item A linear equation \emph{can} have infinitely many solutions. A quadratic equation with
        $a\ne0$ never can. Explain why not --- what is it about the $x^2$ term that makes an
        identity impossible?
        \ansline{For an identity, everything containing the variable has to cancel. In a quadratic
        with $a\ne0$ the $ax^2$ term cannot be cancelled by anything else present, so the variable
        never fully disappears --- and the quadratic formula then hands back at most two values,
        because the only choice in it is the $\pm$.}
  \item In the right column, the answers to ``two'' and ``none'' are ranges of $b$, not single
        values. Say what that means about the graph of $y=x^2+bx+9$ as $b$ increases from $0$
        through $6$ and past it.
        \ansline{At $b=0$ the parabola $y=x^2+9$ sits entirely above the $x$-axis --- no real zeros.
        As $b$ grows, the vertex $\left(-\frac b2,\ 9-\frac{b^2}{4}\right)$ slides left and
        \emph{down}. At exactly $b=6$ the vertex lands on the axis at $(-3,0)$ and the graph touches
        without crossing --- one solution. Past $b=6$ the vertex is below the axis and the graph cuts
        it twice. The discriminant is just an algebraic way of asking how far down that vertex has
        gone.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{extensionbox}

\begin{spiralbox}
\textbf{Coming next --- Lesson 1.3: Functions.} The third and last Algebra 1 domain area, and
today's equations become its machinery. A function's \textbf{zeros} are defined as the solutions of
$f(x)=0$ --- so every technique in problem 6 is a way of finding where a graph crosses the
$x$-axis, and the discriminant in problem 7 is a way of counting those crossings before you draw
anything. The inequalities in problem 4 are how a function's \textbf{domain} gets written down when
some inputs are not allowed. And the literal equations in problem 3 are the reason $y=mx+b$ has
three different ``forms'' in Unit 2: it is one equation, solved for different things.
\end{spiralbox}

\begin{teachernote}
\small
\textbf{Grade problem 2 first.} It takes thirty seconds per paper and it separates students who can
\emph{interpret} a result from students who can only compute one. A student who is fluent everywhere
else but writes ``one solution'' for $7=7$ needs one sentence of correction, not a reteach.

\textbf{Problem 6(d) is the one that carries.} $x^2+7x=-10$ is the only item on the page whose first
move is not the obvious one, and a student who attacks it as $x(x+7)=-10$ will produce confident
nonsense. Pair it with problem 8, which asks for the same idea in words --- if a student gets 6(d)
right but 8 wrong, they have the procedure without the reason and will lose it by Unit 3.

\textbf{Problem 5(b) rewards reading, not algebra.} $s\le31.875$ becomes ``$31$ shirts,'' and
rounding to $32$ blows the budget. \textbf{A.EI.1f} asks students to \emph{interpret} solutions in
context, and this is where that gets graded --- accept no answer that stops at the decimal.

\textbf{The Extension is optional but item (3) is the best question on the page.} It turns the
discriminant into a statement about a vertex sliding down through the $x$-axis, which is exactly the
Unit 3 picture. If any student writes that connection unprompted, put it on the board.
\end{teachernote}
