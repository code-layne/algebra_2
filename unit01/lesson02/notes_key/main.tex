\boxguard[14]
\begin{objectivebox}
\small
By the end of these notes I will be able to:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item read $|x-h|$ as the \textbf{distance} between $x$ and $h$, and say why an absolute value
        equation usually has \emph{two} solutions;
  \item \textbf{isolate} the bars, then split into two cases --- and handle the cases with one
        solution or none;
  \item solve $|X|<c$ and $|X|>c$, say which gives \emph{one} interval and which gives
        \emph{two} rays, and write the answer three ways.
\end{enumerate}
\end{objectivebox}

\vspace{6pt}

\boxguard[16]
\begin{vocabbox}
\small
Fill in each term \textbf{as we name it} in the notes below.
\par
\termans{Absolute value as distance}{$|x-h|$ is how far $x$ is from $h$ on the number line --- never negative.}
\par
\termans{Two-case rule}{$|X|=c$ with $c>0$ splits into $X=c$ or $X=-c$. Isolate the bars first.}
\par
\termans{``And'' vs.\ ``or'' inequality}{$|X|<c$: one interval \emph{between} ($-c<X<c$). $|X|>c$: two rays \emph{outside}.}
\par
\termans{Interval notation}{$[7,17]$ --- a bracket includes the endpoint, a parenthesis excludes it; $\infty$ always gets a parenthesis.}
\par
\termans{No solution}{$|X|=c$ or $|X|<c$ with $c<0$: a distance is never negative, so no $x$ works.}
\par
\end{vocabbox}

% ── I DO: section 1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[14]
\begin{notesbox}{1. Absolute Value Is a Distance --- So It Answers Twice}
\small
\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.50\linewidth}
\[ |x-h| \;=\; \text{the distance between } x \text{ and } h \]
A hiker at mile $x$ is \ans{$|x-12|$} miles from the tower. A distance is never \ans{negative},
and \emph{two} different spots sit the same distance out --- one on each side.
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.46\linewidth}\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.30cm, y=0.42cm]
  \trail
  \draw[very thick, redacc] (7,0.55)--(12,0.55);
  \draw[very thick, redacc] (12,0.55)--(17,0.55);
  \fill[redacc] (7,0) circle (2.8pt);
  \fill[redacc] (17,0) circle (2.8pt);
  \node[redacc, font=\tiny, above] at (9.5,0.6){$5$};
  \node[redacc, font=\tiny, above] at (14.5,0.6){$5$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}
\textbf{Exactly five miles out.} ``Distance from the tower is $5$'' is $|x-12|=5$. The bars
hide \emph{two} equations --- one for each side of the tower:
\begin{work}
  |x - 12| &= 5 \\
  x - 12 &= 5 \quad\text{or}\quad x - 12 = -5 \\
  x &= 17 \quad\text{or}\quad x = 7
\end{work}
Both markers from the hook, straight out of the algebra. \textbf{Verify} by putting each back
in: $|17-12| =$ \ans{$5$} and $|7-12| =$ \ans{$5$}. That is the \textbf{two-case rule}:
$|X| = c$ splits into $X = c$ \emph{or} $X = -c$.

\vspace{8pt}
\textbf{\textcolor{forest}{Isolate the Bars \emph{Before} You Split.}}
The two-case rule is a statement about $|X|$ \emph{alone}. Anything outside the bars gets undone
first --- treat the bars exactly as you would a variable.

\vspace{3pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
\rowcolor{forestbg}
\textbf{$|X| = c$ when\dots} & \textbf{Split into} & \textbf{How many solutions --- and why} \\
$c > 0$ & $X =$ \ans{$c$} \; or \; $X =$ \ans{$-c$} & \ans{two} --- two spots sit that far out \\
$c = 0$ & $X =$ \ans{$0$} & \ans{one} --- only one spot is \emph{zero} away \\
$c < 0$ & --- & \ans{none} --- a distance is never negative \\
\end{tabularx}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Undo, then split.} Solve $3|x+1| - 2 = 10$.
\begin{work}
  3|x+1| - 2 &= 10 \\
  3|x+1| &= 12 \\
  |x+1| &= 4 \\
  x + 1 &= 4 \quad\text{or}\quad x + 1 = -4 \\
  x &= 3 \quad\text{or}\quad x = -5
\end{work}
Splitting at the \emph{first} line would give $3x+1-2=10$ --- a single answer, and a
\ans{wrong} one.
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[34]
\begin{notesbox}{2. One Piece or Two --- and Three Ways to Write It}
\small
Same expression, same $5$, one symbol apart --- and the two answers look nothing alike.

\vspace{3pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|X@{}}
\rowcolor{forestbg}
\textbf{In range: $|x-12| \le 5$} & \textbf{Out of range: $|x-12| \ge 5$} \\
\emph{Closer} to the tower than $5$: \textbf{between}. &
\emph{Farther} from the tower than $5$: \textbf{outside}. \\
One compound inequality, joined by \ans{and}: &
Two separate statements, joined by \ans{or}: \\
$-5 \le x-12 \le 5$, so \; \ans{$7$} $\le x \le$ \ans{$17$} &
$x-12 \le -5$ \; or \; $x-12 \ge 5$, so \; $x \le$ \ans{$7$} \, or \, $x \ge$ \ans{$17$} \\
\centering\arraybackslash
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.20cm, y=0.40cm, baseline=-2pt]
  \trail
  \draw[very thick, greenacc] (7,0)--(17,0);
  \fill[greenacc] (7,0) circle (3.6pt);
  \fill[greenacc] (17,0) circle (3.6pt);
\end{tikzpicture}
&
\centering\arraybackslash
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.20cm, y=0.40cm, baseline=-2pt]
  \trail
  \draw[very thick, redacc] (0,0)--(7,0);
  \draw[very thick, redacc] (17,0)--(20,0);
  \fill[redacc] (7,0) circle (3.6pt);
  \fill[redacc] (17,0) circle (3.6pt);
\end{tikzpicture}
\\
\ans{one} connected piece & \ans{two} separate pieces \\
Set: $\{x \mid$ \ans{$7 \le x \le 17$} $\}$ & Set: $\{x \mid$ \ans{$x \le 7$ or $x \ge 17$} $\}$ \\
Interval: \ans{$[7,17]$} & Interval: $(-\infty,$ \ans{$7$} $] \cup [$ \ans{$17$} $, \infty)$ \\
\end{tabularx}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{The endpoint carries the symbol.} A bracket \textbf{[ ]} and a \ans{filled} dot mean
the endpoint is \emph{in} ($\le$, $\ge$); a parenthesis \textbf{( )} and an \ans{open} dot
mean it is \emph{out} ($<$, $>$). Next to $\infty$ you always write a \ans{parenthesis}, because
$\infty$ is not a number you can reach. Two rays need two intervals and a $\cup$.

\vspace{8pt}
\textbf{\textcolor{forest}{The Crux: Test One Hiker, and Watch the Two Disagree.}}
Students who treat $\le$ and $\ge$ as ``nearly the same'' get the \emph{opposite} set. Prove it
with one hiker.

\vspace{3pt}
\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{goldacc}{goldbg}{\parbox{0.94\linewidth}{\small
A hiker at \textbf{mile 3} is $|3-12| =$ \ans{$9$} miles from the tower. Is $9 \le 5$?
\ans{no} \quad Is $9 \ge 5$? \ans{yes} \quad So mile $3$ belongs to \emph{exactly one}
of the two answers above --- \ans{out of range}. Picking the wrong symbol does not shift the answer a
little; it hands you the opposite set.}}
\end{center}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{The odd cases, from the same picture.} $|x-12| < -2$ asks for a distance less than a
negative number: \ans{no solution}. $|x-12| \ge -2$ asks for a distance at least $-2$, which every
mile satisfies: \ans{all real numbers}. Neither needs algebra --- only the meaning of \emph{distance}.
\end{notesbox}

% ── WE DO: guided practice ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[26]
\begin{practicebox}
\small
\textbf{We work this one together.} Solve $2|x-5| - 3 = 7$, then change one symbol and watch
the answer change shape.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*.}, leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Isolate the bars, then split.
        \begin{work}
          2|x-5| - 3 &= 7 \\
          2|x-5| &= 10 \\
          |x-5| &= 5 \\
          x - 5 &= 5 \quad\text{or}\quad x - 5 = -5 \\
          x &= 10 \quad\text{or}\quad x = 0
        \end{work}
        Verify one: \ans{$x=10$: $2|5|-3 = 7$ \checkmark}
  \item Now $2|x-5| - 3 \le 7$. Closer than or farther than? \ans{closer than} \quad One piece or
        two? \ans{one} \\[3pt]
        Solution: \ans{$0 \le x \le 10$} \quad Interval: \ans{$[0,10]$} \quad
        \begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.36cm, y=0.42cm, baseline=-2pt]
          \numline{-2}{12}
          \draw[very thick, keyred] (0,0)--(10,0);
          \fill[keyred] (0,0) circle (2.8pt);
          \fill[keyred] (10,0) circle (2.8pt);
        \end{tikzpicture}
  \item And $2|x-5| - 3 \ge 7$? \ans{two} piece(s). \quad
        Solution: \ans{$x \le 0$ or $x \ge 10$} \\[3pt]
        Interval: \ans{$(-\infty,0] \cup [10,\infty)$} \quad
        \begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.36cm, y=0.42cm, baseline=-2pt]
          \numline{-2}{12}
          \draw[very thick, keyred] (-2.5,0)--(0,0);
          \draw[very thick, keyred] (10,0)--(12.5,0);
          \fill[keyred] (0,0) circle (2.8pt);
          \fill[keyred] (10,0) circle (2.8pt);
        \end{tikzpicture}
\end{enumerate}
\end{practicebox}
