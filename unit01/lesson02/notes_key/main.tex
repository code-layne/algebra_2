\begin{objectivebox}
\textbf{By the end of this lesson, I will be able to\dots}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item solve multistep linear equations and inequalities in one variable, \emph{naming the property} behind every step;
  \item decide whether a linear equation has \emph{one}, \emph{no}, or \emph{infinitely many} solutions;
  \item graph an inequality's solution set on a number line and rearrange a \emph{literal} equation for a specified variable;
  \item write an equation or inequality from a situation and interpret the answer back in that situation;
  \item solve a quadratic by square roots, by factoring with the \emph{zero product property}, and by the \emph{quadratic formula};
  \item use the \emph{discriminant} to say how many real solutions exist before solving --- and match it to the graph.
\end{itemize}
\end{objectivebox}

\vspace{0.05in}

\begin{vocabbox}
\small
Fill in each term as we build it together.
\par\vspace{2pt}
\vocabans{Equation (vs.\ expression) / solution set}{Two expressions joined by $=$. An expression is a recipe and is \emph{evaluated}; an equation asks a question and is \emph{solved}. Its solution set is the set of all values that make it true.}
\vocabans{Properties of equality}{Add, subtract, multiply, or divide both sides by the same quantity (never divide by $0$). They are the only moves that produce an \emph{equivalent} equation --- same solution set, easier wording.}
\vocabans{Identity vs.\ contradiction}{An identity is true for every value (both sides are the same expression), so the solution set is all real numbers. A contradiction is true for no value, so the solution set is empty.}
\vocabans{Literal equation}{An equation containing several letters, solved for one specified variable --- e.g.\ $A=\frac12 bh$ solved for $h$ gives $h=\frac{2A}{b}$.}
\vocabans{Properties of inequality}{Identical to the properties of equality with one exception: multiplying or dividing both sides by a \emph{negative} number reverses the inequality symbol.}
\vocabans{Zero product property}{If $mn=0$ then $m=0$ or $n=0$. It is what turns a factorization into solutions --- and it works only against zero.}
\vocabans{Discriminant}{The quantity $b^2-4ac$ under the radical in the quadratic formula. Positive gives two real solutions, zero gives one, negative gives none.}
\end{vocabbox}

\boxguard
\begin{hookbox}
\textbf{Solve all three.} Nothing else --- just solve them.

\vspace{2pt}
(a) \; $3(x-2)=x-6$
\begin{work}
  3(x-2) &= x-6 \\
    3x-6 &= x-6 \\
    2x-6 &= -6  \\
      2x &= 0   \\
       x &= 0
\end{work}

(b) \; $3(x-2)=3x-6$
\begin{work}
  3(x-2) &= 3x-6 \\
    3x-6 &= 3x-6 \\
      -6 &= -6
\end{work}

(c) \; $3(x-2)=3x-5$
\begin{work}
  3(x-2) &= 3x-5 \\
    3x-6 &= 3x-5 \\
      -6 &= -5
\end{work}
Two of those did something you were not expecting: the variable \ans{disappeared}. That is not a
mistake --- it is the answer arriving in a different costume.
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item In (b) you were left with a statement that is \ans{true}, so how many values of $x$ work? \ans{all of them}
  \item In (c) you were left with a statement that is \ans{false}, so how many values of $x$ work? \ans{none}
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Now the same question in another family.} How many real solutions does each have?
\quad $x^2=9$: \ans{2} \quad $x^2=0$: \ans{1} \quad $x^2=-9$: \ans{0}
\\[2pt]
\textbf{The idea the whole lesson hangs on:} an expression was a \emph{recipe}; an equation is a
\textbf{question}. The answer is a \emph{solution set}, and it is allowed to hold one number, no
numbers, or every number. By the end of today you will know the count \textbf{before} you solve.
\end{hookbox}

\begin{notesbox}{1. Linear Equations --- and How Many Answers They Have}
An \textbf{equation} joins two expressions with $=$. Unlike an expression, it asks a
\ans{question}, and a \textbf{solution} is a value that makes it \ans{true}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Solve, and name every move.} The \emph{properties of equality} are the only moves that keep
the solution set the same --- which is exactly why they are allowed and nothing else is.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Line} & \textbf{Property that makes it legal} \\
\hline
$5x+3(x-2)=2(x+9)$ & \emph{given} \\
\ans{$5x+3x-6=2x+18$} & distributive property \\
$8x-6=2x+18$ & \emph{combine like terms} \\
\ans{$6x-6=18$} & \ans{subtraction property of equality (subtract $2x$)} \\
\ans{$6x=24$} & \ans{addition property of equality (add $6$)} \\
$x=$ \ans{$4$} & \ans{division property of equality (divide by $6$)} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\textbf{Verify it.} Substitute your answer into the \emph{original} equation --- not into a line
partway down, where a mistake would already be baked in.
\\[2pt]
Left side: \ans{$5(4)+3(4-2)=20+6=26$} \qquad Right side: \ans{$2(4+9)=26$} \qquad Equal? \ans{yes}
\\[2pt]
Checking is not politeness. It is the \ans{definition} of the word ``solution.''

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Back to the Hook: three possible answers.} Fill in what the \emph{last line} tells you.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X l@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Equation} & \textbf{Last line} & \textbf{Solution set} & \textbf{Name} \\
\hline
$3(x-2)=x-6$ & \ans{$x=0$} & \ans{one solution: $x=0$} & --- \\
$3(x-2)=3x-6$ & \ans{$-6=-6$} & \ans{all real numbers} & \ans{identity} \\
$3(x-2)=3x-5$ & \ans{$-6=-5$} & \ans{no solution (empty)} & \ans{contradiction} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\centering If the variable survives $\to$ \ans{exactly one} solution.
\quad If it vanishes leaving a \textbf{true} statement $\to$ \ans{all real numbers}. \\
If it vanishes leaving a \textbf{false} statement $\to$ \ans{no solution}.}}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Write one from a situation.} A repair shop charges a \$40 flat fee plus \$15 per hour of
labor. A bill came to \$130.
\\[2pt]
Equation: \ans{$40+15h=130$} \qquad Solve: \ans{$h=6$}
\\[2pt]
Now answer the \emph{question that was asked}, in a sentence with units:
\ansline{The repair took $6$ hours of labor. (``$x=6$'' is not an answer to the question that was
asked --- the question was about hours.)}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{2. Literal Equations --- Same Moves, Scarier Alphabet}
A \textbf{literal equation} has several letters. ``Solve for $h$'' means: get $h$ \ans{by itself on
one side}, using the same properties of equality as always.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.7}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Formula} & \textbf{Solve for} & \textbf{Result} \\
\hline
$A=\tfrac12 bh$ \; (area of a triangle) & $h$ & \ans{$h=\dfrac{2A}{b}$} \\
$P=2\ell+2w$ \; (perimeter) & $w$ & \ans{$w=\dfrac{P-2\ell}{2}$} \\
$y=mx+b$ & $x$ & \ans{$x=\dfrac{y-b}{m}$} \\
$C=\tfrac59(F-32)$ & $F$ & \ans{$F=\tfrac95 C+32$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\textbf{The one real trap.} In $P=2\ell+2w$, are $2\ell$ and $2w$ like terms? \ans{No} \;
So you may \emph{not} write $P=4\ell w$. And when you divide, you must divide \ans{every} term,
not just the one you were looking at.
\\[2pt]
\emph{Coming attraction:} in Unit 2 you will meet $y=mx+b$ rearranged three different ways and hear
them called ``forms.'' They are not three facts --- they are one equation, solved for different
things.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{3. Linear Inequalities --- and Why the Symbol Flips}
Everything about solving is the same \dots\ except for one move. Let's find it rather than announce it.

\vspace{2pt}
\fbox{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{Start with a statement that is definitely true: \quad $2<6$.
\\[3pt]
Multiply \emph{both sides} by $-1$: \quad $-2$ \ans{$>$} $-6$ \quad
(write in the symbol that is actually true) \\[3pt]
What happened to the order? \ans{It reversed --- the bigger number became the smaller one.}}}
\\[2pt]
On the number line, multiplying by a negative \ans{reflects} everything across zero --- and a
reflection \ans{reverses} the order. So the rule is not a convention somebody chose:
\\[2pt]
\textbf{Multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number \ans{reverses} the inequality symbol.}
(Adding or subtracting a negative does \emph{not} --- only multiplying and dividing.)

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Solve and graph.} Shade the solution set on the number line. Endpoint circles:
\emph{open} $\circ$ for $<$ and $>$, \emph{closed} $\bullet$ for $\le$ and $\ge$.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X c@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Solve} & \textbf{Graph the solution set} \\
\hline
(a) \; $4x-9\le 11$ \qquad $x$ \ans{$\le 5$} & \numline{\closedleft{5}} \\
(b) \; $-3x+7>19$ \qquad $x$ \ans{$<-4$} & \numline{\openleft{-4}} \\
(c) \; $2(x+4)\ge 6x-4$ \qquad $x$ \ans{$\le 3$} & \numline{\closedleft{3}} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Which one made you flip the symbol, and at exactly which step?
\ansline{Item (b), at the last step: dividing both sides of $-3x>12$ by $-3$. Note that (c) does
\emph{not} flip if you collect the $x$ terms on the right ($12\ge 4x$) --- both routes are legal and
both end at $x\le3$.}
In (c) you may end with $3\ge x$. Rewrite it as $x$ \ans{$\le$} $3$ before you graph --- reading
the shading off a backwards inequality is how the picture ends up on the wrong side.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Write one from a situation.} Concert tickets cost \$12 each and you can spend
\textbf{at most} \$75.
\\[2pt]
``At most'' translates to the symbol \ans{$\le$}. \quad Inequality: \ans{$12t\le 75$} \quad
Solve: $t$ \ans{$\le 6.25$}
\\[2pt]
The algebra says $t\le 6.25$. How many tickets can you actually buy, and why is the answer not
$6.25$?
\ansline{At most $6$ tickets. Tickets come in whole numbers, so the context restricts $t$ to
non-negative integers; the algebra's answer has to be interpreted, not just reported.}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{4. Quadratic Equations --- Three Methods and a Head Count}
A \textbf{quadratic equation} can be written $ax^2+bx+c=0$ with $a\ne$ \ans{$0$}. Which method
you use depends on what the equation \emph{looks like}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Method 1 --- Square roots} (use when there is no $x$ term). Isolate the square, then take
the root of both sides. \textbf{Both} roots: the $\pm$ is not optional.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
(a) \; $x^2=49$, \; $x=$ \ans{$\pm 7$} &
(b) \; $2x^2-50=0$, \; $x=$ \ans{$\pm 5$} &
(c) \; $(x-1)^2=16$, \; $x=$ \ans{$5$ or $-3$} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Writing only $x=7$ in (a) does not lose a step --- it loses \ans{a solution}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Method 2 --- Factor, then the zero product property} (use when it factors).
\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.95\linewidth}{\centering \textbf{Zero product property:} if $mn=0$ then
\ans{$m=0$} or \ans{$n=0$}. \\
\textbf{Step 0, every single time:} make one side equal \ans{$0$} first.}}
\end{center}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Equation} & \textbf{Set to zero, then factor completely} & \textbf{Solutions} \\
\hline
$x^2+2x-15=0$ & \ans{$(x+5)(x-3)=0$} & \ans{$x=-5,\ 3$} \\
$x^2-3x=10$ & \ans{$x^2-3x-10=0$, so $(x-5)(x+2)=0$} & \ans{$x=5,\ -2$} \\
$2x^2-18=0$ & \ans{$2(x+3)(x-3)=0$} & \ans{$x=\pm 3$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\emph{Why ``completely'' matters more now.} In Lesson 1.1, stopping at $2(x^2-9)$ cost you a step.
Here it costs you \ans{a solution}. \\
\emph{And why the zero is not negotiable:} from $(x-2)(x+5)=8$, may you conclude $x-2=8$?
\ans{No} \; Why not?
\ansline{Eight can be written as a product of two nonzero numbers in infinitely many ways
($8\cdot1$, $4\cdot2$, $16\cdot\frac12$, \dots), so knowing the product is $8$ forces nothing about
either factor. Zero is the only number that cannot be built from two nonzero factors --- which is
the entire reason the property exists.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Method 3 --- The quadratic formula} (use when nothing else works --- it always works).
\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.72\linewidth}{\centering \TallMath{x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}}}}
\end{center}
Solve $x^2-4x-7=0$. \quad $a=$ \ans{$1$} \quad $b=$ \ans{$-4$} \quad $c=$ \ans{$-7$}
\\[2pt]
Substitute and simplify:
\begin{work}
  x &= \frac{4\pm\sqrt{(-4)^2-4(1)(-7)}}{2(1)} \\
    &= \frac{4\pm\sqrt{16+28}}{2} \\
    &= \frac{4\pm\sqrt{44}}{2}    \\
    &= \frac{4\pm 2\sqrt{11}}{2}  \\
    &= 2\pm\sqrt{11}
\end{work}
--- these are \textbf{irrational} solutions, and simplifying that
radical is Lesson 1.1 doing its job.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{The head count: the discriminant.} The piece under the radical, $b^2-4ac$, decides
everything \emph{before} you solve. Same $a$ and $b$ below --- only $c$ changes.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X l@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Equation} & $b^2-4ac$ & \textbf{Positive / zero / negative?} & \textbf{\# real solutions} \\
\hline
$x^2-2x-3=0$ & \ans{$16$} & \ans{positive} & \ans{two} \\
$x^2-2x+1=0$ & \ans{$0$} & \ans{zero} & \ans{one} \\
$x^2-2x+3=0$ & \ans{$-8$} & \ans{negative} & \ans{none} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\textbf{The same three facts, drawn.} Below are the graphs of those three, in order. Label each one
with the number of times it crosses the $x$-axis.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}c c c@{}}
\minipara{\x*\x-2*\x-3}{-1.7:3.7} &
\minipara{\x*\x-2*\x+1}{-1.3:3.3} &
\minipara{\x*\x-2*\x+3}{-1.0:3.0} \\
crossings: \ans{2} & crossings: \ans{1} & crossings: \ans{0} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The discriminant and the picture are the \ans{same} fact told twice --- a real solution
\emph{is} an $x$-intercept.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Read the graph, then confirm with algebra.} This is $y=x^2-2x-3$.
\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.36\linewidth}\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=0.45cm, y=0.32cm]
  \draw[xstep=1, ystep=1, linegray!45, very thin] (-3,-5) grid (5,4);
  \draw[->, charcoal, thick] (-3.3,0) -- (5.3,0) node[right, font=\scriptsize] {$x$};
  \draw[->, charcoal, thick] (0,-5.3) -- (0,4.4) node[above, font=\scriptsize] {$y$};
  \foreach \t in {-2,2,4} {\node[below right=-1pt, font=\tiny] at (\t,0) {$\t$};}
  \draw[forest, very thick, domain=-1.8:3.8, smooth, variable=\x] plot ({\x},{\x*\x-2*\x-3});
  \fill[keyred] (-1,0) circle (2.6pt);
  \fill[keyred] (3,0) circle (2.6pt);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.60\linewidth}
Read the $x$-intercepts off the graph: \; $x=$ \ans{$-1$} and $x=$ \ans{$3$}
\\[4pt]
Now confirm by factoring $x^2-2x-3=0$: \ans{$(x-3)(x+1)=0$}
\\[4pt]
Do they agree? \ans{yes} \; Finish the sentence: solving $x^2-2x-3=0$ is the same task as
finding the \ans{$x$-intercepts (zeros)} of the graph.
\end{minipage}
\end{center}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{practicebox}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item Solve and check: $7(x-2)=3x+6$.
        \begin{work}
          7(x-2) &= 3x+6 \\
           7x-14 &= 3x+6 \\
           4x-14 &= 6    \\
              4x &= 20   \\
               x &= 5
        \end{work}
        Check: \ans{$7(3)=21=3(5)+6$}
  \item How many solutions does $2(3x+4)=6x+8$ have? \ans{infinitely many} \; How do you know?
        \ans{both sides are $6x+8$ --- an identity}
  \item Solve $-5x+3\ge 23$ and graph the solution set.
        \begin{work}
          -5x+3 &\ge 23 \\
            -5x &\ge 20 \\
              x &\le -4
        \end{work}
        \numline{\closedleft{-4}}
  \item Solve for $r$: \; $C=2\pi r$. \quad $r=$ \ans{$\dfrac{C}{2\pi}$}
  \item Without solving, how many real solutions does $x^2+4x+9=0$ have? \ans{none} \;
        Justify with a number: \ans{$b^2-4ac=16-36=-20<0$}
  \item Solve by factoring: $x^2+x=12$.
        \begin{work}
            x^2+x &= 12 \\
         x^2+x-12 &= 0  \\
       (x+4)(x-3) &= 0  \\
                x &= -4 \quad\text{or}\quad x = 3
        \end{work}
        Verify one solution: \ans{$3^2+3=9+3=12$ \; true}
\end{enumerate}
\end{practicebox}
