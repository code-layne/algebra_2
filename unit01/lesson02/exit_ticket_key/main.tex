No notes. Show enough work that a wrong answer can still earn a right idea.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=8pt]
  \item \textbf{Solve, then classify:} $4(x-3)=2(2x-5)+2$.

        \vspace{2pt}
        Work: \ans{$4x-12=4x-10+2$, so $4x-12=4x-8$; subtract $4x$: \; $-12=-8$}

        \vspace{2pt}
        Circle one: \quad one solution \quad$\cdot$\quad \ans{no solution} \quad$\cdot$\quad
        infinitely many solutions

        \vspace{2pt}
        What in your last line told you which?
        \ansline{The variable dropped out and left $-12=-8$, which is \emph{false} --- so no value
        of $x$ makes the equation true. It is a contradiction.}

  \item \textbf{Solve by factoring:} $x^2-3x=10$.

        \vspace{2pt}
        Step 1 --- get one side to zero: \ans{$x^2-3x-10=0$}

        \vspace{2pt}
        Step 2 --- factor and apply the zero product property: \; $x=$ \ans{$5$ or $-2$
        \; (from $(x-5)(x+2)=0$)}

        \vspace{2pt}
        \textbf{Verify one of your solutions} in the original equation.
        \ansline{At $x=5$: \; $5^2-3(5)=25-15=10$. \; (Or at $x=-2$: \; $4+6=10$.)}

  \item \textbf{SOL-style multiple choice.} What is the solution set of \; $-3x+7>19$ \; ?
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\Alph*., leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=2pt]
          \item $x>-4$
          \item $x<-4$ \quad \textcolor{keyred}{\textbf{$\leftarrow$ correct}}
          \item $x>4$
          \item $x<4$
        \end{enumerate}

        \vspace{2pt}
        In one sentence, what did the \emph{wrong} answer you were most tempted by get wrong?
        \ansline{Answers vary --- A keeps the symbol after dividing by a negative, C and D divide
        by $3$ instead of $-3$ and lose the sign of the endpoint.}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{teachernote}
\small
\textbf{Answer 3: B.} $-3x+7>19 \Rightarrow -3x>12 \Rightarrow x<-4$. \textbf{A} does everything
right except reverse the symbol --- the most common error on this standard. \textbf{C} and
\textbf{D} divide by $+3$, producing the wrong endpoint; between them, \textbf{C} also skips the
flip. A student choosing A understands the arithmetic and forgot one rule; a student choosing C or D
has a sign problem underneath.

\textbf{Sort the collected tickets into four piles:} \emph{got it} / \emph{classification} (item 1
answered ``one solution,'' or abandoned mid-page because the $x$ disappeared) / \emph{inequality
direction} (item 3) / \emph{zero-first} (item 2 attacked as $x(x-3)=10$).

\textbf{The zero-first pile is the one to act on.} A student who writes $x(x-3)=10$ and concludes
``$x=10$ or $x-3=10$'' has the zero product property but not its precondition, and that is the
single most common quadratic error on the SOL. It is also the pile that matters most for Lesson 1.3,
where every \emph{zero of a function} is found by solving exactly this kind of equation --- so a
student who cannot set one to zero cannot find an $x$-intercept. Re-run item 2 with new numbers at
the top of 1.3 if the pile is large.

\textbf{Item 1 is the Hook, graded.} If a student wrote ``$x=0$'' or left it blank, the Hook did not
land, and the fix is not more practice --- it is one sentence: the variable vanishing is an answer,
and which answer depends only on whether what remains is true.
\end{teachernote}
