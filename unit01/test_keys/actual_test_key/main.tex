\parthead{Part A --- Vocabulary (8 pts)}
Write the letter of the best description next to each term.

\vspace{4pt}
\noindent
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.34\textwidth}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em, itemsep=7pt, label=\arabic*.]
  \item Counterexample \ans{F}
  \item Closure \ans{C}
  \item Factor completely \ans{A}
  \item Literal equation \ans{G}
  \item Contradiction \ans{D}
  \item Discriminant \ans{B}
  \item Zero of a function \ans{H}
  \item Zero product property \ans{E}
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.63\textwidth}
\small
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item[(A)] To rewrite an expression as a product --- GCF first --- until no factor breaks down further over the integers.
  \item[(B)] The number $b^2-4ac$, read \emph{before} solving to predict how many real solutions a quadratic has.
  \item[(C)] The property that an operation performed on members of a set always produces another member of that set.
  \item[(D)] An equation true for no value at all, so its solution set is empty.
  \item[(E)] If a product equals $0$, then at least one factor equals $0$ --- the rule that turns a factored quadratic into solutions.
  \item[(F)] A single example that proves a general claim false; no number of successes can replace it.
  \item[(G)] An equation with several letters, solved for one letter in terms of the others.
  \item[(H)] An input whose output is $0$; also a solution of $f(x)=0$ and an $x$-intercept of the graph.
\end{description}
\end{minipage}

\parthead{Part B --- Multiple Choice (12 pts)}
Circle the best answer.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=9pt]
  \item Which property justifies the step $7+(3+x)=(7+3)+x$?
    \\[3pt]\hspace*{1.2em} (A) Commutative property of addition
    \hspace{0.9cm} \textbf{(B) Associative property of addition} \ans{$\leftarrow$}
    \\[2pt]\hspace*{1.2em} (C) Distributive property
    \hspace{2.35cm} (D) Additive identity
  \item Simplified with no negative exponents, $\dfrac{24m^7n^{-2}}{8m^3n}$ is
    \hfill \textbf{(A) $\dfrac{3m^4}{n^3}$} \ans{$\leftarrow$} \quad (B) $\dfrac{3m^4}{n}$ \quad (C) $3m^4n^3$ \quad (D) $\dfrac{16m^4}{n^3}$
  \item The solution of $-3x+5> 20$ is
    \hfill (A) $x> -5$ \quad \textbf{(B) $x< -5$} \ans{$\leftarrow$} \quad (C) $x< 5$ \quad (D) $x> 5$
  \item How many \emph{real} solutions does $3x^2+2x+4=0$ have?
    \hfill (A) two \quad (B) one \quad \textbf{(C) none} \ans{$\leftarrow$} \quad (D) infinitely many
  \item Which relation is \emph{not} a function?
    \\[3pt]\hspace*{1.2em} (A) $\{(-4,2),(-1,2),(3,2),(6,2)\}$
    \hspace{0.9cm} \textbf{(B) $\{(2,-4),(2,1),(5,3)\}$} \ans{$\leftarrow$}
    \\[2pt]\hspace*{1.2em} (C) $\{(-1,1),(0,0),(1,1),(2,4)\}$
    \hspace{0.9cm} (D) $y=-2x+9$
  \item \textbf{SOL-style.} Which equation represents the line shown?
    \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}c l@{}}
    \gridplot{\draw[forest, very thick, domain=-5:5, smooth, variable=\x] plot ({\x},{-0.75*\x+3});}
    &
    \begin{minipage}[c]{0.46\linewidth}
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*), leftmargin=1.7em, itemsep=3pt]
      \item $y=-\tfrac43 x+3$
      \item $y=-\tfrac34 x+3$ \ans{$\leftarrow$}
      \item $y=\tfrac34 x+3$
      \item $y=-\tfrac34 x-3$
    \end{enumerate}
    \end{minipage}
    \\
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{teachernote}
Item 1: associativity re-\emph{groups}; commutativity re-\emph{orders}. Nothing moved past anything
here, so (A) is the distractor that separates the two. Item 2: subtract exponents;
$n^{-2-1}=n^{-3}$, then move it down. (B) subtracts as $-2-1=-1$, (C) drops the negative on the way
down, (D) subtracts the coefficients instead of dividing. Item 3: dividing by $-3$ \textbf{flips}
the inequality --- (A) is the missed flip, (C)/(D) lose the sign of the endpoint. Item 4:
$b^2-4ac=4-48=-44<0$; a student who tries to factor has missed why the discriminant exists. Item 5:
one input, one output --- $x=2$ is paired with both $-4$ and $1$. (A) repeats an \emph{output},
which is allowed, and is the distractor worth sorting for. Item 6: from $(0,3)$ the line goes down
$3$, right $4$, hitting $(4,0)$. (A) is rise and run swapped, (C) misreads the direction, (D) the
sign of the intercept.
\end{teachernote}

\parthead{Part C --- Short Answer \& Computation (40 pts)}
Show your work.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=10pt]
  \item \textbf{Numbers and justification.}
        \\[4pt]
        (a) Label each number \textbf{R} (rational) or \textbf{I} (irrational).
        \\[3pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}$\sqrt{16}$ \ans{R} \quad $\sqrt{5}$ \ans{I} \quad
        $\tfrac73$ \ans{R} \quad $0.75$ \ans{R} \quad $\sqrt[3]{10}$ \ans{I}
        \\[4pt]
        (b) Name the property that justifies each step.
        \\[3pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}$4(x-2)=4x-8$ \ans{distributive property}
        \\[3pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}$3x+7-7=19-7$ \ans{subtraction property of equality}
        \\[3pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}$5\cdot x=x\cdot 5$ \ans{commutative property of multiplication}
        \\[4pt]
        (c) ``The set of whole numbers is closed under subtraction.'' Disprove it.
        \\[2pt] \ans{One counterexample is enough: $3-8=-5$, and $-5$ is not a whole number. Listing cases that \emph{do} work ($8-3=5$) proves nothing --- that is the asymmetry from Lesson 1.0.}

  \item \textbf{Translate and evaluate.}
        \\[4pt]
        (a) Write an expression for ``three more than five times a number $m$.'' \ans{$5m+3$}
        \\[4pt]
        (b) Evaluate $\dfrac{|p-q|}{4}+\sqrt{r}$ \; for $p=2$, $q=-10$, $r=9$.
        \ans{$p-q=2-(-10)=12$, so $|12|=12$ and $\dfrac{12}{4}=3$; $\sqrt{9}=3$. Total: $3+3=6$. Subtract \emph{first} --- the bars are grouping symbols, and $2-10=-8$ is the sign error to watch for.}
        \\
        (c) Evaluate $\sqrt[3]{x}+3y$ \; for $x=-8$, $y=\tfrac13$.
        \ans{$\sqrt[3]{-8}=-2$ (an \emph{odd} index accepts a negative radicand), and $3\cdot\tfrac13=1$, so $-2+1=-1$.}

  \item \textbf{Exponents and polynomial operations.}
        \\[6pt]
        (a) $(4a^3b^2)(-3a^4b^5)$ \hspace{1.8cm} (b) $\dfrac{15m^4n^3}{5m^4n^7}$ \; (no negative exponents)
        \\[4pt]
        \ans{(a) Multiply coefficients, \emph{add} exponents: $-12a^{7}b^{7}$.\\[3pt]
        (b) Divide coefficients, \emph{subtract} exponents: $3m^{0}n^{-4}=\dfrac{3}{n^{4}}$. The $m$'s cancel completely --- $m^{0}=1$, not $0$.}
        \\
        (c) $(3x-2)(4x+5)$ \hspace{2.2cm} (d) $(5x^2+2x-3)-(2x^2-4x+1)$
        \\[4pt]
        \ans{(c) $12x^2+15x-8x-10=12x^2+7x-10$.\\[3pt]
        (d) The minus sign is a factor of $-1$ --- it flips \textbf{every} sign in the second polynomial: $5x^2+2x-3-2x^2+4x-1=3x^2+6x-4$.}

  \item \textbf{Factor completely.} GCF first, every time.
        \\[6pt]
        (a) $2x^2-50$ \hspace{2.7cm} (b) $x^2-x-12$
        \\[4pt]
        \ans{(a) GCF $2$ first: $2(x^2-25)=2(x-5)(x+5)$. Stopping at $2(x^2-25)$ is unfinished --- the GCF \emph{uncovered} the difference of squares.\\[3pt]
        (b) Product $-12$, sum $-1$: $-4$ and $+3$, so $(x-4)(x+3)$.}
        \\
        (c) $3x^2+10x+8$ \hspace{2.1cm} (d) $5x^3-15x^2+10x$
        \\[4pt]
        \ans{(c) $a\cdot c=24$; $4$ and $6$ add to $10$: $3x^2+4x+6x+8=x(3x+4)+2(3x+4)=(3x+4)(x+2)$.\\[3pt]
        (d) GCF $5x$: $5x(x^2-3x+2)=5x(x-1)(x-2)$. Dropping the $x$ from the GCF loses a factor --- and later, a solution.}

  \item \textbf{Radicals and rational exponents.}
        \\[4pt]
        (a) $\sqrt{72}=$ \ans{$6\sqrt{2}$} \hspace{1.4cm}
        (b) $\sqrt[3]{54}=$ \ans{$3\sqrt[3]{2}$}
        \\[4pt]
        (c) Write $\sqrt{y^5}$ with a rational exponent. \ans{$y^{5/2}$}
        \\[4pt]
        (d) Write $27^{1/3}$ as a radical, then evaluate it. \ans{$\sqrt[3]{27}=3$}
        \\[4pt]
        (e) $\sqrt{8}+\sqrt{18}=$
        \ans{Simplify first, decide second: $2\sqrt{2}+3\sqrt{2}=5\sqrt{2}$. Before simplifying these look like unlike radicals; $\sqrt{26}$ is the error to watch for.}

  \item \textbf{Linear equations and inequalities.}
        \\[4pt]
        (a) Solve $2(3x-5)=4x+6$.
        \ans{Distribute: $6x-10=4x+6$. Subtract $4x$: $2x-10=6$. Add $10$: $2x=16$. Divide by $2$: $x=8$. Check: $2(24-5)=38$ and $4(8)+6=38$ \checkmark}
        \\
        (b) Classify each solution set as \textbf{one solution}, \textbf{no solution}, or
        \textbf{infinitely many}. You should not need to finish solving.
        \\[3pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}$4x-9=4x+2$ \ans{no solution} \hspace{0.8cm}
        $3(x-2)=3x-6$ \ans{infinitely many}
        \\[4pt]
        (c) Solve $P=2\ell+2w$ for $w$. \ans{$w=\dfrac{P-2\ell}{2}$}
        \\[4pt]
        (d) Solve $-3x+2\le 11$, then graph the solution on the number line.
        \\[2pt] \hspace*{1.2em}$x$ \ans{$\ge -3$}
        \begin{center}\numline{%
          \draw[keyred, very thick] (-3,0) -- (6.4,0);
          \fill[keyred] (-3,0) circle (0.22);}
        \end{center}

  \item \textbf{Quadratic equations.}
        \\[6pt]
        (a) Solve by factoring: $x^2+5x-14=0$
        \ans{$(x+7)(x-2)=0$, so by the zero product property $x=-7$ or $x=2$. The equation must be set equal to $0$ \emph{before} factoring --- that is what makes the property apply --- and the roots are the sign-flips of the numbers in the factors.}
        \\
        (b) Solve by square roots: $(x-2)^2=36$
        \ans{Take the square root of both sides and write the $\pm$ yourself: $x-2=\pm 6$, so $x=8$ or $x=-4$. Dropping the $\pm$ loses half the answer.}
        \\
        (c) Solve with the quadratic formula: $x^2-6x+2=0$ \; (exact answer)
        \ans{$a=1$, $b=-6$, $c=2$: $x=\dfrac{6\pm\sqrt{36-8}}{2}=\dfrac{6\pm\sqrt{28}}{2}=\dfrac{6\pm 2\sqrt{7}}{2}=3\pm\sqrt{7}$. Simplify the radical, then divide \emph{every} term by $2$.}
        \\
        (d) \emph{Without solving}, use the discriminant to say how many real solutions
        $2x^2-5x-3=0$ has. \ans{two real solutions}
        \\[2pt] \hspace*{1.2em}Discriminant: \ans{$25+24=49>0$}

  \item \textbf{Read the graph.} The linear function $g$ is graphed below; each gridline is one unit.
        \begin{center}
        \gridplot{\draw[forest, very thick, domain=-2.6:5, smooth, variable=\x] plot ({\x},{1.5*\x-3});}
        \end{center}
        (a) $g(4)=$ \ans{$3$} \hspace{1.2cm}
        (b) Solve $g(x)=0$: \; $x=$ \ans{$2$}
        \\[4pt]
        (c) That number in (b) is called the \ans{zero} of $g$, and on the graph it is the
        point \ans{$(2,0)$}.
        \\[4pt]
        (d) Slope: \ans{$\tfrac32$}; \quad $y$-intercept: \ans{$(0,-3)$}; \quad
        equation of $g$: \ans{$y=\tfrac32 x-3$}
        \\[4pt]
        (e) Is $g$ increasing or decreasing? \ans{increasing} \quad Domain: \ans{all real numbers}
        \\[4pt]
        (f) Write the equation of the line through $(0,1)$ \textbf{parallel} to $g$
        \ans{$y=\tfrac32 x+1$}, and the line through $(0,-3)$ \textbf{perpendicular} to $g$
        \ans{$y=-\tfrac23 x-3$}.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{teachernote}
Item 1(c) is the Lesson 1.0 idea being assessed: a counterexample is a \emph{single} number, and a
list of successes earns nothing. Item 2(b): watch for $|2|-|{-10}|$ --- the bars group the whole
subtraction --- and for $2-10$. Item 3(d) is the most-missed on this test; a student who writes
$3x^2-2x-2$ has distributed the minus to only the first term. Item 4: ``completely'' is the graded
word --- (a) without the second factoring step and (d) without the $x$ in the GCF are both half
answers. Item 6(b) should be answered by \emph{looking}, not solving; if a student solved and got
$-9=2$, give full credit but note the shortcut. Item 6(d): the flip and the \emph{closed} circle are
separate points --- $\le$ became $\ge$, and the endpoint $-3$ is included. Item 7(a): $x=7$ and
$x=-2$ is the sign-flip error --- the roots are the opposites of the numbers in the factors. Item
7(b): $x=8$ only (no $\pm$) is the classic half-answer. Item 8(c) is the unit's punchline ---
\emph{zero}, \emph{solution of $g(x)=0$}, and \emph{$x$-intercept} are three names for one thing;
insist the point be an ordered pair. Item 8(f): the perpendicular slope is the negative
\emph{reciprocal}, so $-\tfrac23$, not $-\tfrac32$.
\end{teachernote}

\parthead{Part D --- Extended Response (12 pts)}
Explain your reasoning in full sentences.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=16pt]
  \item \textbf{Three families, six questions.} Each table below is generated by a linear, a
        quadratic, or an exponential rule.
        \begin{center}
        \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
        \begin{tabular}{@{}c@{\hspace{1.1cm}}c@{\hspace{1.1cm}}c@{}}
        \begin{tabular}{@{}c|c@{}}
          $x$ & $A(x)$ \\ \hline
          $0$ & $7$ \\ $1$ & $11$ \\ $2$ & $15$ \\ $3$ & $19$ \\
        \end{tabular}
        &
        \begin{tabular}{@{}c|c@{}}
          $x$ & $B(x)$ \\ \hline
          $0$ & $2$ \\ $1$ & $6$ \\ $2$ & $18$ \\ $3$ & $54$ \\
        \end{tabular}
        &
        \begin{tabular}{@{}c|c@{}}
          $x$ & $C(x)$ \\ \hline
          $0$ & $3$ \\ $1$ & $4$ \\ $2$ & $7$ \\ $3$ & $12$ \\
        \end{tabular}
        \\
        \end{tabular}
        \end{center}
        \textbf{(a)} Name the family of each table and give the numerical evidence --- constant
        difference, constant \emph{second} difference, or constant ratio.
        \textbf{(b)} Write a rule for each function.
        \textbf{(c)} Which function is largest at $x=1$? At $x=4$? Show the values.
        \textbf{(d)} A classmate says ``$B$ is just the biggest one.'' Use your answers to (c) to
        explain why ``which is bigger'' is not a fair question until someone says \emph{where}.
        \textbf{(e)} If $B$ modeled a population of bacteria in hours, what domain would make sense,
        and why is that narrower than the domain of the rule by itself?
        \ans{(a) $A$ is \textbf{linear}: the first differences are $4,4,4$ --- a constant difference. $B$ is \textbf{exponential}: the ratios are $6/2=18/6=54/18=3$ --- a constant ratio. $C$ is \textbf{quadratic}: the first differences $1,3,5$ are not constant, but their differences are $2,2$ --- a constant \emph{second} difference.\\[3pt]
        (b) $A(x)=4x+7$ (slope $4$, starts at $7$); $B(x)=2\cdot 3^{x}$ (starting value $2$, ratio $3$); $C(x)=x^{2}+3$.\\[3pt]
        (c) At $x=1$: $A(1)=11$, $B(1)=6$, $C(1)=4$, so $A$ is largest. At $x=4$: $A(4)=23$, $B(4)=2\cdot 81=162$, $C(4)=19$, so $B$ is largest --- by a lot.\\[3pt]
        (d) The three functions trade places. At $x=1$ the linear function is on top and the exponential is in the middle; by $x=4$ the exponential has passed both and is still pulling away. A constant ratio eventually beats a constant difference and even a constant second difference, but ``eventually'' is the whole point --- before the crossing, $B$ is not the biggest at all. So ``which is bigger'' has no answer until the input is named.\\[3pt]
        (e) Hours cannot be negative, so the domain in context is $x\ge 0$ --- and if the count is only taken on the hour, the whole numbers $0,1,2,3,\dots$ The rule $2\cdot 3^{x}$ by itself accepts every real number, including negatives. The context, not the algebra, is what shrinks the domain.}

  \item \textbf{Modeling and solution sets.} Two shops quote a price for printing team shirts.
        \\[3pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}\textbf{Inkline:} a one-time \$60 setup fee, then \$8 per shirt.
        \\[2pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}\textbf{Threadworks:} no setup fee, \$14 per shirt.
        \\[4pt]
        \textbf{(a)} Write a function for the total cost at each shop for $s$ shirts.
        \textbf{(b)} Find the number of shirts at which the two costs are equal. Show every step,
        and name the property of equality that justifies each one.
        \textbf{(c)} Verify your answer by substituting it into \emph{both} functions.
        \textbf{(d)} Which shop is cheaper for $5$ shirts? For $25$ shirts? Give the costs.
        \textbf{(e)} Suppose Threadworks changed its quote to a \$60 setup fee plus \$8 per shirt.
        Setting the two costs equal would give $8s+60=8s+60$. What is the solution set, what is that
        kind of equation called, and what does it mean about the two shops?
        \textbf{(f)} What domain makes sense for $s$ in this situation, and why?
        \ans{(a) $I(s)=8s+60$ and $T(s)=14s$. The setup fee is the $y$-intercept (a one-time cost); the per-shirt price is the slope.\\[3pt]
        (b) $8s+60=14s$. Subtract $8s$ from both sides (\textbf{subtraction property of equality}): $60=6s$. Divide both sides by $6$ (\textbf{division property of equality}): $s=10$ shirts.\\[3pt]
        (c) $I(10)=8(10)+60=140$ and $T(10)=14(10)=140$. Both give \$140, so $s=10$ checks. \checkmark\\[3pt]
        (d) At $5$ shirts: $I(5)=100$, $T(5)=70$ --- \textbf{Threadworks} is cheaper, because the \$60 setup fee has not paid for itself yet. At $25$ shirts: $I(25)=260$, $T(25)=350$ --- \textbf{Inkline} is cheaper, by \$90. The break-even point found in (b) is exactly where the answer switches.\\[3pt]
        (e) Every $s$ makes it true, so the solution set is \textbf{all real numbers} and the equation is an \textbf{identity} --- the two sides are the same expression written twice. In context it means the shops would charge identically for any order, so cost could never decide between them.\\[3pt]
        (f) $s$ counts shirts, so it must be a whole number $0,1,2,3,\dots$ --- there is no such thing as $7.4$ shirts or $-3$ shirts. The functions themselves would happily accept any real number; the situation is what restricts the domain.}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Scoring Part D (6 pts each).} D1: 1 pt (a) all three families named \emph{with} the numerical
evidence --- a family named with no evidence earns half, since the evidence is the skill; 1 pt (b)
all three rules ($2\cdot 3^{x}$, not $3^{x}+2$ --- the starting value multiplies); 1 pt (c) both
comparisons with values shown; 1 pt (d) the trade-places argument; 1 pt (e) $x\ge 0$ (or whole
numbers); 1 pt (e) the contrast between the rule's domain and the context's. Do not accept
``exponential always wins'' in (d) --- at $x=1$ it is in the middle, and that is the item. D2: 1 pt
(a) both functions; 1 pt (b) $s=10$; 1 pt (b) \emph{both} properties named by name --- this is the
A.EI.1 justification clause and the reason Lesson 1.0 exists; 1 pt (c) the substitution into both;
1 pt (d) both comparisons with costs; 1 pt (e) identity \emph{and} all real numbers \emph{and} the
interpretation. Award (f) inside the (e) point if (e) is otherwise complete. A student who answers
(e) with ``$s=0$'' has confused an identity with a solved equation --- reteach against the
one/none/infinitely-many table from Lesson 1.2.
\end{teachernote}
