Write the letter of the best description next to each term.

\vspace{4pt}
\noindent
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.34\textwidth}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em, itemsep=7pt, label=\arabic*.]
  \item Rational number \ans{D}
  \item Associative property \ans{F}
  \item Factor completely \ans{G}
  \item Identity \ans{A}
  \item Zero product property \ans{E}
  \item Radicand \ans{B}
  \item Zero of a function \ans{H}
  \item Parallel lines \ans{C}
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.63\textwidth}
\small
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item[(A)] An equation true for every real number, so its solution set is all real numbers.
  \item[(B)] The expression written underneath the radical symbol.
  \item[(C)] Two distinct lines with equal slopes, so they never meet.
  \item[(D)] A real number that can be written as a ratio of two integers; its decimal terminates or repeats.
  \item[(E)] If a product equals $0$, then at least one factor equals $0$ --- the rule that turns a factored quadratic into solutions.
  \item[(F)] The property that lets you re-group terms without moving any of them past another.
  \item[(G)] To rewrite an expression as a product --- GCF first --- until no factor breaks down further over the integers.
  \item[(H)] An input whose output is $0$; also a solution of $f(x)=0$ and an $x$-intercept of the graph.
\end{description}
\end{minipage}

\parthead{Part C --- Short Answer \& Computation (40 pts)}
Show your work.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=10pt]
  \item \textbf{Numbers and justification.}
        \\[4pt]
        (a) Label each number \textbf{R} (rational) or \textbf{I} (irrational).
        \\[3pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}$\sqrt{81}$ \ans{R} \quad $\sqrt{15}$ \ans{I} \quad
        $-\tfrac49$ \ans{R} \quad $0.\overline{6}$ \ans{R} \quad $\sqrt[3]{7}$ \ans{I}
        \\[4pt]
        (b) Name the property that justifies each step.
        \\[3pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}$8(x+3)=8x+24$ \ans{distributive property}
        \\[3pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}$(2+x)+7=2+(x+7)$ \ans{associative property of addition}
        \\[3pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}$4x\cdot 1=4x$ \ans{multiplicative identity}
        \\[4pt]
        (c) ``The set of natural numbers is closed under division.'' Disprove it.
        \\[2pt]
        \ansline{One counterexample is enough: $5\div 2=2.5$, not a natural number.}
        \ansline{Cases that \emph{do} work ($6\div 3=2$) prove nothing --- that is the asymmetry.}

  \item \textbf{Translate and evaluate.}
        \\[4pt]
        (a) Write an expression for ``four less than the square of a number $n$.'' \ans{$n^2-4$}
        \\[4pt]
        (b) Evaluate $\dfrac{|a-b|}{4}+\sqrt{c}$ \; for $a=-6$, $b=6$, $c=36$.
        \begin{work}
          \frac{|a-b|}{4}+\sqrt{c} &= \frac{|-6-6|}{4}+\sqrt{36} \\
                                   &= \frac{|-12|}{4}+6 \\
                                   &= 3+6 \\
                                   &= 9
        \end{work}
        (c) Evaluate $\sqrt[3]{x}-6y$ \; for $x=-8$, $y=\tfrac13$.
        \begin{work}
          \sqrt[3]{x}-6y &= \sqrt[3]{-8}-6\left(\tfrac13\right) \\
                         &= -2-2 \\
                         &= -4
        \end{work}

  \item \textbf{Exponents and polynomial operations.}
        \\[4pt]
        (a) $(5m^4n)(-4m^2n^6)$
        \begin{work}
          (5m^4n)(-4m^2n^6) &= (5)(-4)\,m^{4+2}n^{1+6} \\
                            &= -20m^{6}n^{7}
        \end{work}
        (b) $\dfrac{42u^4v^2}{7u^4v^6}$ \; (no negative exponents)
        \begin{work}
          \frac{42u^4v^2}{7u^4v^6} &= 6u^{4-4}v^{2-6} \\
                                   &= 6u^{0}v^{-4} \\
                                   &= \frac{6}{v^{4}}
        \end{work}
        (c) $(4x-3)(2x+5)$
        \begin{work}
          (4x-3)(2x+5) &= 8x^2+20x-6x-15 \\
                       &= 8x^2+14x-15
        \end{work}
        (d) $(6x^2-2x+3)-(x^2+5x-4)$
        \begin{work}
          (6x^2-2x+3)-(x^2+5x-4) &= 6x^2-2x+3-x^2-5x+4 \\
                                 &= 5x^2-7x+7
        \end{work}

  \item \textbf{Factor completely.} GCF first, every time.
        \\[4pt]
        (a) $2x^2-50$
        \begin{work}
          2x^2-50 &= 2(x^2-25) \\
                  &= 2(x-5)(x+5)
        \end{work}
        (b) $x^2-4x-21$
        \begin{work}
          x^2-4x-21 &= (x-7)(x+3)
        \end{work}
        (c) $3x^2+11x+6$
        \begin{work}
          3x^2+11x+6 &= 3x^2+9x+2x+6 \\
                     &= 3x(x+3)+2(x+3) \\
                     &= (3x+2)(x+3)
        \end{work}
        (d) $5x^3-25x^2+30x$
        \begin{work}
          5x^3-25x^2+30x &= 5x(x^2-5x+6) \\
                         &= 5x(x-2)(x-3)
        \end{work}

  \item \textbf{Radicals and rational exponents.}
        \\[4pt]
        (a) $\sqrt{98}=$ \ans{$7\sqrt{2}$} \hspace{1.2cm}
        (b) $\sqrt[3]{24}=$ \ans{$2\sqrt[3]{3}$}
        \\[4pt]
        (c) Write $\sqrt{x^5}$ with a rational exponent. \ans{$x^{5/2}$}
        \\[4pt]
        (d) Write $27^{2/3}$ as a radical, then evaluate it. \ans{$\left(\sqrt[3]{27}\right)^2=3^2=9$}
        \\[4pt]
        (e) $\sqrt{8}+\sqrt{32}=$
        \begin{work}
          \sqrt{8}+\sqrt{32} &= 2\sqrt{2}+4\sqrt{2} \\
                             &= 6\sqrt{2}
        \end{work}

  \item \textbf{Linear equations and inequalities.}
        \\[4pt]
        (a) Solve $5(2x-3)=6x+9$.
        \begin{work}
          5(2x-3) &= 6x+9 \\
           10x-15 &= 6x+9 \\
            4x-15 &= 9 \\
               4x &= 24 \\
                x &= 6
        \end{work}
        (b) Classify each solution set as \textbf{one solution}, \textbf{no solution}, or
        \textbf{infinitely many}. You should not need to finish solving.
        \\[3pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}$2x-5=2x+1$ \ans{no solution} \hspace{0.8cm}
        $3(x+4)=3x+12$ \ans{infinitely many}
        \\[4pt]
        (c) Solve $V=\tfrac13 Bh$ for $h$.
        \begin{work}
             V &= \tfrac13 Bh \\
            3V &= Bh \\
          \frac{3V}{B} &= h
        \end{work}
        (d) Solve $-4x-3<9$, then graph the solution on the number line.
        \begin{work}
          -4x-3 &< 9 \\
            -4x &< 12 \\
              x &> -3
        \end{work}
        \begin{center}\numline{%
          \draw[keyred, very thick] (-3,0) -- (6.4,0);
          \draw[keyred, very thick, fill=white] (-3,0) circle (0.22);}
        \end{center}

  \item \textbf{Quadratic equations.}
        \\[4pt]
        (a) Solve by factoring: $x^2+2x-8=0$
        \begin{work}
            x^2+2x-8 &= 0 \\
          (x+4)(x-2) &= 0 \\
            x=-4 \quad&\text{or}\quad x=2
        \end{work}
        (b) Solve by square roots: $(x+2)^2=36$
        \begin{work}
          (x+2)^2 &= 36 \\
              x+2 &= \pm 6 \\
              x=4 \quad&\text{or}\quad x=-8
        \end{work}
        (c) Solve with the quadratic formula: $x^2+6x-3=0$ \; (exact answer)
        \begin{work}
          x &= \frac{-6\pm\sqrt{6^2-4(1)(-3)}}{2(1)} \\
            &= \frac{-6\pm\sqrt{36+12}}{2} \\
            &= \frac{-6\pm\sqrt{48}}{2} \\
            &= \frac{-6\pm 4\sqrt{3}}{2} \\
            &= -3\pm 2\sqrt{3}
        \end{work}
        (d) \emph{Without solving}, use the discriminant to say how many real solutions
        $x^2-10x+25=0$ has.
        \\[2pt] \hspace*{1.2em}Discriminant: \ans{$100-100=0$} \quad
        Number of real solutions: \ans{one (repeated)}

  \item \textbf{Read the graph.} The linear function $g$ is graphed below; each gridline is one unit.
        \begin{center}
        \gridplot{\draw[forest, very thick, domain=-2.4:3.4, smooth, variable=\x] plot ({\x},{-2*\x+2});}
        \end{center}
        (a) $g(-2)=$ \ans{$6$} \hspace{1.2cm}
        (b) Solve $g(x)=0$: \; $x=$ \ans{$1$}
        \\[4pt]
        (c) That number in (b) is called the \ans{zero} of $g$, and on the graph it is the
        point \ans{$(1,0)$}.
        \\[4pt]
        (d) Slope: \ans{$-2$}; \quad $y$-intercept: \ans{$(0,2)$}; \quad
        equation of $g$: \ans{$y=-2x+2$}
        \\[4pt]
        (e) Is $g$ increasing or decreasing? \ans{decreasing} \quad Domain: \ans{all reals}
        \\[4pt]
        (f) Write the equation of the line through $(0,-4)$ \textbf{parallel} to $g$
        \ans{$y=-2x-4$}, and the line through $(0,3)$ \textbf{perpendicular} to $g$
        \ans{$y=\tfrac12 x+3$}.
\end{enumerate}

\parthead{Part D --- Extended Response (12 pts)}
Explain your reasoning in full sentences.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=14pt]
  \item \textbf{Three families, five questions.} Each table below is generated by a linear, a
        quadratic, or an exponential rule.
        \begin{center}
        \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
        \begin{tabular}{@{}c@{\hspace{1.1cm}}c@{\hspace{1.1cm}}c@{}}
        \begin{tabular}{@{}c|c@{}}
          $x$ & $A(x)$ \\ \hline
          $0$ & $2$ \\ $1$ & $7$ \\ $2$ & $12$ \\ $3$ & $17$ \\
        \end{tabular}
        &
        \begin{tabular}{@{}c|c@{}}
          $x$ & $B(x)$ \\ \hline
          $0$ & $1$ \\ $1$ & $3$ \\ $2$ & $9$ \\ $3$ & $27$ \\
        \end{tabular}
        &
        \begin{tabular}{@{}c|c@{}}
          $x$ & $C(x)$ \\ \hline
          $0$ & $4$ \\ $1$ & $5$ \\ $2$ & $8$ \\ $3$ & $13$ \\
        \end{tabular}
        \\
        \end{tabular}
        \end{center}
        \textbf{(a)} Name the family of each table and give the numerical evidence --- constant
        difference, constant \emph{second} difference, or constant ratio.
        \\[2pt]
        \ansline{$A$ is \textbf{linear} --- first differences $5,5,5$, a constant difference.}
        \ansline{$B$ is \textbf{exponential} --- ratios $3/1=9/3=27/9=3$, a constant ratio.}
        \ansline{$C$ is \textbf{quadratic} --- differences $1,3,5$ are not constant, but \emph{their} differences are $2,2$.}
        \textbf{(b)} Write a rule for each function.
        \\[4pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}$A(x)=$ \ans{$5x+2$} \quad $B(x)=$ \ans{$3^{x}$} \quad $C(x)=$ \ans{$x^{2}+4$}
        \\[6pt]
        \textbf{(c)} Which function is largest at $x=1$? At $x=4$? Show the values.
        \begin{work}
          A(1)=7, \quad B(1)&=3, \quad C(1)=5 \\
          A(4)=22, \quad B(4)&=81, \quad C(4)=20
        \end{work}
        \textbf{(d)} A classmate says ``$B$ is just the biggest one.'' Use your answers to (c) to
        explain why ``which is bigger'' is not a fair question until someone says \emph{where}.
        \\[2pt]
        \ansline{At $x=1$ the \emph{linear} function is on top ($7$) and $B$ is the \emph{smallest} ($3$).}
        \ansline{By $x=4$ the exponential has passed both ($81$) and is still pulling away.}
        \ansline{A constant ratio wins \emph{eventually} --- so the question needs a \emph{where}.}
        \textbf{(e)} If $B$ modeled a population of bacteria in hours, what domain would make sense,
        and why is that narrower than the domain of the rule by itself?
        \\[2pt]
        \ansline{Hours cannot be negative, so the context domain is $x\ge 0$ --- and if the count is}
        \ansline{taken on the hour, the whole numbers $0,1,2,3,\dots$ The rule $3^{x}$ by itself}
        \ansline{accepts every real number. The context, not the algebra, is what shrinks the domain.}

  \item \textbf{Modeling and solution sets.} Two shops quote a price for printing team shirts.
        \\[3pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}\textbf{Ridgeway:} a one-time \$30 setup fee, then \$8 per shirt.
        \\[2pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}\textbf{Lakeside:} no setup fee, \$14 per shirt.
        \\[4pt]
        \textbf{(a)} Write a function for the total cost at each shop for $s$ shirts.
        \\[4pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}$R(s)=$ \ans{$8s+30$} \hspace{1.4cm} $L(s)=$ \ans{$14s$}
        \\[6pt]
        \textbf{(b)} Find the number of shirts at which the two costs are equal. Show every step.
        \begin{work}
          8s+30 &= 14s \\
             30 &= 6s \\
              5 &= s
        \end{work}
        Name the property of equality that justifies each step above.
        \\[3pt]
        \hspace*{1.2em}Step 1: \ans{subtraction property of equality} \quad Step 2: \ans{division property of equality}
        \\[6pt]
        \textbf{(c)} Verify your answer by substituting it into \emph{both} functions.
        \begin{work}
          R(5) &= 8(5)+30 = 70 \\
          L(5) &= 14(5) = 70
        \end{work}
        \textbf{(d)} Which shop is cheaper for $3$ shirts? For $20$ shirts? Give the costs.
        \begin{work}
          R(3)=54, \quad L(3)&=42 \\
          R(20)=190, \quad L(20)&=280
        \end{work}
        \textbf{(e)} Suppose Lakeside changed its quote to a \$30 setup fee plus \$8 per shirt.
        Setting the two costs equal would give $8s+30=8s+30$. What is the solution set, what is
        that kind of equation called, and what does it mean about the two shops?
        \\[2pt]
        \ansline{Every $s$ makes it true, so the solution set is \textbf{all real numbers}.}
        \ansline{That kind of equation is an \textbf{identity} --- one expression written twice.}
        \ansline{In context: the shops would charge the same for any order, so price could not decide.}
        \textbf{(f)} What domain makes sense for $s$ in this situation, and why?
        \\[2pt]
        \ansline{$s$ counts shirts, so the whole numbers $0,1,2,3,\dots$ --- no $-3$ or $7.4$ shirts.}
        \ansline{The functions accept any real number; the situation is what restricts the domain.}
\end{enumerate}

\parthead{Scoring Guide --- Teacher Only (72 pts total)}

\begin{teachernote}[Parts A and B --- 20 pts]
\textbf{Part A (8 pts, 1 each).} 1--D, 2--F, 3--G, 4--A, 5--E, 6--B, 7--H, 8--C. The pair worth
watching is 2/F against the distributive property: associativity \emph{re-groups}, commutativity
\emph{re-orders}, and the distributive property is the only one that changes the number of terms.
\\[4pt]
\textbf{Part B (12 pts, 2 each).} 1--B, 2--A, 3--B, 4--C, 5--B, 6--B. Item 2: subtract exponents,
$q^{-1-3}=q^{-4}$, then move it down --- (B) subtracts as $-1-3=-2$, (C) drops the negative on the
way down, (D) subtracts the coefficients instead of dividing. Item 3: dividing by $-5$
\textbf{flips} the inequality; (A) is the missed flip. Item 4: $b^2-4ac=4-60=-56<0$; a student who
tries to factor has missed why the discriminant exists. Item 5: one input, one output --- $x=4$ is
paired with both $-2$ and $3$. (A) repeats an \emph{output}, which is allowed, and is the
distractor worth sorting for. Item 6: from $(0,3)$ the line goes up $1$, right $2$; (A) is rise and
run swapped, (C) misreads the direction, (D) the sign of the intercept.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Part C --- 40 pts, 5 per item]
\textbf{1.} 1 pt each part of (a)--(b) grouped: R, I, R, R, I; distributive / associative of
addition / multiplicative identity; 2 pts (c). (c) is the Lesson 1.0 idea being assessed --- a
counterexample is a \emph{single} number, and a list of successes earns nothing.
\textbf{2.} 1 pt (a); 2 pts (b); 2 pts (c). In (b) watch for $|-6|-|6|$ --- the bars group the whole
subtraction. In (c) the \emph{odd} index is what accepts the negative radicand.
\textbf{3.} 1 pt each; 1 pt for the work being legible in the ruled space. (b) must land on
$u^{0}=1$, not $0$. (d) is the most-missed item on the test: the minus sign is a factor of $-1$ and
flips \textbf{every} sign --- a student who writes $5x^2+3x-1$ distributed it to the first term only.
\textbf{4.} 1 pt each, plus 1 pt for GCF-first discipline. ``Completely'' is the graded word: (a)
stopped at $2(x^2-25)$ and (d) missing the $x$ in the GCF are both half answers --- and (d) without
the $x$ loses a solution later.
\textbf{5.} 1 pt each part. (d) must show the radical \emph{and} the value.
\textbf{6.} 1 pt each of (a)--(c), 2 pts (d). (b) should be answered by \emph{looking}, not solving;
if a student solved and got $-5=1$, give full credit but name the shortcut. In (d) the flip and the
\emph{open} circle are separate points --- $<$ stays strict, so the endpoint $-3$ is excluded.
\textbf{7.} 1 pt each of (a)--(c), 2 pts (d). (a): $x=4$ and $x=-2$ is the sign-flip error --- the
roots are the opposites of the numbers in the factors. (b): $x=4$ only (no $\pm$) is the classic
half-answer. (c): simplify $\sqrt{48}=4\sqrt3$ first, then divide \emph{every} term by $2$.
\textbf{8.} 1 pt (a)--(b), 1 pt (c), 1 pt (d), 1 pt (e), 1 pt (f). (c) is the unit's punchline ---
\emph{zero}, \emph{solution of $g(x)=0$}, and \emph{$x$-intercept} are three names for one thing;
insist the point be an ordered pair. (f): the perpendicular slope is the negative
\emph{reciprocal}, so $\tfrac12$, not $-\tfrac12$ and not $2$.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Part D --- 12 pts, 6 per item]
\textbf{D1.} 1 pt (a) all three families named \emph{with} the numerical evidence --- a family named
with no evidence earns half, since the evidence is the skill; 1 pt (b) all three rules ($3^{x}$,
and $C(x)=x^2+4$ read off the constant second difference); 1 pt (c) both comparisons with values
shown; 2 pts (d) the trade-places argument; 1 pt (e) $x\ge 0$ (or the whole numbers) \emph{and} the
contrast with the rule's own domain. Do not accept ``exponential always wins'' in (d) --- at $x=1$
it is the \emph{smallest} of the three, and that is the entire item.
