\begin{notesbox}{Practice}
Assume all variables represent \textbf{positive} real numbers except where a problem says otherwise.
No calculator.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=11pt]
  \item \textbf{Translate both directions.} Fill in every empty cell.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.9}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X | X X@{}}
    \hline
    \textbf{Radical form} & \textbf{Rational exponent} & \textbf{Rational exponent} & \textbf{Radical form} \\
    \hline
    $\sqrt[3]{x^{4}}$ & \ans{$x^{4/3}$} & $m^{1/5}$   & \ans{$\sqrt[5]{m}$} \\
    $\sqrt{y^{7}}$    & \ans{$y^{7/2}$} & $p^{5/6}$   & \ans{$\sqrt[6]{p^{5}}$} \\
    $\left(\sqrt[4]{w}\right)^{5}$ & \ans{$w^{5/4}$} & $(2k)^{2/3}$ & \ans{$\sqrt[3]{(2k)^{2}}$} \\
    \hline
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}

  \item \textbf{Evaluate.} Take the root first.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.9}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X X X@{}}
    (a) \; $49^{1/2}=$ \ans{$7$} & (b) \; $8^{2/3}=$ \ans{$4$} &
    (c) \; $81^{3/4}=$ \ans{$27$} & (d) \; $32^{2/5}=$ \ans{$4$} \\
    (e) \; $100^{-1/2}=$ \ans{$\dfrac{1}{10}$} & (f) \; $64^{-2/3}=$ \ans{$\dfrac{1}{16}$} &
    (g) \; $(-125)^{1/3}=$ \ans{$-5$} & (h) \; $\left(\dfrac{16}{81}\right)^{3/4}=$ \ans{$\dfrac{8}{27}$} \\
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}
    \emph{Work for the harder ones:} (f) $\dfrac{1}{\left(\sqrt[3]{64}\right)^{2}}=\dfrac{1}{4^{2}}$. \;
    (h) $\left(\sqrt[4]{16/81}\right)^{3}=\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right)^{3}$.

  \item \textbf{Simplify with the exponent properties.} Leave every answer with a single
        \emph{positive} rational exponent.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{2.0}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
    (a) \; $x^{2/3}\cdot x^{1/6} = $ \ans{$x^{5/6}$} &
    (b) \; $\dfrac{c^{7/4}}{c^{1/2}} = $ \ans{$c^{5/4}$} \\
    (c) \; $\left(t^{4/5}\right)^{5/2} = $ \ans{$t^{2}$} &
    (d) \; $\sqrt[3]{x}\cdot\sqrt{x} = $ \ans{$x^{5/6}$} \\
    (e) \; $\dfrac{\sqrt[4]{n^{3}}}{\sqrt[8]{n}} = $ \ans{$n^{5/8}$} &
    (f) \; $\left(27x^{9}\right)^{2/3} = $ \ans{$9x^{6}$} \\
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}
    \emph{Work:} (a) $\tfrac{4}{6}+\tfrac{1}{6}=\tfrac{5}{6}$. \; (b) $\tfrac{7}{4}-\tfrac{2}{4}$. \;
    (c) $\tfrac{4}{5}\cdot\tfrac{5}{2}=2$. \; (d) $\tfrac{1}{3}+\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{5}{6}$. \;
    (e) $\tfrac{6}{8}-\tfrac{1}{8}$. \; (f) $27^{2/3}=9$ and $x^{9\cdot 2/3}=x^{6}$.

  \item \textbf{The absolute-value rule.} Here $x$ may be \emph{any} real number.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}l l l@{}}
    (a) \; $\sqrt{x^{2}}=$ \ans{$|x|$} & (b) \; $\sqrt[3]{x^{3}}=$ \ans{$x$} &
    (c) \; $\sqrt[4]{x^{4}}=$ \ans{$|x|$} \\
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    Explain the pattern: which indices need absolute value bars, and why does the other kind not?
    \ansline{\textbf{Even} indices need the bars. An even power destroys the sign, so $x^{2}$ and
    $x^{4}$ are never negative --- and the radical then returns the \emph{principal} (non-negative)
    root, which cannot give a negative $x$ back. Writing $|x|$ is how we say ``whatever the sign
    was, the answer is non-negative.'' \textbf{Odd} indices need nothing: an odd power keeps the
    sign and the odd root gives it back, so $\sqrt[3]{(-2)^{3}}=\sqrt[3]{-8}=-2$ exactly.}

  \item \textbf{Back to domains (Lesson 6.0, in exponent language).} For each function, state
        whether the domain is restricted, and give the domain. Look at the \emph{denominator}.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.8}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X X X@{}}
    \hline
    \textbf{Function} & \textbf{Denominator: even or odd?} & \textbf{Restricted?} & \textbf{Domain} \\
    \hline
    $f(x)=x^{1/2}$   & \ans{even ($2$)} & \ans{yes} & \ans{$[0,\infty)$} \\
    $g(x)=x^{1/3}$   & \ans{odd ($3$)} & \ans{no} & \ans{all reals} \\
    $h(x)=x^{2/5}$   & \ans{odd ($5$)} & \ans{no} & \ans{all reals} \\
    $j(x)=(x-3)^{1/4}$ & \ans{even ($4$)} & \ans{yes} & \ans{$[3,\infty)$} \\
    \hline
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}
    \emph{Note on $j$:} the base is $x-3$, not $x$, so the condition is $x-3\ge 0$ --- the same
    ``set the radicand $\ge 0$'' move as Lesson 6.0.

  \item \textbf{Explain.} Yesterday you learned that an \emph{even index} restricts a radical
        function's domain. Today you learned that an \emph{even denominator} does the same thing.
        Explain why these are not two rules but one. \ansline{Because the denominator \emph{is} the
        index. Writing $\sqrt[n]{a}$ as $a^{1/n}$ puts the index in the denominator, so
        $x^{1/2}$ and $\sqrt{x}$ are the same function written two ways. Saying ``even denominator''
        and saying ``even index'' are the same sentence in two notations --- and the underlying
        reason is the same in both: an even power of a real number is never negative, so an even
        root of a negative number does not exist.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{extensionbox}
\textbf{Why a bigger animal does not eat proportionally more.} Biologists model an animal's resting
metabolic rate --- roughly, the calories per day it burns just staying alive --- as a rational
power of its body mass. For a mammal of mass $w$ kilograms,
\[
  M(w) = 70\,w^{3/4} \ \text{ kilocalories per day.}
\]
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$w$ (kg)        & $1$ & $16$ & $81$ & $256$ \\ \hline
$M(w)$ (kcal/day) & $70$ & \ans{$560$} & \ans{$1890$} & \ans{$4480$} \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Complete the table without a calculator. (Each mass was chosen to be a perfect fourth power.)
        \ansline{$16^{3/4}=\left(\sqrt[4]{16}\right)^{3}=2^{3}=8$, so $M=70\cdot 8=560$; likewise
        $81^{3/4}=3^{3}=27\Rightarrow 1890$, and $256^{3/4}=4^{3}=64\Rightarrow 4480$.}
  \item Write $M(w)$ in radical form. Which of the two equivalent radical forms is easier to compute
        with, and why? \ansline{$M(w)=70\left(\sqrt[4]{w}\right)^{3}$, or equivalently
        $70\sqrt[4]{w^{3}}$. The first is easier: rooting first keeps the numbers small
        ($\sqrt[4]{16}=2$), while the second asks for $\sqrt[4]{4096}$.}
  \item From $w=16$ to $w=256$ the mass is multiplied by $16$. By what factor is the metabolic rate
        multiplied? Show the arithmetic, then say in one sentence what the exponent $\frac{3}{4}$
        --- a number \emph{less} than $1$ --- is doing to the growth.
        \ansline{$4480\div 560=8$, so the rate multiplies by only $8$ while the mass multiplies by
        $16$. (Consistently: $16^{3/4}=8$.) An exponent below $1$ \emph{damps} the growth --- the
        output grows, but slower than the input, so a $16\times$ heavier mammal burns only about
        $8\times$ the calories.}
  \item Solve $M=70w^{3/4}$ for $w$ in terms of $M$. Which power did you raise both sides to, and
        why that one? \ansline{Divide first: $\dfrac{M}{70}=w^{3/4}$. Then raise both sides to
        $\dfrac{4}{3}$ --- the \emph{reciprocal} --- because
        $\left(w^{3/4}\right)^{4/3}=w^{1}=w$. So $w=\left(\dfrac{M}{70}\right)^{4/3}$.}
  \item Your answer to (d) is the \textbf{inverse} of $M$ --- the same relationship run backwards,
        exactly as in Lesson 6.0. What does it let a biologist do that $M$ itself does not?
        \ansline{$M$ predicts calories from a known mass; the inverse \emph{estimates the mass} from
        a measured metabolic rate --- useful for an animal you can put in a respiration chamber but
        cannot easily weigh. Inputs and outputs have traded places, exactly like the swapped tables
        in Lesson 6.0.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{extensionbox}

\begin{spiralbox}
\textbf{Coming next --- Lesson 6.2: Simplifying Radicals; Adding \& Subtracting.} Today you learned
that $\sqrt[n]{a}=a^{1/n}$, which makes an exponent rule out of every radical rule. Next you will use
two of them constantly --- $\sqrt[n]{ab}=\sqrt[n]{a}\cdot\sqrt[n]{b}$ and
$\sqrt[n]{a/b}=\sqrt[n]{a}\big/\sqrt[n]{b}$ --- to pull perfect $n$th powers out from under a
radical ($\sqrt{72}=6\sqrt{2}$), and then discover that radicals add only when they are
\emph{like radicals}, in exactly the way $3x+5x$ works but $3x+5y$ does not.
\end{spiralbox}

\begin{teachernote}
Items 1--3 are the routine load and should take about fifteen minutes. \textbf{Item 4 is the one to
grade closely}: it is the only place all week where variables are \emph{not} assumed positive, and
students who have been dropping the bars all period will drop them here too. If more than a third of
the class misses it, open 6.2 by asking for $\sqrt{x^{2}}$ at $x=-3$ before touching the product
property. \textbf{Items 5--6 are the conceptual spine} --- 6 in particular asks students to state
that the two domain rules are one rule in two notations, which is the whole justification for
teaching this lesson between 6.0 and 6.2. Accept any answer that connects ``denominator'' to
``index''; do not require the full evenness argument.
The \textbf{Extension} is Kleiber's law, and its shape is deliberately the same as the Activity's
Kepler problem: a rational-exponent model, a table of perfect powers, a growth-rate interpretation,
and an inverse found by raising both sides to the reciprocal power. Part (c) is the payoff --- an
exponent between $0$ and $1$ means the output grows more slowly than the input, which is exactly the
flattening shape students read off the square root graph in Lesson 6.0.
\end{teachernote}
