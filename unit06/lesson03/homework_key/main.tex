\begin{notesbox}{Practice}
Every solution below needs a visible common-base step. An answer with no rewriting shown is not a
solution.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=12pt]
  \item \textbf{Solve each equation.}
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.9}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X c@{\hspace{1.6em}} l X c@{}}
    \hline
    \textbf{(a)} $2^{x}=128$ & \ans{$2^{x}=2^{7}$} & $x=$ \ans{7} &
    \textbf{(b)} $3^{\,2x}=81$ & \ans{$3^{\,2x}=3^{4}$} & $x=$ \ans{2} \\
    \textbf{(c)} $5^{x}=\frac{1}{25}$ & \ans{$5^{x}=5^{-2}$} & $x=$ \ans{$-2$} &
    \textbf{(d)} $16^{x}=64$ & \ans{$2^{\,4x}=2^{6}$} & $x=$ \ans{$\frac32$} \\
    \textbf{(e)} $\left(\frac12\right)^{x}=32$ & \ans{$2^{-x}=2^{5}$} & $x=$ \ans{$-5$} &
    \textbf{(f)} $27^{\,x-1}=9$ & \ans{$3^{\,3x-3}=3^{2}$} & $x=$ \ans{$\frac53$} \\
    \textbf{(g)} $4^{\,x+2}=8^{x}$ & \ans{$2^{\,2x+4}=2^{\,3x}$} & $x=$ \ans{4} &
    \textbf{(h)} $6^{\,3x}=\sqrt{6}$ & \ans{$6^{\,3x}=6^{1/2}$} & $x=$ \ans{$\frac16$} \\
    \hline
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}
    \emph{Work space --- show the rewriting for at least four of the eight.}
    \vspace{1.05in}

    Three of your eight answers are not whole numbers. List them, and say in one sentence why that is
    not a sign of a mistake. \ans{(d) $\frac32$, (f) $\frac53$, (h) $\frac16$ --- the exponent is a position on the $x$-axis, and nothing requires a curve to cross a given height at a whole number.}

  \item \textbf{Read it, then prove it.} The curve is $y=2^{x}$.
    \begin{center}
    \begin{minipage}[c]{0.40\linewidth}
    \centering
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.38]
      \lgrid{-3}{4}{-5}{9}
      \begin{scope}
        \clip (-3,-5) rectangle (4,9);
        \expgraph{1}{0.693147}{0}{0}{-3}{4}
      \end{scope}
      \solline{-3}{4}{8}
      \solline{-3}{4}{2}
      \draw[redacc, dashed, very thick] (-3,-4)--(4,-4);
      \node[goldacc, font=\scriptsize, left] at (-1.2,8.6) {$y=8$};
      \node[goldacc, font=\scriptsize, left] at (-1.2,2.6) {$y=2$};
      \node[redacc, font=\scriptsize, left] at (-1.2,-4.6) {$y=-4$};
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{minipage}
    \begin{minipage}[c]{0.56\linewidth}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.7}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X c@{}}
    \hline
    \textbf{Equation} & \textbf{Common base} & \textbf{$x=$} \\
    \hline
    $2^{x}=8$ & \ans{$2^{x}=2^{3}$} & \ans{3} \\
    $2^{x}=2$ & \ans{$2^{x}=2^{1}$} & \ans{1} \\
    $2^{x}=-4$ & \ans{impossible} & \ans{none} \\
    \hline
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{minipage}
    \end{center}
    \vspace{2pt}
    \textbf{(a)} What horizontal line would you have to draw to make the solution $x=0$?
    \ans{$y=1$} \\[4pt]
    \textbf{(b)} What horizontal line would give the solution $x=-1$? \ans{$y=\frac12$} \\[4pt]
    \textbf{(c)} Explain why \emph{no} horizontal line drawn below the $x$-axis will ever produce a
    solution. \ans{The range of $y=2^{x}$ is $(0,\infty)$, and $y=0$ is a horizontal asymptote --- the curve stays above it forever, so nothing below the $x$-axis is ever an output.}

  \item \textbf{Sort them.} Decide whether each equation can be solved by writing both sides over a
    common base. Write \textbf{yes} or \textbf{no}, and solve the ones you can.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.75}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l c X c@{\hspace{1.4em}} l c X c@{}}
    \hline
    \textbf{(a)} $2^{x}=16$ & \ans{yes} & \ans{$2^{x}=2^{4}$} & \ans{4} &
    \textbf{(b)} $2^{x}=12$ & \ans{no} & \ans{---} & \ans{---} \\
    \textbf{(c)} $5^{\,x+1}=\sqrt5$ & \ans{yes} & \ans{$5^{\,x+1}=5^{1/2}$} & \ans{$-\frac12$} &
    \textbf{(d)} $7^{x}=1$ & \ans{yes} & \ans{$7^{x}=7^{0}$} & \ans{0} \\
    \textbf{(e)} $10^{x}=45$ & \ans{no} & \ans{---} & \ans{---} &
    \textbf{(f)} $\left(\frac14\right)^{x}=8$ & \ans{yes} & \ans{$2^{-2x}=2^{3}$} & \ans{$-\frac32$} \\
    \hline
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}
    You should have exactly two \textbf{no}s. For each of those two, answer this: does the equation
    still have a solution, and between which two whole numbers does it sit?
    \ans{Both do. $2^{x}=12$ sits between $x=3$ ($2^{3}=8$) and $x=4$ ($2^{4}=16$); $10^{x}=45$ sits between $x=1$ ($10$) and $x=2$ ($100$).}

  \item \textbf{Error analysis.} Name the error, then give the correct solution.

    \textbf{(a)} Jamal solved $8^{x}=32$ this way: ``$\left(2^{3}\right)^{x}=2^{5}$, so
    $2^{\,3+x}=2^{5}$, so $3+x=5$ and $x=2$.''
    \ansline{A power of a power \emph{multiplies} the exponents: $\left(2^{3}\right)^{x}=2^{\,3x}$, not $2^{\,3+x}$. Jamal used the product rule instead.}
    \ansline{Correct: $2^{\,3x}=2^{5}$, so $3x=5$ and $x=\frac53$. (His answer fails the check: $8^{2}=64$, not $32$.)}

    \textbf{(b)} Elise solved $9^{\,x+2}=27$ this way: ``$3^{\,2x+2}=3^{3}$, so $2x+2=3$ and
    $x=\frac12$.''
    \ansline{Elise multiplied the $2$ by the $x$ but not by the $2$ in $x+2$: $\left(3^{2}\right)^{x+2}=3^{\,2(x+2)}=3^{\,2x+4}$.}
    \ansline{Correct: $2x+4=3$, so $x=-\frac12$. Writing the parentheses before removing them prevents this every time.}

  \item \textbf{One answer or two?} Solve both equations, then explain the difference using the
    \emph{shape} of each graph --- not the algebra.

    \vspace{3pt}
    \hspace*{1.2em} $x^{2}=64$ \; gives $x=$ \ans{$\pm8$} \hfill
    $2^{x}=64$ \; gives $x=$ \ans{6}

    \vspace{2pt}
    \ansline{The parabola has a turning point, so it climbs to the height $64$ on both sides of its vertex --- one output, two inputs.}
    \ansline{The exponential has no turning point; it only ever increases, so it reaches the height $64$ once and never returns to it.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{extensionbox}
\textbf{When matching bases tells you there is nothing to find.} Consider
\[
  4^{x}\cdot 2^{\,x+1} = 8^{\,x-1}.
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Write each side as a single power of $2$, then set the exponents equal. Something strange
        happens --- write down the equation you end up with and say what it tells you.
  \item Now rewrite each side as a \emph{multiple of} $8^{x}$. Fill in the two constants:
        the left side is \ans{2} $\cdot\,8^{x}$ and the right side is \ans{$\frac18$}
        $\cdot\,8^{x}$.
  \item In Lesson 6.2's language, both sides are vertical stretches of the same curve $y=8^{x}$.
        Explain why two such curves can never intersect. (Hint: setting them equal leads to
        $\frac{15}{8}\cdot 8^{x}=0$. Why is that impossible?)
  \item Compare this equation with $2^{x}=5$. Both defeated today's method, but for completely
        different reasons. Say what each reason is, in one sentence apiece --- and say which of the
        two equations actually has a solution.
\end{enumerate}
\ansline{(a) $4^{x}\cdot2^{\,x+1}=2^{\,2x}\cdot2^{\,x+1}=2^{\,3x+1}$ and $8^{\,x-1}=2^{\,3x-3}$, so $3x+1=3x-3$, i.e. $1=-3$.}
\ansline{That is false for every $x$, so the equation has \emph{no solution} --- the method did not fail, it answered.}
\ansline{(c) $8^{x}$ is never $0$ (its range is $(0,\infty)$), so $\frac{15}{8}\cdot8^{x}$ is never $0$ either; the left curve sits $16$ times higher than the right one at every input, and two curves that differ by a constant factor never meet.}
\ansline{(d) $4^{x}\cdot2^{\,x+1}=8^{\,x-1}$ has no solution --- the graphs never cross. $2^{x}=5$ \emph{does} have a solution, between $x=2$ and $x=3$, and only our method is missing.}
\end{extensionbox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Grade problem 1 by the rewriting column, not the answer column.} Several of these can be
guessed --- (a), (b), (g) especially --- and a guessed answer will collapse on the unit test. The
closing question about non-whole answers exists because students erase correct fractional work; if the
class listed fewer than three, they changed something.

\textbf{Problem 2(b) is the only item on the page that needs a negative exponent read off a picture},
and it is the one most likely to come back blank. $y=\frac12$ is a legal horizontal line; students who
say ``you can't'' have quietly restricted themselves to integer heights.

\textbf{Problem 4 is Devon's and Elise's errors from the Activity, one night later and with new
numbers.} That repetition is deliberate: (a) is add-versus-multiply and (b) is failure to distribute
across a binomial exponent, and they are the only two mechanical errors this lesson produces. If a
student misses both, reteach the parentheses habit --- write $3^{\,2(x+2)}$ before writing anything
else --- rather than the individual problems.

\textbf{The Extension is the most important thing in tonight's assignment and is worth class time
tomorrow.} It separates two failures that look identical on paper: an equation with \emph{no solution}
(the graphs never meet) and an equation whose solution exists but is \emph{out of reach} (the graphs
meet at a place our algebra cannot name). Lesson 6.4 ends on the second kind and Unit 7 resolves it, so
students who cannot tell them apart will decide that logarithms are magic. Part (c) is also a genuine
Lesson 6.2 callback --- two vertical stretches of one curve --- so it rewards students who kept those
notes.
\end{teachernote}
