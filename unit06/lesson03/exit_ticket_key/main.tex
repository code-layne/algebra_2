\vspace{0.08in}

No notes. On items 1 and 2, the common-base step is part of the answer --- show it.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=12pt]
  \item \textbf{Solve.} \quad $3^{\,x+1}=81$

        \vspace{4pt}
        Step 1 (one common base): \ans{$3^{\,x+1}=3^{4}$} \quad Step 2 (exponents equal):
        \ans{$x+1=4$}

        \vspace{5pt}
        \hfill $x=$ \ans{3}

  \item \textbf{Solve.} \quad $25^{x}=125$ \quad \emph{(Careful: the two sides do not start with the
        same base.)}

        \vspace{4pt}
        Step 1 (one common base): \ans{$5^{\,2x}=5^{3}$} \quad Step 2 (exponents equal):
        \ans{$2x=3$}

        \vspace{5pt}
        \hfill $x=$ \ans{$\frac32$}

  \item \textbf{Explain, don't compute.} A classmate is trying to solve $2^{x}=-8$ by rewriting $-8$
        as a power of $2$. Explain why no such rewriting exists. Use the \emph{range} of $y=2^{x}$ in
        your answer.

        \vspace{2pt}
        \ansline{The range of $y=2^{x}$ is $(0,\infty)$: every power of $2$ is positive, no matter what the exponent is.}
        \ansline{So $-8$ is not an output the function ever produces, the line $y=-8$ never meets the curve, and the equation has no solution.}

  \item \textbf{Multiple choice.} Which equation \emph{cannot} be solved by rewriting both sides with
        a common base?
        \begin{enumerate}[label=\Alph*., leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=1pt]
          \item $9^{x}=27$
          \item $5^{x}=\frac{1}{125}$
          \item $2^{x}=10$ \quad \textcolor{keyred}{\textbf{$\leftarrow$ correct}}
          \item $8^{x}=\sqrt{2}$
        \end{enumerate}

        \vspace{4pt}
        For the one you chose: does that equation still \emph{have} a solution? Circle one ---
        \textcolor{keyred}{\textbf{yes}} \; / \; \textbf{no} --- and say how you know.

        \vspace{2pt}
        \ansline{$10$ is positive, so it is in the range; the line $y=10$ crosses the curve between $x=3$ ($2^{3}=8$) and $x=4$ ($2^{4}=16$).}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Answer 4: C.} The three distractors are all solvable and each rehearses one rewrite:
\textbf{A} $3^{\,2x}=3^{3}$, $x=\frac32$; \textbf{B} $5^{x}=5^{-3}$, $x=-3$; \textbf{D}
$2^{\,3x}=2^{1/2}$, $x=\frac16$. A student who picks D has decided that a radical or a fraction ``does
not count'' as a power --- that is a Unit 5 gap, not a Lesson 6.3 gap, and it is fixed by pointing at
Warm-Up item 1.

\textbf{Item 4's follow-up is the most valuable line on the page and should be read before anything
else.} ``No'' is the wrong answer, and it is a different misconception from anything in items 1--3:
it collapses \emph{my method fails} into \emph{there is nothing to find}. Lesson 6.4 ends on exactly
this distinction and Unit 7 resolves it, so a class that answers ``no'' in numbers needs five minutes
at the start of 6.4 with the curve and the line $y=10$ drawn on the board.

\textbf{Sort the collected tickets into four piles:} \emph{got it} / \emph{rewrite} (item 2 left as
$2x=3$ unsolved, or $25$ and $125$ never converted to base $5$) / \emph{range} (item 3 answered with
algebra, or ``$-8=2^{-3}$'') / \emph{the wall} (item 4's follow-up answered ``no''). The
\emph{rewrite} pile needs practice, not re-teaching --- assign the homework's problem 1 and check
tomorrow. The \emph{range} pile is the one to re-open with the graph from Section 4 of the notes.

\textbf{Item 2's answer is a fraction on purpose.} Students who distrust $x=\frac32$ will change it to
$x=3$ or $x=2$; a wrong answer of exactly $3$ almost always means they matched $125$'s exponent
without rewriting $25$ --- Priya's error from the Activity, one period later.
\end{teachernote}
