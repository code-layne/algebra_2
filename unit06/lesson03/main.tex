\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=forestbg, colframe=forest, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students can solve an exponential equation by rewriting \emph{both}
    sides as powers of a single base and then setting the exponents equal --- and can say why that is
    allowed rather than treating it as a rule. The justification is a fact they already own: an
    exponential graph has no turning points (Lesson 6.0), so it never produces the same output twice,
    which is exactly what forces $b^{m}=b^{n}$ to mean $m=n$. Students also read the same solution off
    a graph as the crossing point of a curve and a horizontal line, and use the \textbf{range} to
    decide when an exponential equation has no solution at all. The lesson ends by separating two
    failures that look identical on paper: $2^{x}=-3$ has \emph{no} solution, while $2^{x}=5$ has one
    that today's method cannot reach.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Standards (2023 VA SOL): none --- this lesson is \textit{beyond-SOL / precalculus prep}.}
    The 2023 standards contain \emph{no} \texttt{A2.EI} strand for exponential equations (that strand
    runs absolute value $\to$ quadratic $\to$ quadratic systems $\to$ rational $\to$ radical $\to$
    polynomial and stops) and no \texttt{A2.EO} standard for exponential expressions. The lesson is
    kept as a full lesson anyway, because Unit 7 cannot run without it and because 6.4's closing wall
    is dishonest if students have never solved an exponential equation at all. Per the unit ruling,
    6.3 content is taught and assessed on the unit test but stays \textbf{off the SOL-style
    multiple-choice items} --- which is why today's exit ticket carries an ordinary multiple-choice
    item instead of the SOL-style item used in 6.0--6.2. Two standards are \emph{exercised} without
    being claimed: \textbf{A2.EO.2c} (radical $\leftrightarrow$ rational exponent, Lesson 5.1) supplies
    every rewrite in Step 1, and \textbf{A2.F.2a} (domain and range) is what rules solutions out in
    Section 4.
\end{tcolorbox}

\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Ideas \& Skills} & \textbf{Key Understandings (the ``why'')} \\[2pt]
\hline
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item Rewrite a number as a power of a given base, including negative and fractional exponents ($\frac{1}{27}=3^{-3}$, $\sqrt2=2^{1/2}$).
  \item Apply the \textbf{one-to-one property}: if $b^{m}=b^{n}$ with $b>0$, $b\neq1$, then $m=n$.
  \item Solve $b^{\,\text{expr}}=b^{\,\text{expr}}$ in three steps: one base, equal exponents, solve.
  \item Use \textbf{power of a power} correctly --- $\left(2^{3}\right)^{x}=2^{3x}$ --- and distribute across a \emph{binomial} exponent.
  \item Accept and check a non-integer solution using rational exponents.
  \item Read the solution of $b^{x}=c$ off a graph as a crossing point.
  \item Decide from the \textbf{range} when $b^{x}=c$ has no solution.
  \item Tell ``no solution'' apart from ``a solution this method cannot reach.''
\end{itemize}
&
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item \emph{You may drop the base because an exponential never uses the same output twice.} That is Lesson 6.0's ``no turning points,'' cashed in one unit later.
  \item The property is not a new rule to memorize --- it is a consequence of a shape students already described.
  \item $b\neq1$ and $b>0$ were rules about \emph{models} in 6.1. Today they become the conditions that make \emph{solving} valid: $1^{5}=1^{9}$ breaks everything.
  \item Step 1 is Unit 5 work. Almost every wrong answer today is an exponent-rule slip, not a solving slip.
  \item A fractional answer is not a symptom of error --- the exponent is a location on the $x$-axis.
  \item Solving and graphing are two views of one question: where does the curve meet the line?
  \item ``No solution'' is a \emph{range} fact, settled without algebra.
  \item The method's limits are the point: knowing where it stops is what makes 6.4 and Unit 7 make sense.
\end{itemize}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{sky}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\textbf{Exponential equation} & An equation with the variable in an exponent: $2^{x}=32$, $9^{\,x-2}=27^{x}$. \\
\textbf{Common base} & One base that \emph{both} sides can be rewritten as a power of. Finding it is Step 1, and it is where the work is. \\
\textbf{One-to-one property of exponents} & \TallMath{b^{\,m}=b^{\,n} \;\Longrightarrow\; m=n \quad (b>0,\; b\neq1)} \\
\textbf{Why it holds} & An exponential has no turning points, so it is one-to-one --- no output is ever produced by two different inputs. \\
\textbf{Power of a power} & $\left(b^{m}\right)^{n}=b^{mn}$. The exponents \emph{multiply}. Contrast $b^{m}\cdot b^{n}=b^{m+n}$. \\
\textbf{Rational exponent} & $\sqrt[n]{b}=b^{1/n}$ (Lesson 5.1) --- what makes $4^{x}=\sqrt8$ solvable. \\
\textbf{Solution as an intersection} & The solution of $b^{x}=c$ is the $x$-coordinate where $y=b^{x}$ meets $y=c$. \\
\textbf{No solution (range)} & If $c\le0$ the line never meets the curve, because the range is $(0,\infty)$. \\
\textbf{Out of reach vs.\ nonexistent} & $2^{x}=5$ has a solution the method cannot name; $2^{x}=-3$ has none at all. Different failures. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{fixedskillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{forestbg}
    The Warm-Up rehearses exactly what today needs, in the order the lesson uses it. \textbf{(1)}
    Rewriting numbers as powers --- $4$, $32$, $\frac18$, $\sqrt2$ over base $2$; $81$, $\frac1{27}$
    over base $3$; $125$ over $5$; $\sqrt[3]{7}$ over $7$ --- which is Unit 5 (\texttt{A2.EO.2c})
    and is the whole of Step 1. \textbf{(2)} The add-versus-multiply contrast, done \emph{numerically}
    before it is ever stated as a rule: $2^{3}\cdot2^{4}=128=2^{7}$ against
    $\left(2^{3}\right)^{4}=4096=2^{12}$. This is the single most common error of the lesson, and
    students who compute both numbers cannot later claim $\left(2^{3}\right)^{x}=2^{\,3+x}$.
    \textbf{(3)} A table of $y=2^{x}$ and one question: does this function ever produce the same
    output twice? The answer is a fact about \emph{shape} (Lesson 6.0: no turning points), and it is
    the justification for everything in the lesson --- so do not let it pass as a table observation.
    Debrief items 2 and 3 at the board and leave $2^{3}\cdot2^{4}=2^{7}$ and
    $\left(2^{3}\right)^{4}=2^{12}$ written where they will stay all period.
\end{fixedskillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{forestbg}
    Two graphs on the board, and one gold horizontal line at the same height on each: $y=x^{2}$ with
    $y=4$, and $y=2^{x}$ with $y=4$. Under them, the two equations: \textbf{$x^{2}=4$} and
    \textbf{$2^{x}=4$}. Students solve both in ten seconds --- $\pm2$ and $2$ --- and that is not the
    question. Ask instead: \textbf{``The line is at the same height in both pictures. Why does it hit
    one curve twice and the other exactly once?''} Take answers until someone reaches the parabola's
    turning point: it climbs to a height of $4$ on the way up and hits the same height again on the way
    down, while the exponential only ever climbs. Then make it a general claim: \textbf{``Which fact
    from Lesson 6.0 guarantees the right-hand picture can never look like the left-hand one?''} ---
    \emph{no turning points, no extrema; always increasing or always decreasing.} Close by stating the
    consequence and writing it above both graphs, where it stays all period: \textbf{an exponential
    never uses the same output twice, so if the bases match, the exponents have no choice but to match
    too.} Do not introduce the property's fine print here; Section 1 earns it.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{forestbg}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. The permission slip.} Define \emph{exponential equation}, then box the one-to-one property.
State it as a consequence, never as a new rule: a horizontal line meets a curve with no turning points
at most once, so $b^{m}=b^{n}$ forces $m=n$. Then spend real time on the fine print, because it pays a
debt from Lesson 6.1. Students were told a model needs $b>0$ and $b\neq1$ and accepted it. Now put
$1^{5}=1^{9}$ on the board: with $b=1$ the graph is flat, every output repeats, and matching bases
proves nothing. The restriction was never arbitrary --- it is precisely the condition today's method
requires. Add $(-4)^{1/2}$ for $b>0$ and move on.

\textbf{2. Getting the bases to match.} Fill the powers-of-$2$/$3$/$5$ table together; it is Warm-Up
item 1 with more entries and it is Step 1 for the rest of the year. Then the red box, which is the
whole error surface of the lesson: \emph{power of a power multiplies} (point at the Warm-Up numbers,
do not re-derive), and \emph{the multiplication has to reach both terms} of a binomial exponent ---
$\left(3^{2}\right)^{x+4}=3^{\,2(x+4)}=3^{\,2x+8}$. Insist on the parentheses being written before
they are removed. Nearly every wrong answer today comes out of these two lines.

\columnbreak

\textbf{3. Three steps, four examples.} One base, equal exponents, solve. Work A ($2^{\,x+1}=32$),
B ($8^{x}=4$), C ($3^{x}=\frac1{27}$), D ($9^{\,x-2}=27^{x}$) across the table. \textbf{B is the one to
slow down on:} $x=\frac23$, and a fractional answer reads as ``I did it wrong'' to most students, who
will erase correct work. Check it with rational exponents in full --- $8^{2/3}=\left(\sqrt[3]8\right)^{2}=4$
--- and say the sentence out loud: the exponent is a location on the $x$-axis, and nothing requires it to
be a whole number. \textbf{D is the distribution trap;} a student who writes $3^{\,2x-2}$ gets $x=-2$
and never sees why.

\textbf{4. The same answer, seen.} Every equation solved today is a crossing point. On $y=2^{x}$ the
gold line $y=4$ meets the curve at $x=2$ --- which is exactly where $y=2^{x}-4$ crossed the $x$-axis in
Lesson 6.2, and 6.2's homework and exit ticket both promised this moment. The red line $y=-3$ never
meets it: not an algebra failure but a \textbf{range} fact, $(0,\infty)$. Establish the count --- one
solution when $c>0$, none when $c\le0$, never two --- and name where the $\pm$ reflex comes from. Then
the blue box: $2^{x}=5$. The line \emph{does} cross, between $x=2$ and $x=3$, so a solution exists;
$5$ is simply not a power of $2$. Post all three unsolved equations. Preview Lesson~6.4.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
Circulate during the notes practice and Tier work. Watch for and correct:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{adding} where power-of-a-power \textbf{multiplies} --- $\left(2^{3}\right)^{x}=2^{\,3+x}$; this is \emph{the} signature error, and the fix is Warm-Up item 2, not a lecture;
  \item failing to distribute across a binomial exponent --- $9^{\,x-2}$ written as $3^{\,2x-2}$;
  \item comparing exponents that sit on \textbf{different bases} --- ``$9^{x}=27$, so $x=3$'' (ask: \emph{whose} exponent is $x$?);
  \item erasing a correct fractional answer because it ``looks wrong'' --- $\frac23$, $\frac32$, $\frac53$, $\frac16$ all appear today;
  \item reading $=1$ as ``no exponent'' and declaring no solution, instead of $b^{0}$;
  \item dropping the negative when a reciprocal is rewritten --- $\frac{1}{27}=3^{-3}$, not $3^{3}$;
  \item the $\pm$ reflex from Unit 2 --- an exponential equation of this shape never has two answers;
  \item answering ``no solution'' for $2^{x}=5$: the correct statement is that the solution exists and this method cannot reach it;
  \item in Tier E, treating $1^{x}=1$ as having one solution rather than infinitely many.
\end{itemize}
Cold-call prompts: ``What base are you rewriting to, and why that one?'' ``Did the exponents add or
multiply there? Prove it with the Warm-Up numbers.'' ``Whose exponent is $x$ --- the $9$'s or the
$3$'s?'' ``Is your answer a whole number? Should it be?'' ``Does that equation have no solution, or a
solution we can't find yet? Those are different sentences.''
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\textbf{Make the Bases Match --- and Say Why You're Allowed To} activity (tiered; mirrors the notes).
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate.} A rewriting table over bases $2$, $3$, and $5$ (including $1=b^{0}$ and
reciprocals), then six one-rewrite equations: $2^{x}=64$, $5^{\,x-1}=25$, $3^{x}=\frac19$, $4^{x}=64$,
$10^{\,2x}=1000$, and $7^{\,x+2}=1$. Two are then \emph{proved} by substitution. The last equation is
the one to debrief: $=1$ means $7^{0}$, and students who read it as ``no exponent'' will say no
solution.

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching.} Six equations where both sides must move --- $9^{x}=27$,
$8^{\,x+1}=16$, $\left(\frac13\right)^{x}=81$, $25^{x}=125^{\,x-1}$, $4^{\,3x}=8^{\,x+2}$,
$6^{\,2x-1}=\frac1{36}$ --- with three non-integer answers among them. Then the same work read off a
graph of $y=3^{x}$ against $y=9$, $y=1$, and $y=-2$, closing on \emph{how many times can a horizontal
line cross an exponential curve?} Then the \textbf{error analysis}: Devon adds instead of multiplying
($8^{x}=4 \Rightarrow x=-1$), Priya never rewrites the left side ($9^{x}=27\Rightarrow x=3$). The two
need different re-teaches, and students are asked which one Warm-Up item 2 would fix.

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension.} Three parts. \emph{Radicals are exponents too:} $2^{x}=\sqrt[3]2$,
$4^{x}=\sqrt8$, $\left(\frac15\right)^{\,x-1}=\sqrt[3]{25}$, and $2^{x}\cdot8^{\,x-1}=32$, which needs
the product rule on top of everything else. \emph{The fine print, tested:} students construct
$1^{m}=1^{n}$ themselves, discover $1^{x}=1$ has infinitely many solutions, and then state the
\emph{solving} reason for $b\neq1$. \emph{A colony:} $P(t)=50\cdot2^{t}$ reaches $3200$ at $t=6$ and
$12{,}800$ at $t=8$ --- but $1000$ needs $2^{t}=20$, which has no common base and must be trapped
between $t=4$ and $t=5$. Part (e) is the item of the day: one of $50\cdot2^{t}=1000$ and
$50\cdot2^{t}=0$ has no solution, the other has one we cannot yet find.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (independent, no notes), four items on one page: \textbf{(1)} solve
$3^{\,x+1}=81$ with the common-base step shown; \textbf{(2)} solve $25^{x}=125$, where the two sides
do not start on the same base and the answer is $\frac32$ --- a wrong answer of exactly $3$ means the
student matched $125$'s exponent without rewriting $25$, which is Priya's error from the Activity;
\textbf{(3)} explain, without algebra, why $2^{x}=-8$ cannot be solved, using the \emph{range}; and
\textbf{(4)} \textbf{multiple choice} --- which of $9^{x}=27$, $5^{x}=\frac1{125}$, $2^{x}=10$,
$8^{x}=\sqrt2$ \emph{cannot} be solved with a common base (answer: $2^{x}=10$) --- followed by the most
informative line on the page: \emph{does that equation still have a solution?} ``No'' is wrong, and it
is a different misconception from anything in items 1--3. \emph{Note:} this is deliberately an ordinary
multiple-choice item and \textbf{not} an SOL-style item --- 6.3 has no SOL standard, and per the unit
ruling its content stays off the SOL-style items while still being assessed on the unit test. Sort the
collected tickets into \emph{got it / rewrite / range / the wall}. The \emph{rewrite} pile needs
practice, not re-teaching; the \emph{range} pile gets the Section 4 graph again; a large \emph{wall}
pile means Lesson 6.4 opens with five minutes on $y=2^{x}$ and the line $y=10$.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} eight equations spanning every rewrite type, three of which have non-integer answers
--- and students must list those three and explain why that is not a symptom of error; a graph of
$y=2^{x}$ read in \emph{both} directions (given the lines $y=8$, $y=2$, $y=-4$, find the solutions;
then given the solutions $x=0$ and $x=-1$, name the lines --- $y=1$ and $y=\frac12$, the second of
which students often think is illegal); a \textbf{sorting task} on whether a common base exists, whose
two ``no'' rows ($2^{x}=12$ and $10^{x}=45$) must then be trapped between consecutive integers;
\textbf{error analysis} repeating the Activity's two mechanical errors with new numbers (Jamal adds,
Elise fails to distribute); and a closing $x^{2}=64$ versus $2^{x}=64$ that sends students back to the
Hook. \textbf{Extension:} $4^{x}\cdot2^{\,x+1}=8^{\,x-1}$, which reduces to $3x+1=3x-3$ --- a
contradiction, so there is genuinely \textbf{no solution}. Students then rewrite each side as a
multiple of $8^{x}$ ($2\cdot8^{x}$ and $\frac18\cdot8^{x}$) and explain in \emph{Lesson 6.2's} language
why two vertical stretches of one curve never meet, since $8^{x}$ is never $0$. Part (d) is the payoff:
compare this with $2^{x}=5$ and name the two different reasons the method failed. \textbf{Preview:}
Lesson~6.4, \emph{Compound Interest, Half-Life, and $e$} --- where the numbers come from money and
medicine and nobody arranges them conveniently: $1000(1.05)^{t}=2000$, and $2$ is not a power of
$1.05$. Students leave with four unsolved equations on a list --- $2^{x}=5$, $132(0.85)^{t}=32$,
$(0.9)^{t}=2.118$, and now the investment --- every one of which has an answer. Do not name logarithms;
that is Unit 7.
\end{skillbox}
