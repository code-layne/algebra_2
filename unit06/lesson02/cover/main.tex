\begin{remindbox}
\small Every exponential graph in this unit is the parent $y=b^{x}$ with four things done to it:
$g(x)=a\,b^{\,x-h}+k$. The letter $a$ stretches or compresses the graph and, when it is
\textbf{negative}, flips it over the $x$-axis. The letter $h$ slides it sideways --- \emph{right} when
it is subtracted, exactly as it did for the parabola and the square root. Replacing $x$ with $-x$
flips the graph over the $y$-axis, which for this family turns \textbf{growth into decay}, since
$b^{-x}=\left(\frac1b\right)^{x}$. \textbf{But only $k$ moves the horizontal asymptote}, from $y=0$ to
$y=k$ --- and because the range is the side of the asymptote the graph lives on, $k$ is the only
letter that can change the range: $(k,\infty)$ when $a>0$, and $(-\infty,k)$ when $a<0$. Always a
parenthesis at $k$, never a bracket; the asymptote is a boundary the range \emph{excludes}. One more
consequence: Lesson 6.0 said an exponential never crosses the $x$-axis, and a vertical shift is
exactly what can change that --- the graph has an $x$-intercept precisely when $a$ and $k$ have
\textbf{opposite signs}. Reading a graph backwards runs in the same order: the dashed line gives $k$,
the $y$-intercept is $a+k$ (so subtract $k$ before you claim $a$), and one more point gives $b$.
\end{remindbox}
