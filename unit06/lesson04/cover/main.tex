\begin{remindbox}
\small In Lesson 6.0 you found a radical function's domain by \emph{solving} an inequality. Today you
stop solving. Written as $y=a\sqrt{x-h}+k$, a radical graph is the parent moved so that its
\textbf{anchor point} --- the endpoint of a square root, the inflection point of a cube root --- sits
at $(h,k)$. Everything follows from that one point: the domain is $[h,\infty)$ and the range is
$[k,\infty)$, both starting right there. The signs behave as they always have --- $k$ is outside and
moves the graph \emph{vertically in the same direction} as its sign; $h$ is inside and moves it
\emph{horizontally in the opposite direction}, so $\sqrt{x-3}$ goes \textbf{right} $3$. The
coefficient $a$ stretches ($|a|>1$) or compresses ($|a|<1$) the graph vertically and never moves the
anchor; a \textbf{negative} $a$ flips the arm downward, which makes the anchor an absolute
\emph{maximum} and turns the range into $(-\infty,k]$. Run it backwards to get the equation from a
graph: read the anchor for $h$ and $k$, then substitute one more point to find $a$ --- \emph{anchor
first, always}. Two warnings. If the radicand is not in the form $x-h$, \textbf{factor first}:
$\sqrt{4x-8}=\sqrt{4(x-2)}=2\sqrt{x-2}$ starts at $x=2$, not $8$. And because that factoring is
always available, squeezing a radical's \emph{input} and stretching its \emph{output} turn out to be
the same transformation --- $\sqrt{9x}$ and $3\sqrt{x}$ are one graph with two names.
\end{remindbox}
