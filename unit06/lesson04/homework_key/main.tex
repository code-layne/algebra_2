\begin{notesbox}{Practice}
Write every domain and range in \textbf{interval notation}. Show how you found each answer.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=12pt]
  \item \textbf{From the equation only.} No graphing --- read each row off the transformation form.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X X X X@{}}
    \hline
    \textbf{Function} & \textbf{Parent} & \textbf{Transformations, in order} & \textbf{Anchor point} & \textbf{Domain \& range} \\
    \hline
    $\sqrt{x-5}+2$ & \ans{$\sqrt{x}$} & \ans{right 5, up 2} & \ans{$(5,2)$} & \ans{$[5,\infty)$; $[2,\infty)$} \\
    $\sqrt[3]{x+3}-1$ & \ans{$\sqrt[3]{x}$} & \ans{left 3, down 1} & \ans{$(-3,-1)$} & \ans{all reals; all reals} \\
    $-4\sqrt{x}+2$ & \ans{$\sqrt{x}$} & \ans{stretch by 4, reflect over the $x$-axis, up 2} & \ans{$(0,2)$} & \ans{$[0,\infty)$; $(-\infty,2]$} \\
    $\tfrac12\sqrt{x+6}-3$ & \ans{$\sqrt{x}$} & \ans{left 6, compress by $\tfrac12$, down 3} & \ans{$(-6,-3)$} & \ans{$[-6,\infty)$; $[-3,\infty)$} \\
    \hline
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}

  \item \textbf{Read the graph.} The graph is $m(x)=-\sqrt{x+4}+3$.
    \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.44]
      \lgrid{-6}{13}{-3}{5}
      \begin{scope}
        \clip (-6,-3) rectangle (13,5);
        \draw[very thick, forest, domain=-4:13, samples=110, smooth] plot (\x,{3-sqrt((\x)+4)});
      \end{scope}
      \fill[forest] (-4,3) circle (4pt) node[above right, font=\scriptsize]{$(-4,3)$};
      \fill[charcoal] (0,1) circle (2.6pt) node[above right, font=\scriptsize]{$(0,1)$};
      \fill[charcoal] (5,0) circle (2.6pt) node[above right, font=\scriptsize]{$(5,0)$};
      \fill[charcoal] (12,-1) circle (2.6pt);
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{center}
    $a=\mans{-1}$, \; $h=\mans{-4}$, \; $k=\mans{3}$ \quad Anchor: \ans{$(-4,3)$} \\[3pt]
    Domain: \ans{$[-4,\infty)$} \quad Range: \ans{$(-\infty,3]$} \\[3pt]
    $x$-intercept: \ans{$(5,0)$} \quad $y$-intercept: \ans{$(0,1)$} \\[3pt]
    Increasing or decreasing on its domain? \ans{decreasing} \\[3pt]
    Absolute \ans{maximum} of \ans{$3$} at $x=\mans{-4}$; \; no absolute \ans{minimum} \\[3pt]
    End behavior: as $x\to+\infty$, $m(x)\to\mans{-\infty}$. \; As $x\to-\infty$: \ans{the graph
    stops at $x=-4$}

  \item \textbf{Write the equation --- square root.} The graph below has the form
        $y=a\sqrt{x-h}+k$.
    \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.44]
      \lgrid{-5}{11}{-2}{6}
      \begin{scope}
        \clip (-5,-2) rectangle (11,6);
        \draw[very thick, forest, domain=-3:11, samples=110, smooth] plot (\x,{4-sqrt((\x)+3)});
      \end{scope}
      \fill[forest] (-3,4) circle (4pt) node[above right, font=\scriptsize]{$(-3,4)$};
      \fill[charcoal] (1,2) circle (2.6pt) node[above right, font=\scriptsize]{$(1,2)$};
      \fill[charcoal] (6,1) circle (2.6pt) node[above right, font=\scriptsize]{$(6,1)$};
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{center}
    Anchor \ans{$(-3,4)$} $\Rightarrow$ $h=\mans{-3}$, $k=\mans{4}$. \; Substitute $(1,2)$:
    $a=\mans{-1}$. \\[3pt]
    \textbf{Equation:} $y=$ \ans{$-\sqrt{x+3}+4$} \quad Check it against $(6,1)$:
    \ans{$-\sqrt{9}+4=1$ \checkmark}

    \vspace{2pt}
    {\small\emph{Work for $a$:} $2=a\sqrt{1+3}+4 \Rightarrow 2=2a+4 \Rightarrow -2=2a \Rightarrow
    a=-1$. The graph falls, so a negative $a$ was expected before any algebra.}

  \item \textbf{Write the equation --- cube root.} The graph below has the form
        $y=a\sqrt[3]{x-h}+k$.
    \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.40]
      \lgrid{-8}{10}{-6}{4}
      \begin{scope}
        \clip (-8,-6) rectangle (10,4);
        \cbrtplot{2}{1}{-1}{-8}{10}
      \end{scope}
      \fill[forest] (1,-1) circle (5pt) node[right, font=\scriptsize]{$(1,-1)$};
      \fill[charcoal] (9,3) circle (3pt) node[below right, font=\scriptsize]{$(9,3)$};
      \fill[charcoal] (-7,-5) circle (3pt) node[above right, font=\scriptsize]{$(-7,-5)$};
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{center}
    Inflection point \ans{$(1,-1)$} $\Rightarrow$ $h=\mans{1}$, $k=\mans{-1}$. \;
    Substitute $(9,3)$: $a=\mans{2}$. \\[3pt]
    \textbf{Equation:} $y=$ \ans{$2\sqrt[3]{x-1}-1$} \\[3pt]
    Why does this function have \emph{no} endpoint, even though it has an $h$ and a $k$?
    \ans{Because the index is odd, so no input is forbidden --- nothing stops the graph.}

  \item \textbf{Factor first.} Rewrite each in the form $a\sqrt[n]{x-h}+k$, then give the anchor
        point and the domain.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
    \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X X X@{}}
    \hline
    \textbf{Function} & \textbf{Rewritten} & \textbf{Anchor} & \textbf{Domain} \\
    \hline
    $y=\sqrt{25x}$ & \ans{$5\sqrt{x}$} & \ans{$(0,0)$} & \ans{$[0,\infty)$} \\
    $y=\sqrt{16x-32}$ & \ans{$4\sqrt{x-2}$} & \ans{$(2,0)$} & \ans{$[2,\infty)$} \\
    $y=\sqrt[3]{27x-27}$ & \ans{$3\sqrt[3]{x-1}$} & \ans{$(1,0)$} & \ans{all reals} \\
    \hline
    \end{tabularx}
    \end{center}

  \item \textbf{Explain.} The graphs of $y=-\sqrt{x}$ and $y=\sqrt{-x}$ are \emph{different}. The
        graphs of $y=-\sqrt[3]{x}$ and $y=\sqrt[3]{-x}$ are \emph{the same}. Explain both facts at
        once, using the words \textbf{index} and \textbf{symmetry}.
        \ansline{Both pairs ask the same question --- flip the outputs, or flip the inputs? --- and
        the \textbf{index} decides whether the two flips agree. A square root has an \emph{even}
        index, so it accepts only $x\ge0$: $-\sqrt{x}$ lives to the right of the $y$-axis and hangs
        below the $x$-axis, while $\sqrt{-x}$ accepts only $x\le0$ and so lives to the \emph{left}
        and above. They do not even share a domain, let alone a graph. A cube root has an
        \emph{odd} index, which is exactly the statement that $\sqrt[3]{-x}=-\sqrt[3]{x}$ --- the
        origin \textbf{symmetry} of Lesson 6.0, $f(-x)=-f(x)$. Moving the minus sign from the inside
        to the outside changes nothing, so the vertical flip and the horizontal flip produce one
        curve. The square root has no symmetry to trade on, so its two flips stay apart.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{extensionbox}
\textbf{How far can you see?} On a clear day, from an eye height of $h$ feet above the water, the
distance to the horizon is about
\[
  d(h) = \sqrt{1.5h} \ \text{ miles.}
\]
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$h$ (feet of eye height) & $6$ & $24$ & $54$ & $96$ & $150$ \\ \hline
$d(h)$ (miles) & $3$ & $6$ & $9$ & $12$ & $15$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Give the domain of $d$ from the radicand. Does the context restrict it any further?
        \ansline{$1.5h\ge0$ gives $h\ge0$, so $[0,\infty)$ --- and the context agrees exactly, since
        a negative eye height is meaningless. (In practice it also has a sensible upper limit; the
        formula assumes you are close enough to the surface for the curvature model to hold.)}
  \item The graph of $d$ has its anchor point at $(0,0)$. What does that point mean for a swimmer
        with their eyes right at water level? \ansline{Eye height $0$ gives viewing distance $0$:
        with your eyes exactly at the surface the horizon is at your eyes, and you can see no
        distance at all. Every inch you rise buys you some view --- and the first inches buy the
        most, because the graph is steepest at the anchor.}
  \item From the table: going from $h=6$ to $h=24$ multiplies the eye height by $4$ but multiplies
        the viewing distance by only $2$. Use the shape of a square root graph to explain why.
        \ansline{A square root grows like the $\frac12$ power, so multiplying the input by $4$
        multiplies the output by $\sqrt4=2$. On the graph that is the flattening: each extra foot of
        height adds less distance than the foot before it. To \emph{double} your view you must
        \emph{quadruple} your height --- $6\to24$, then $24\to96$, which the table confirms
        ($6\to12$ miles).}
  \item Use the product property to rewrite $d(h)=\sqrt{1.5h}$ in the form $a\sqrt{h}$, giving $a$ to
        two decimal places. Then finish this sentence: \emph{the horizon formula is just the parent
        $y=\sqrt{h}$ with a single \underline{\hspace{2.4cm}} applied to it.}
        \ansline{$\sqrt{1.5h}=\sqrt{1.5}\cdot\sqrt{h}\approx 1.22\sqrt{h}$ --- so the horizon formula
        is the parent $y=\sqrt h$ with a single \textbf{vertical stretch} (by about $1.22$) applied
        to it. No shift, no reflection: same anchor, same domain, same shape, just scaled.}
  \item A sailor climbs $h$ feet up the mast of a ship whose deck already sits $20$ feet above the
        water, so their eye height above the water is $h+20$ and the formula becomes
        $D(h)=\sqrt{1.5(h+20)}$. Describe how the graph of $D$ differs from the graph of $d$, and
        give the anchor point of $D$. \ansline{$D$ is $d$ shifted $20$ units to the \emph{left}
        (the $+20$ is inside, so it moves the graph the opposite way). Its anchor point is
        $(-20,0)$. Nothing else changes --- the stretch of $\approx1.22$ and the shape are the
        same.}
  \item Part (e)'s anchor point is not a place any sailor can stand. Explain why, and state the
        domain that the \emph{context} actually allows --- then give $D(0)$ and say what it means.
        \ansline{$h=-20$ would mean climbing $20$ feet \emph{below} the deck --- down to the
        waterline. Algebra allows it; the mast does not. The context domain is $h\ge0$, so the
        usable part of the graph starts at $h=0$, well to the right of the anchor.
        $D(0)=\sqrt{1.5(20)}=\sqrt{30}\approx5.5$ miles: standing on the deck without climbing at
        all, the sailor already sees about $5.5$ miles. \emph{An anchor point can sit outside the
        domain a situation allows --- read the context, not only the equation.}}
\end{enumerate}
\end{extensionbox}

\begin{teachernote}
\small
\textbf{Item 1} is the whole lesson in twenty blanks and should take five minutes. The row that
separates understanding from copying is $-4\sqrt{x}+2$: students who write the range as
$[2,\infty)$ have found the anchor without noticing that a negative $a$ turns the arm --- and
therefore the range --- upside down. \textbf{Item 2} is deliberately the same shape, read off a
picture instead, so the two can be marked against each other.

\textbf{Items 3 and 4 are the SOL's harder direction} (\textbf{A2.F.1b}). Both are set up so the sign
of $a$ can be predicted before any algebra: item 3's graph falls, so $a<0$. Students who substitute
first and get $a=-1$ ``by surprise'' have not yet connected the coefficient to the picture. Item 4's
cube root reinforces that the anchor works identically even when it is not an endpoint.

\textbf{Item 6 is the conceptual item} and is worth the most discussion time. A complete answer must
do more than say ``cube roots are odd'' --- it has to connect $\sqrt[3]{-x}=-\sqrt[3]{x}$ to why the
two square-root graphs cannot possibly coincide (they do not share a domain). Accept any wording
that gets both halves.

\textbf{The extension is the unit's fifth model} (after free-fall, Kepler, Kleiber, the pendulum, and
the golden ratio) and the first where the payoff is a \emph{transformation} rather than a value. Part
(d) is the point of the whole thing: a real formula from navigation turns out to be nothing but the
parent square root with one vertical stretch, which is what today's lesson claims all radical
functions are. Part (f) is the sharpest item --- an anchor point that exists algebraically but not
physically. Students who answered part (a) thoughtfully will see it immediately.
\end{teachernote}
