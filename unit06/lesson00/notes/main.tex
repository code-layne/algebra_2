Radical functions made us ask \emph{where is the graph allowed to exist}. Exponential functions hand
the whole $x$-axis back and take something else away: \textbf{an entire half of the $y$-axis}, with a
line the graph gets closer and closer to but \emph{never} touches.
\end{hookbox}

\begin{notesbox}{1. What Makes a Function Exponential --- and How to Spot One in a Table}
Every family this year has kept the variable in the \textbf{base}. An \textbf{exponential function}
moves it into the \textbf{exponent}:
\[
  \underbrace{y=x^{2}}_{\text{\small variable is the \emph{base} --- quadratic}}
  \qquad\text{versus}\qquad
  \underbrace{y=2^{x}}_{\text{\small variable is the \emph{exponent} --- exponential}}
\]
The general form is
\[
  f(x) = a\,b^{\,x}, \qquad a\ne 0,\quad b>0,\quad b\ne 1 .
\]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item $a$ is the \textbf{initial value}. Because $b^{0}=$ \blank{0.8cm} for \emph{every} legal
        base, $f(0)=a$ --- so $a$ is always the \blank{2.6cm}.
  \item $b$ is the \textbf{base}, also called the growth or decay \textbf{factor}. It is what you
        \textbf{multiply} by every time $x$ goes up by $1$.
  \item Why must $b\ne 1$? Because $1^{x}=$ \blank{0.8cm} for every $x$, which would make the graph
        a \blank{3.0cm} line --- not a new family at all.
  \item Why must $b>0$? A negative base collapses: $(-4)^{1/2}$ is not a real number, so the graph
        would be a scatter of holes instead of a smooth curve.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{The fingerprint test.} With evenly spaced $x$-values, a table names the family for you ---
no graph required.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Family} & \textbf{What stays constant} & \textbf{Example ($y$-values)} \\
\hline
Linear (Unit 1) & the \emph{difference} & $2,\ 5,\ 8,\ 11,\ 14$ \quad (add \blank{0.9cm} each time) \\
Quadratic (Unit 2) & the \emph{second} difference & $1,\ 4,\ 9,\ 16,\ 25$ \quad (differences $3,5,7,9$; those change by $+2$ each time) \\
\textbf{Exponential (Unit 6)} & the \textbf{ratio} & $2,\ 6,\ 18,\ 54,\ 162$ \quad (multiply by \blank{0.9cm} each time) \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
That last row has a name: a \textbf{constant ratio}. Divide any output by the one before it ---
$\frac{6}{2}=$ \blank{0.7cm}, \; $\frac{18}{6}=$ \blank{0.7cm}, \; $\frac{54}{18}=$
\blank{0.7cm} --- and if you always get the same number, the function is exponential and that
number is \blank{1.0cm}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Growth or decay?} Compare the base to $1$ --- \emph{not} to $0$.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
\hline
$b>1$ & exponential \textbf{growth} & each step multiplies by more than $1$, so the outputs \blank{2.4cm} \\
$0<b<1$ & exponential \textbf{decay} & each step multiplies by a proper fraction, so the outputs \blank{2.4cm} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Domain:} \blank{3.2cm} \quad \textbf{Range:} \blank{3.2cm} \\
        \emph{Watch the bracket.} $0$ is \textbf{not} in the range.
  \item \textbf{$y$-intercept:} \blank{1.8cm} \quad (that is $a=1$, exactly as predicted).
  \item \textbf{$x$-intercept / zeros:} \blank{2.2cm}. \; From Warm-Up item~1, $2^{x}$ is a
        \blank{2.2cm} number for every real $x$, so it never equals $0$. \emph{This family never
        has a zero.}
  \item \textbf{Increasing / decreasing:} increasing on \blank{3.0cm}; never \blank{2.2cm}.
  \item \textbf{Positive / negative:} $y>0$ on \blank{3.0cm}; \quad $y<0$ \blank{2.4cm}.
  \item \textbf{Extrema:} \blank{2.6cm}. There is no maximum (it climbs forever) --- and no minimum
        either, even though the graph clearly flattens out. Section 4 explains that second one.
  \item \textbf{Horizontal asymptote:} $y=$ \blank{1.0cm} \quad (the dashed line).
  \item \textbf{End behavior:} as $x\to+\infty$, $y\to$ \blank{1.2cm} --- and \emph{fast}. \\
        As $x\to-\infty$, $y\to$ \blank{1.2cm}, flattening onto the asymptote from
        \blank{1.8cm}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{2pt}
The base is now a \emph{fraction}, so every step \textbf{shrinks} the output --- and the picture is
the growth parent's mirror image.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Domain:} \blank{3.2cm} \quad \textbf{Range:} \blank{3.2cm} \quad
        (\emph{identical} to the growth parent).
  \item \textbf{$y$-intercept:} \blank{1.8cm} \quad \textbf{$x$-intercept:} \blank{1.8cm}
  \item \textbf{Increasing / decreasing:} \blank{3.4cm}
  \item \textbf{Extrema:} \blank{2.6cm}, for the same reason as before.
  \item \textbf{Horizontal asymptote:} $y=$ \blank{1.0cm} --- the same line, but the graph now
        approaches it on the \blank{1.8cm} side instead of the left.
  \item \textbf{End behavior:} as $x\to+\infty$, $y\to$ \blank{1.2cm}; \; as $x\to-\infty$,
        $y\to$ \blank{1.2cm}.
  \item \textbf{Why these two are secretly the same graph.} Since
        $\left(\frac12\right)^{x}=\dfrac{1}{2^{x}}$, the decay parent is the growth parent with the
        exponent \blank{2.4cm}, and its graph is the growth graph reflected across the
        \blank{1.6cm}-axis. Lesson 6.2 makes this a rule.
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{4. The Horizontal Asymptote: a Floor the Graph Never Stands On}
A \textbf{horizontal asymptote} is a horizontal line the graph approaches as $x$ runs toward
$+\infty$ or $-\infty$. You met these in Unit 4 with rational functions. Here they take on a new job:
the asymptote is the \textbf{boundary of the range}.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$x$ & $-1$ & $-4$ & $-10$ & $-20$ \\ \hline
$2^{x}$ & $0.5$ & $0.0625$ & $0.00098\ldots$ & $0.00000095\ldots$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The outputs are being halved over and over. They get as close to $0$ as you like --- and they are
never \blank{1.6cm} and never \blank{1.6cm}. Halving a positive number leaves a positive number,
forever.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item Therefore the range is $(0,\infty)$ and \textbf{not} $[0,\infty)$. Write the reason in your
        own words: \writeline
  \item Therefore there is \textbf{no absolute minimum}. A minimum must be a value the function
        \emph{actually reaches}, and $0$ is never reached.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{3pt}

\emph{touches} it.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item Unit 5's endpoint was \emph{in} the domain and \emph{in} the range, so it produced an
        absolute \blank{1.8cm}. An asymptote is in \blank{2.0cm} of them.
  \item Unit 4's rational graphs had horizontal asymptotes too --- but a rational graph is
        \emph{allowed} to cross its horizontal asymptote in the middle. An exponential graph
        $y=ab^{x}$ never crosses its own, because $b^{x}$ is never \blank{1.4cm}.
  \item \textbf{The trade.} Unit 5 took away half the \blank{2.0cm} (no negative radicands). Unit 6
        gives that back --- every real number is a legal exponent --- but takes away half the
        \blank{2.0cm} instead.
\end{itemize}
\end{notesbox}

Read \emph{every} characteristic off the anchor $f(x)=4\left(\frac12\right)^{x}$.

  \end{scope}

  \fill[charcoal] (1,2) circle (2.6pt) node[above right, font=\scriptsize]{$(1,2)$};
  \fill[charcoal] (2,1) circle (2.6pt) node[above right, font=\scriptsize]{$(2,1)$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item $a=$ \blank{0.9cm} and $b=$ \blank{0.9cm}, so this is exponential \blank{1.8cm}
        (because $b$ is \blank{1.6cm} than $1$).
  \item \textbf{Domain:} \blank{3.2cm} \quad \textbf{Range:} \blank{3.2cm}
  \item \textbf{$y$-intercept:} \blank{1.8cm} \quad \textbf{$x$-intercept:} \blank{1.8cm}
  \item \textbf{Increasing / decreasing:} \blank{3.2cm}
  \item \textbf{Positive / negative:} $f(x)>0$ on \blank{2.8cm}; \quad $f(x)<0$ \blank{2.2cm}.
  \item \textbf{Extrema:} \blank{3.4cm}
  \item \textbf{Horizontal asymptote:} $y=$ \blank{1.0cm}, approached as $x\to$ \blank{1.4cm}.
  \item \textbf{End behavior:} as $x\to+\infty$, $f(x)\to$ \blank{1.2cm}; \; as $x\to-\infty$,
        $f(x)\to$ \blank{1.2cm}.
  \item \textbf{Check the constant ratio} using the labeled points: $\frac{f(1)}{f(0)}=\frac{2}{4}=$
        \blank{1.0cm}, and $\frac{f(2)}{f(1)}=\frac{1}{2}=$ \blank{1.0cm}. That is $b$, read
        straight off the graph.
\end{enumerate}

And against the families you already know: a \textbf{polynomial} could turn around many times; a
\textbf{rational} function broke apart at its vertical asymptotes; a \textbf{radical} function began
somewhere and stopped. An \textbf{exponential} function does none of those --- it is one smooth,
unbroken, never-turning curve living entirely on one side of a line it never crosses.
\end{notesbox}
