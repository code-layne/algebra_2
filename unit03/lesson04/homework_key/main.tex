\begin{notesbox}{Practice}
Constant term on top, leading coefficient on the bottom, and every candidate gets a $\pm$. When you
find a zero, divide it out and \emph{finish} --- an answer is not done until every factor is prime.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=11pt]
  \item \textbf{Just the list.} Write every possible rational zero. Reduce and remove repeats.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item $f(x) = x^3 + 3x^2 - 6x - 8$: \ans{$\pm 1,\ \pm 2,\ \pm 4,\ \pm 8$}
      \item $g(x) = 2x^3 - 5x^2 - 4x + 3$: \ans{$\pm 1,\ \pm 3,\ \pm\tfrac12,\ \pm\tfrac32$}
      \item $h(x) = 6x^3 + x^2 - 5x - 2$: \ans{$\pm 1,\ \pm 2,\ \pm\tfrac12,\ \pm\tfrac13,\ \pm\tfrac16,\ \pm\tfrac23$}

        \vspace{2pt}
        How many candidates are on list (c)? \ans{$12$}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Leading coefficient $1$.} Find all the zeros. Show the synthetic division on your own
    paper.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item $f(x) = x^3 + 3x^2 - 6x - 8$: \; first zero $r = $ \ans{$-1$}, depressed polynomial
        \ans{$x^2 + 2x - 8$}

        \vspace{2pt}
        Completely factored: \ans{$(x+1)(x+4)(x-2)$} \quad Zeros: \ans{$-1,\ -4,\ 2$}
      \item $p(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 3x + 10$: \; completely factored \ans{$(x+1)(x-2)(x-5)$}

        \vspace{2pt}
        Zeros: \ans{$-1,\ 2,\ 5$}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Leading coefficient not $1$.} Solve $2x^3 - 5x^2 - 4x + 3 = 0$.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item A candidate that works: $r = $ \ans{$3$} \; (show your test: \ans{$54-45-12+3=0$})
      \item Depressed polynomial: \ans{$2x^2 + x - 1$} \; which factors as \ans{$(2x-1)(x+1)$}
      \item Completely factored: \ans{$(x-3)(2x-1)(x+1)$}
      \item Solutions: \ans{$x = -1,\ \tfrac12,\ 3$} \; Which one would an integers-only list have missed?
        \ans{$\tfrac12$}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Degree 4.} Solve $x^4 - 4x^3 - x^2 + 16x - 12 = 0$. You will need the workflow
    \emph{twice}.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item Candidates: \ans{$\pm 1,\ \pm 2,\ \pm 3,\ \pm 4,\ \pm 6,\ \pm 12$}
      \item First zero $r_1 = $ \ans{$1$}, depressed polynomial \ans{$x^3 - 3x^2 - 4x + 12$}
      \item Second zero $r_2 = $ \ans{$2$}, next depressed polynomial \ans{$x^2 - x - 6$}
      \item Completely factored: \ans{$(x-1)(x-2)(x-3)(x+2)$} \quad Solutions: \ans{$x = -2,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3$}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{No hints this time.} On Lesson 3.3's exit ticket you were \emph{told} to divide
    $x^3 + 2x^2 - 5x - 6$ by $(x+1)$. Today nobody tells you.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item Candidates: \ans{$\pm 1,\ \pm 2,\ \pm 3,\ \pm 6$}
      \item Solve $x^3 + 2x^2 - 5x - 6 = 0$: \ans{$x = -3,\ -1,\ 2$}
      \item \textbf{Verify} your answer two ways: substitute one solution to show it gives $0$
        \ans{$f(2) = 8+8-10-6 = 0$}, and state what the graph of this function must look like at each solution
        \ans{it crosses the $x$-axis at all three (each zero has multiplicity 1)}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Not every zero is rational.} Solve $x^3 - 2x^2 - 3x + 6 = 0$.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item Candidates: \ans{$\pm 1,\ \pm 2,\ \pm 3,\ \pm 6$} \quad The one that works: $r = $ \ans{$2$}
      \item Depressed polynomial: \ans{$x^2 - 3$} \quad Solve it: $x = $ \ans{$\pm\sqrt{3}$}
      \item Two of the three solutions were never on your candidate list. Explain why the Rational
        Root Theorem could not have found them. \ans{$\pm\sqrt3$ are irrational --- they cannot be
        written as a ratio of integers, and the theorem only ever produces rational candidates.}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Explain.} Answer each in one or two sentences.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item Why is the candidate list for a polynomial with leading coefficient $1$ made only of
        integers? \ansline{The denominator $q$ must divide the leading coefficient, and the only divisors of $1$ are $\pm 1$. So $\frac{p}{q} = \pm p$, an integer.}
      \item A classmate tests every candidate on a cubic and none of them gives $0$. They conclude
        that they made an arithmetic mistake. Are they necessarily right? \ansline{No. The list only contains \emph{possible} rational zeros --- a polynomial such as $x^3-3x-1$ has none at all, and its zeros are irrational. They should check their arithmetic, but an empty result is a legitimate outcome.}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{extensionbox}
\textbf{Build them, don't just break them.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item A rectangular planter box holds $V(x) = 2x^3 + 3x^2 - 3x - 2$ cubic feet, where $x$ is
        measured in feet. Find all three dimensions as linear factors, then explain why $x$ must be
        greater than $1$ for the box to be physically possible.

        \ans{Candidates $\pm1, \pm2, \pm\tfrac12$; $V(1)=0$, and dividing gives $2x^2+5x+2 =
        (2x+1)(x+2)$. So $V(x) = (x-1)(2x+1)(x+2)$ --- the dimensions are $(x-1)$, $(2x+1)$, and
        $(x+2)$ feet. All three must be positive lengths; $2x+1$ and $x+2$ are positive as soon as
        $x>0$, but $x-1>0$ forces $x>1$.}
  \item Check your answer to (a) at $x = 3$: compute $V(3)$ directly, then multiply your three
        dimensions at $x = 3$. Do they agree?

        \ans{$V(3) = 54+27-9-2 = 70$; the dimensions are $2$, $7$, and $5$, and $2\cdot 7\cdot 5 =
        70$. They agree.}
  \item Write a cubic with \emph{integer} coefficients whose zeros are $-\tfrac23$, $1$, and $4$.
        (Start from the factors $(3x+2)$, $(x-1)$, and $(x-4)$.) Then confirm that $-\tfrac23$ really
        does appear on your polynomial's own candidate list.

        \ans{$(3x+2)(x-1)(x-4) = 3x^3 - 13x^2 + 2x + 8$. Its candidates are $\pm\frac{p}{q}$ with
        $p \mid 8$ and $q \mid 3$: $\pm1, \pm2, \pm4, \pm8, \pm\tfrac13, \pm\tfrac23, \pm\tfrac43,
        \pm\tfrac83$ --- and $-\tfrac23$ is on it.}
  \item A polynomial with integer coefficients has $\tfrac{3}{5}$ as a zero. What is the smallest
        possible value its leading coefficient could have, and why?

        \ans{$5$. The fraction is already in lowest terms, so its denominator $5$ must divide the
        leading coefficient; the smallest positive multiple of $5$ is $5$ itself --- for example
        $5x-3$.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{extensionbox}

\begin{spiralbox}
\textbf{Coming next --- Lesson 3.5: The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra \& Complex Zeros.} Twice today
a cubic gave up only one or two real solutions and then stalled: $x^3-2x^2-3x+6$ needed
$\pm\sqrt{3}$ to reach three, and a depressed polynomial like $x^2+4$ has no real solutions at all.
Tomorrow you find out that this is not a shortage --- a degree-$n$ polynomial has \emph{exactly} $n$
solutions once imaginary numbers are allowed, they arrive in conjugate pairs, and you can run the
whole process backward to build a polynomial from a list of zeros.
\end{spiralbox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{What to grade for.} Problem 1 is the whole lesson in miniature and is worth more than its
size suggests --- 1(c) is the only place students meet a leading coefficient with four divisors, and
the twelve-candidate answer requires reducing $\tfrac26$ to $\tfrac13$ and discarding $\tfrac22$.
Problems 2--4 are the workflow at three difficulty levels; if a student can do 3 they can do 2.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Problem 5 is the loop closing.} That polynomial is Lesson~3.3's exit ticket, where the
divisor was handed over. The point is not the answer --- students already know it --- but that they
can now generate the divisor themselves. Part (c) is the A2.EI.6d verification clause: an answer is
checked by substitution \emph{and} against the graph.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Problems 6 and 7(b) are the honest limits} and set up Lesson~3.5. Expect 7(b) to be the most
frequently missed item on the page: students are trained to treat an empty result as an error. Read
one or two responses aloud at the start of the next class.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Extension (a)} is a genuine modeling item --- the domain restriction $x>1$ comes from the
dimension $(x-1)$, not from the algebra, and students who skip it have factored without
interpreting. Extension (d) tests the theorem as structure rather than procedure, and is the single
best one-question check of whether a student understands where $q$ comes from.
\end{teachernote}
