\begin{notesbox}{Practice}
The degree tells you how many zeros to find. Conjugate pairs tell you that imaginary zeros never come
alone. Count every answer against the degree before you move on.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=8pt]
  \item \textbf{Just count.} Each polynomial is already factored. Give the degree and the zeros
    counted with multiplicity.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item $f(x) = (x-7)(x+2)^3$: \; degree \ans{$4$}, zeros \ans{$7, -2, -2, -2$}
      \item $g(x) = (x^2+1)(x-5)^2$: \; degree \ans{$4$}, zeros \ans{$i, -i, 5, 5$}
      \item $h(x) = (x^2+4)^2(x-1)$: \; degree \ans{$5$}, zeros \ans{$2i, 2i, -2i, -2i, 1$}

        \vspace{2pt}
        In (c), how many of those zeros are imaginary? \ans{$4$} \; Is that number even?
        \ans{yes}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Number and type, without solving.} Each polynomial has \emph{real} coefficients.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item Degree $3$; its graph passes straight through the $x$-axis exactly once. \;
        Real: \ans{$1$} \; Imaginary: \ans{$2$}
      \item Degree $4$; its graph \emph{touches} the $x$-axis at $x=3$ and meets it nowhere else. \;
        Real: \ans{$2$} \; Imaginary: \ans{$2$}
      \item Degree $5$; its graph passes straight through at $x=0$, $x=2$, and $x=-4$, and nowhere
        else. \; Real: \ans{$3$} \; Imaginary: \ans{$2$}
      \item Degree $6$; its graph never touches the $x$-axis. \; Real: \ans{$0$} \;
        Imaginary: \ans{$6$}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Solve over $\mathbb{C}$.} $x^3 + 2x^2 + 4x + 8 = 0$ \; (this is the quotient
    Lesson 3.3's homework produced when you divided $x^4-16$ by $x-2$ --- now finish it).
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item Factored form (grouping works): \ans{$x^2(x+2)+4(x+2) = (x+2)(x^2+4)$}
      \item All solutions: \ans{$-2,\ 2i,\ -2i$}
      \item Number and type: \ans{$1$} real, \ans{$2$} imaginary
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Solve over $\mathbb{C}$ --- this one needs the full workflow.}
    $x^3 - 7x^2 + 17x - 15 = 0$
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item Candidates: \ans{$\pm1, \pm3, \pm5, \pm15$} \; One that works: $r = $ \ans{$3$}
      \item Depressed polynomial: \ans{$x^2-4x+5$} \; Its discriminant: \ans{$16-20=-4$}
      \item All solutions: \ans{$3,\ 2+i,\ 2-i$}
      \item \textbf{Verify} one of the imaginary solutions is paired correctly, and explain how the
        degree confirms you are finished. \ans{$2+i$ and $2-i$ are conjugates \checkmark; degree $3$
        promises exactly $3$ zeros and $1+2=3$.}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Closing a loop.} Solve $x^4 - 16 = 0$ completely.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item Factor as a difference of squares --- \emph{twice} if you can:
        \ans{$(x^2-4)(x^2+4) = (x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4)$}
      \item All four solutions: \ans{$2,\ -2,\ 2i,\ -2i$}
      \item In Lesson 3.2 you would have said $x^2+4$ is prime and stopped. Is it still prime over
        the \emph{complex} numbers? \ans{no} \; Factor it: \ans{$(x-2i)(x+2i)$}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Build them.} Write each polynomial in factored form \emph{and} standard form.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item Zeros $3$ (multiplicity $2$) and $-1$. \; Factored: \ans{$(x-3)^2(x+1)$} \;
        Standard: \ans{$x^3-5x^2+3x+9$}
      \item Real coefficients; zeros include $-4$ and $2i$. \; Full zero list: \ans{$-4, 2i, -2i$}

        \vspace{2pt}
        Factored: \ans{$(x+4)(x^2+4)$} \; Standard: \ans{$x^3+4x^2+4x+16$}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Deduce the missing one.} A polynomial of degree $5$ with real coefficients has zeros
    $3$, $-1$ (multiplicity $2$), and $2-i$.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item How many zeros have you been given, counted with multiplicity? \ans{$4$}
      \item What is the fifth zero, and how do you know? \ans{$2+i$ --- the conjugate of $2-i$, which
        real coefficients require}
      \item How many $x$-intercepts does the graph of this function have? \ans{$2$ (at $3$ and $-1$)}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Explain.} Answer each in one or two sentences.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item Why must every polynomial of \emph{odd} degree with real coefficients have at least one
        real zero? Give both reasons --- the conjugate-pair one and the end-behavior one.
        \ansline{Imaginary zeros come in pairs, so their number is even; an odd degree minus an even number is odd, so at least one real zero is left.}
        \ansline{And an odd-degree graph's arms point in opposite directions, so it must pass through the $x$-axis at least once.}
      \item A classmate solves a cubic, finds one real solution, and says ``the other two are
        missing.'' Rewrite their sentence so it is correct, and say what a graph can and cannot show.
        \ansline{``The other two are imaginary'' --- nothing is missing; a cubic always has exactly three complex zeros.}
        \ansline{A graph shows every real zero as an $x$-intercept, but it can never show an imaginary zero.}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{extensionbox}
\textbf{Design and justify.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Show that for any real numbers $a$ and $b$, the product
        $\big(x-(a+bi)\big)\big(x-(a-bi)\big)$ has real coefficients, and state what it equals.
        (Group the factors as $(x-a) \mp bi$ first.)

        \ans{$\big((x-a)-bi\big)\big((x-a)+bi\big) = (x-a)^2 - b^2i^2 = (x-a)^2 + b^2 = x^2 - 2ax + (a^2+b^2)$. Since $a,b$ are real, so are $2a$ and $a^2+b^2$.}
  \item What is the smallest possible degree of a polynomial with real coefficients whose zeros
        include $0$, $\sqrt{5}$, and $3-2i$? Explain which zeros do and do not force a partner ---
        and be careful, because only one of these rules applies to $\sqrt{5}$.

        \ans{Degree $4$. Only $3-2i$ forces a partner ($3+2i$); $0$ and $\sqrt{5}$ are \emph{real}
        numbers, and real coefficients place no pairing requirement on real zeros. (It is
        \emph{rational} coefficients that would force $-\sqrt{5}$ along --- a different hypothesis.)}
  \item Write a quartic with real coefficients whose graph has \emph{exactly two} $x$-intercepts,
        then a quartic whose graph has \emph{none}. Give each in factored form and justify the number
        of intercepts using the counting rules, not a graph.

        \ans{Two intercepts: $(x-1)(x+3)(x^2+1)$ --- two real zeros, one conjugate pair, $2+2=4$.
        None: $(x^2+1)(x^2+9)$ --- all four zeros imaginary ($\pm i, \pm 3i$), two full pairs, so no
        real zeros exist to be intercepts.}
  \item Explain why no polynomial with real coefficients and degree $4$ can have exactly \emph{three}
        $x$-intercepts, each a simple crossing, and no other zeros.

        \ans{Three simple crossings use three of the four zeros, leaving exactly one --- and a single
        leftover zero cannot be imaginary (they come in pairs), so it would have to be real, giving a
        fourth intercept or a repeated one. The description contradicts itself.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{extensionbox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Items 1--2 are the fastest diagnostic on the page} and are worth grading first. A student who
can count $(x^2+4)^2(x-1)$ to five zeros and read ``degree $6$, no intercepts $\Rightarrow$ six
imaginary zeros'' has the theorem. Item~2(b) is the one that separates: a \emph{touch} is a double
zero, so it uses two of the four slots and leaves two imaginary --- students who count the touch once
get $1$ real and $3$ imaginary, and the odd number should be their own red flag.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Item 5 closes the longest loop in the unit.} $x^4-16$ was divided in Lesson~3.3, its quotient
$x^3+2x^2+4x+8$ was factored to $(x+2)(x^2+4)$ there, and $x^2+4$ was declared prime in Lesson~3.2.
Part~(c) is where students find out that ``prime'' was always relative to a number system. If only
one item gets discussed at the start of Lesson~3.6, make it this one.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Extension (b) is deliberately a trap} and is the only place students meet the distinction
between real and rational coefficients. Many will pair $\sqrt5$ with $-\sqrt5$ and answer $5$. That is
the \emph{right instinct} applied to the wrong hypothesis --- worth full credit if the reasoning is
stated, and worth a minute of class time either way, since the irrational conjugate pairing is real
mathematics they will meet again.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Common errors.} $(x+4)(x-2i)$ left unpaired in 6(b); $(x+4)(x^2-4)$ from the $i^2$ sign slip;
$(x-3)^2(x+1)$ expanded as $x^3-5x^2+3x-9$ (constant sign); and, in item~4, students who find $r=3$
and then report only ``$x=3$'' because the depressed quadratic ``has no solutions.'' That last one is
the whole lesson, and it should be corrected in writing, not just marked wrong.
\end{teachernote}
