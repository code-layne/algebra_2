\begin{headlinebox}{forestbg}
\small\textbf{One Formula, Every Quadratic.} With your partner, always start in \textbf{standard form}
$ax^2+bx+c=0$, read $a,b,c$ \emph{with signs}, and compute the \textbf{discriminant} $b^2-4ac$ \emph{first}
--- it tells you what kind of answer to expect. Then run the formula and simplify.
\end{headlinebox}

\vspace{0.06in}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{1. Standard form, then $a,b,c$.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{3pt}
  \item $x^2-5x=-6 \Rightarrow$ \ans{$x^2-5x+6=0$} \hfill $a=\ans{$1$}\ b=\ans{$-5$}\ c=\ans{$6$}$
  \item $3x^2+2=7x \Rightarrow$ \ans{$3x^2-7x+2=0$} \hfill $a=\ans{$3$}\ b=\ans{$-7$}\ c=\ans{$2$}$
\end{itemize}

\textbf{2. Compute the discriminant $b^2-4ac$.}
\[ x^2-4x+1=0:\ \ans{$12$} \qquad x^2+2x+5=0:\ \ans{$-16$} \qquad x^2-6x+9=0:\ \ans{$0$} \]

\textbf{3. Classify the roots} from the discriminant (circle one for each).
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}c X@{}}
\textbf{$b^2-4ac$} & \textbf{Roots (circle one)} \\
\hline
$25$  & \ans{two real} \quad / \quad one repeated real \quad / \quad two complex \\
$0$   & two real \quad / \quad \ans{one repeated real} \quad / \quad two complex \\
$-9$  & two real \quad / \quad one repeated real \quad / \quad \ans{two complex} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching Proficiency},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{1. Solve with the formula.} Give simplest radical or $a+bi$ form.
\[ x^2-3x-10=0:\ x=\ans{$5,\ -2$} \qquad x^2-4x+1=0:\ x=\ans{$2\pm\sqrt{3}$} \]

\textbf{2. Complex cases.} The discriminant is negative --- keep both conjugate roots.
\[ x^2-2x+5=0:\ x=\ans{$1\pm2i$} \qquad 2x^2+3x+2=0:\ x=\ans{$-\dfrac{3}{4}\pm\dfrac{\sqrt{7}}{4}i$} \]

\textbf{3. Verify.} Substitute $x=5$ into $x^2-3x-10$ (from \#1) and show it gives $0$.
\ansline{$(5)^2-3(5)-10=25-15-10=0$. \checkmark}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier E --- Extension},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{1. Model (projectile).} A rocket's height is $h(t)=-16t^2+32t+20$ (feet, $t$ in seconds). Find when
it hits the ground ($h=0$) with the formula, and keep only the sensible root.
\[ t=\ans{$\dfrac{-32\pm48}{-32}=2.5,\ -0.5$} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad t=\ans{$2.5$}\text{ s} \]
\hfill{\small\itshape Reject $t=-0.5$ (time can't be negative).}

\textbf{2. Discriminant as a dial (parameter).}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{3pt}
  \item For what value(s) of $k$ does $x^2+kx+9=0$ have exactly \emph{one repeated} real root? \ans{$k=\pm6$ (since $k^2-36=0$)}
  \item For what values of $k$ does $x^2-4x+k=0$ have \emph{two complex} roots? \ans{$k>4$ (since $16-4k<0$)}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3. Verify a complex root.} Substitute $x=1+2i$ into $x^2-2x+5$ (Tier A \#2) and show it gives $0$.
\ansline{$(1+2i)^2-2(1+2i)+5=(-3+4i)-(2+4i)+5=0$. \checkmark}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{teachernote}
Tier R keeps students off the formula so they master the \emph{setup}: standard form, signed $a,b,c$, and
reading the discriminant's sign. Watch \#1 for sign flips moving terms across, and \#4 --- A has two
$x$-intercepts ($D>0$), C is tangent at the vertex ($D=0$), B floats above the axis ($D<0$). Tier A is the
solving core; insist on writing the discriminant before substituting, and on simplifying
($\frac{4\pm2\sqrt3}{2}=2\pm\sqrt3$, not left over $2$). In \#2, $2x^2+3x+2=0$ has $2a=4$ in the
denominator --- a common divide-only-part slip. Tier E \#1: applying the formula to
$-16t^2+32t+20=0$ directly gives $t=\frac{-32\pm48}{-32}$; students often prefer clearing signs to
$4t^2-8t-5=0$ first --- both give $t=2.5$ s. Tier E \#2 is the payoff: the discriminant is a \emph{dial}
that sets the root type. Tier E \#3 closes the loop back to Lesson 3.4 --- a complex root really does
satisfy the equation.
\end{teachernote}
