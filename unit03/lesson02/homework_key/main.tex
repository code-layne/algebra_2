\begin{notesbox}{Practice}
Factor \textbf{completely} every time. Take the GCF out first, then count the terms to choose your
tool --- and check each factor before you call a problem done.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=11pt]
  \item \textbf{GCF first.}
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item $18x^4 - 24x^3 + 30x^2 = $ \ans{$6x^2(3x^2 - 4x + 5)$}
      \item $14x^3y - 21x^2y^2 = $ \ans{$7x^2y(2x - 3y)$}
      \item Factor out $-5x$: \; $-5x^3 + 20x^2 - 35x = $ \ans{$-5x(x^2 - 4x + 7)$}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Two terms.} Name the pattern, then factor.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item $25x^2 - 81 = $ \ans{$(5x-9)(5x+9)$}
      \item $x^3 + 64 = $ \ans{$(x+4)(x^2-4x+16)$}
      \item $8x^3 - 125y^3 = $ \ans{$(2x-5y)(4x^2+10xy+25y^2)$}
      \item $x^2 + 49 = $ \ans{prime --- a sum of squares}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Three terms.}
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item $x^2 + 16x + 64 = $ \ans{$(x+8)^2$}
      \item $x^2 - 2xy - 15y^2 = $ \ans{$(x-5y)(x+3y)$}
      \item $2x^2 + 11x + 12 = $ \ans{$(2x+3)(x+4)$} \quad (grouping: product $24$, sum $11$)
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Four terms --- grouping.}
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item $x^3 + 3x^2 + 7x + 21 = $ \ans{$(x+3)(x^2+7)$}
      \item $x^3 - 4x^2 - 9x + 36 = $ \ans{$(x-4)(x-3)(x+3)$} \quad (this one keeps going!)
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Factor completely --- more than one step.}
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item $2x^4 - 32 = $ \ans{$2(x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4)$}
      \item $x^4 - 10x^2 + 9 = $ \ans{$(x-1)(x+1)(x-3)(x+3)$}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Back to a graph you already read.} In Lesson 3.0 you read the graph of
        $q(x) = x^4 - 5x^2 + 4$ and found four $x$-intercepts.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item Factor $q(x)$ completely: \ans{$(x-1)(x+1)(x-2)(x+2)$}
      \item List the zeros: \ans{$1,\ -1,\ 2,\ -2$}
      \item Substitute $x = 0$ into your factored form to find the $y$-intercept: \ans{$4$}
            Does it agree with the graph? \ans{Yes}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Explain.} $x^2 + 9$ cannot be factored, but $x^3 + 27$ can. Explain the difference,
        and include the factorization of $x^3+27$ as part of your answer. \ansline{A \emph{sum of squares} has no integer factorization: factoring $x^2+9$ would need two integers with product $9$ and sum $0$, and no such pair exists. Cubes are different --- the identity $a^3+b^3=(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2)$ works because the four middle products cancel in pairs, so $x^3+27=(x+3)(x^2-3x+9)$. ``Sums never factor'' is a false rule; it is true for squares only.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{extensionbox}
\textbf{Prove it, then use it.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Multiply $(a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)$ out completely --- all six products --- and show which four
        cancel. What is left? \ans{$a^3 - a^2b + ab^2 + a^2b - ab^2 + b^3$; the $\pm a^2b$ pair and the $\pm ab^2$ pair cancel, leaving $a^3+b^3$.}
  \item A solid cube of ice measures $x$ cm on each edge. A cubical hole $3$ cm on each edge is
        carved out of one corner. Write the remaining volume as a difference of two cubes, then
        factor it. \ans{$V = x^3 - 27 = (x-3)(x^2+3x+9)$}
  \item Evaluate your factored expression at $x = 5$ cm, and check it against
        $5^3 - 3^3$. Do they agree? \ans{$(5-3)(25+15+9) = 2 \cdot 49 = 98$, and $125-27=98$ --- yes.}
  \item The factor $(x - 3)$ is the difference of the two edge lengths. Explain why it makes sense
        that the volume must contain that factor --- what happens to the leftover ice when $x = 3$?
        \ansline{At $x=3$ the hole is the whole cube, so no ice is left and $V=0$. Any expression for $V$ must therefore be zero at $x=3$, which is exactly what the factor $(x-3)$ guarantees.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{extensionbox}

\begin{spiralbox}
\textbf{Coming next --- Lesson 3.3: Dividing Polynomials.} Today's toolkit is powerful but it has a
boundary: nothing you learned cracks $x^3 - 3x + 2$, even though you know from Lesson 3.0 that it
equals $(x-1)^2(x+2)$. Next you will learn to \emph{divide} one polynomial by another --- long
division and the faster synthetic division --- and the \textbf{Remainder} and \textbf{Factor
Theorems}, which let you test whether $(x-r)$ is a factor before you divide. That is the tool that
gets past today's wall.
\end{spiralbox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Grading focus.} The problems that carry the standard are 2(d), 4(b), 5, 6, and 7 --- every
one of them tests \emph{completely} or \emph{prime}, not method. Items 1(a) and 1(c) leave a prime
quadratic behind on purpose ($3x^2-4x+5$ and $x^2-4x+7$ have negative discriminants), so students who
try to force a factorization there need the reassurance that ``stop'' is sometimes the right answer.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Item 6 is the whole unit in one problem:} it closes a loop that opened in Lesson~3.0 (read the
graph), ran through 3.1 (multiply forward), and lands here (factor backward). Part (c) is the check
worth pointing at in the debrief --- substituting $x=0$ into the \emph{factored} form gives
$(-1)(1)(-2)(2)=4$, the $y$-intercept students already read off the graph. \textbf{Item 7} is the one
to read for reasoning rather than answer: the common wrong explanation is ``sums never factor,'' and
the cube identity is the counterexample.
\end{teachernote}
