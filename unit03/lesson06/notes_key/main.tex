\begin{objectivebox}
\textbf{By the end of this lesson, I will be able to\dots}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item predict every feature of a polynomial's graph from its equation --- end behavior, $x$- and $y$-intercepts, the behavior at each zero, the number of turning points, and where the graph is above or below the $x$-axis --- and match that prediction to the correct graph;
  \item read a polynomial graph the other direction: write a possible equation in factored form from its $x$-intercepts, its behavior at each one, and its $y$-intercept;
  \item state a polynomial's domain, range, increasing/decreasing intervals, and relative vs.\ absolute extrema from its graph;
  \item build a polynomial model, restrict it to the inputs that make sense, and answer a real question with the feature that matches it.
\end{itemize}
\end{objectivebox}

\begin{vocabbox}
\small
Fill in each term as we build it together.
\vspace{2pt}

\termblanklong{Sign chart}
\ansline{A table that tests one $x$-value in each interval between consecutive zeros and records only the sign of $f(x)$ --- where the graph is above or below the axis.}
\termblanklong{Behavior at a zero (cross / touch / flatten)}
\ansline{Odd multiplicity: the graph crosses. Even multiplicity: it touches and turns back. Multiplicity $3$ or more: it flattens, then crosses.}
\termblanklong{Turning point}
\ansline{A point where the graph changes from increasing to decreasing or back; a degree-$n$ polynomial has at most $n-1$ of them.}
\termblanklong{Relative extremum vs.\ absolute extremum}
\ansline{A relative (local) max/min is the highest or lowest point \emph{nearby}; an absolute (global) one is highest or lowest on the whole graph.}
\termblanklong{Feasible domain (of a model)}
\ansline{The inputs that make sense in the real situation --- usually far smaller than the polynomial's own domain of all real numbers.}
\end{vocabbox}

\begin{notesbox}{1. The Five-Step Graph Plan}
Nothing in this lesson is new. Every step is a tool from an earlier lesson; today they run in order.

\vspace{4pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.45}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{|C{0.7cm}|X|C{2.1cm}|X|}
\hline
\rowcolor{forestbg}
\textbf{\#} & \textbf{What you do} & \textbf{From} & \textbf{What it decides about the picture} \\ \hline
1 & Find the degree and the leading coefficient. & 3.0, 3.1 & \ans{which way both arms point} \\ \hline
2 & Factor completely; set each factor to $0$. & 3.2--3.4 & \ans{where the graph meets the $x$-axis} \\ \hline
3 & Record the \textbf{multiplicity} of each zero. & 3.0 & \ans{whether it crosses, touches, or flattens there} \\ \hline
4 & Evaluate $f(0)$. & --- & \ans{the $y$-intercept --- one exact point} \\ \hline
5 & Test one $x$ in each interval between zeros. & new today & \ans{above or below the axis on every stretch} \\ \hline
\end{tabularx}

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Two rules to have in front of you.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Behavior at a zero.} Odd multiplicity $\Rightarrow$ the graph \ans{crosses} the
        axis. Even multiplicity $\Rightarrow$ the graph \ans{touches} the axis and turns back.
        Multiplicity $3$ or more $\Rightarrow$ it still crosses, but it \ans{flattens} out first.
  \item \textbf{Turning points.} A polynomial of degree $n$ has \emph{at most} \ans{$n-1$}
        turning points. Run that backward: a graph with $4$ turning points must have degree at
        least \ans{$5$}.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{What the plan cannot give you.} It locates every zero exactly and every arm exactly, but not
the \emph{coordinates} of the turning points --- those need a table of values or technology. Knowing
that a peak is somewhere between two zeros is enough for everything on this page.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{2. Worked Example A --- the Unit Anchor}
Analyze $g(x) = x^3 - 3x + 2 = (x-1)^2(x+2)$ completely, then check it against Graph 1 above.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Step 1 --- arms.} Degree \ans{$3$}, leading coefficient \ans{$1$}. Odd degree,
positive lead: as $x \to -\infty$, $g(x) \to$ \ans{$-\infty$}; as $x \to +\infty$, $g(x) \to$
\ans{$+\infty$}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Steps 2--3 --- zeros and behavior.} $x = $ \ans{$1$} (multiplicity \ans{$2$}) $\Rightarrow$
the graph \ans{touches}; \quad $x = $ \ans{$-2$} (multiplicity \ans{$1$}) $\Rightarrow$ the
graph \ans{crosses}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Step 4 --- $y$-intercept.} $g(0) = $ \ans{$2$} \; $\Rightarrow$ \; the point
\ans{$(0,2)$}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Step 5 --- sign chart.} The zeros $-2$ and $1$ split the axis into three intervals.

\vspace{2pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{|C{2.6cm}|C{1.6cm}|C{3.4cm}|C{2.2cm}|X|}
\hline
\rowcolor{forestbg}
\textbf{Interval} & \textbf{Test $x$} & \textbf{$(x-1)^2(x+2)$} & \textbf{Sign} & \textbf{Above / below} \\ \hline
$x<-2$     & $-3$ & \ans{$(-4)^2(-1)=-16$} & \ans{$-$} & \ans{below} \\ \hline
$-2<x<1$   & $0$  & \ans{$(-1)^2(2)=2$}    & \ans{$+$} & \ans{above} \\ \hline
$x>1$      & $2$  & \ans{$(1)^2(4)=4$}     & \ans{$+$} & \ans{above} \\ \hline
\end{tabularx}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Assemble it.} The graph comes up from $-\infty$ on the left, crosses at \ans{$x=-2$}, stays
\ans{above} the axis all the way to $x=1$, \ans{touches} the axis there without crossing, and
leaves upward. Turning points visible: \ans{$2$} --- and the degree allows at most
\ans{$2$}. \checkmark

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Notice the trap the sign chart catches.} Between $-2$ and $1$ the graph is above the axis,
and \emph{after} $1$ it is above the axis again. The sign did \ans{not} change at $x=1$ --- which
is exactly what an even multiplicity means.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{3. Worked Example B --- a Negative Lead and a Flattened Zero}
Analyze $q(x) = -(x+2)(x-1)^3$ before you look at the graph.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{1. Arms.} Degree \ans{$4$} (degrees add: $1 + 3$), leading coefficient \ans{$-1$}.
So as $x \to -\infty$, $q(x) \to$ \ans{$-\infty$}, and as $x \to +\infty$, $q(x) \to$ \ans{$-\infty$}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{2--3. Zeros.} $x = $ \ans{$-2$} (multiplicity \ans{$1$}), behavior: \ans{crosses};
\quad $x = $ \ans{$1$} (multiplicity \ans{$3$}), behavior: \ans{flattens, then crosses}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{4. $y$-intercept.} $q(0) = -(2)(-1)^3 = $ \ans{$2$}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{5. Sign chart.} \quad
$x<-2$: test $-3$, sign \ans{$-$} \quad $-2<x<1$: test $0$, sign \ans{$+$} \quad
$x>1$: test $2$, sign \ans{$-$}

\vspace{4pt}
Now check yourself against the real graph. (Each horizontal gridline is $4$ units.)
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.5]
  \lgrid{-3}{3}{-3}{3}
  \begin{scope}
    \clip (-3,-3) rectangle (3,3);
    \draw[very thick, forest, domain=-2.4:2.0, samples=170, smooth]
      plot (\x,{(0 - (\x)*(\x)*(\x)*(\x) + (\x)*(\x)*(\x) + 3*(\x)*(\x) - 5*(\x) + 2)/4});
  \end{scope}
  \foreach \x in {-2,-1,1,2}
    \draw[charcoal] (\x,-0.15)--(\x,0.15) node[below=1pt, font=\tiny]{$\x$};
  \fill[charcoal] (-2,0) circle (2.5pt);
  \fill[charcoal] (1,0)  circle (2.5pt);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item Both arms point \ans{down}, as an even degree with a negative lead requires.
  \item At $x=1$ the graph does something a simple crossing never does: it \ans{flattens} before
        passing through. That is the fingerprint of multiplicity \ans{$3$}.
  \item How many turning points does the graph have? \ans{$1$} \; The degree allows at most
        \ans{$3$}, so this is legal.
\end{itemize}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{4. Running It Backward: From a Graph to an Equation}
This is Lesson 3.5's build-from-zeros skill (A2.EI.6a) with the zeros read off a picture instead of
handed to you. (Each horizontal gridline is $2$ units.)
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.5]
  \lgrid{-3}{3}{-3}{4}
  \begin{scope}
    \clip (-3,-3) rectangle (3,4);
    \draw[very thick, forest, domain=-2.3:2.3, samples=150, smooth]
      plot (\x,{(0 - (\x)*(\x)*(\x) + 3*(\x) + 2)/2});
  \end{scope}
  \foreach \x in {-2,-1,1,2}
    \draw[charcoal] (\x,-0.15)--(\x,0.15) node[below=1pt, font=\tiny]{$\x$};
  \fill[charcoal] (-1,0) circle (2.5pt);
  \fill[charcoal] (2,0)  circle (2.5pt);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item $x$-intercepts: \ans{$x=-1$ and $x=2$}. \; At $x=-1$ the graph \ans{touches} the axis, so that
        factor is squared; at $x=2$ it \ans{crosses}, so that factor appears once.
  \item Skeleton: $f(x) = a\,($\ans{$x+1$}$)^2\,($\ans{$x-2$}$)$. \; Its degree is
        \ans{$3$}.
  \item \textbf{Find $a$ with the $y$-intercept.} The graph passes through $(0,$\ans{$2$}$)$, so
        $f(0) = a(1)^2(-2) = $ \ans{$2$} $\Rightarrow a = $ \ans{$-1$}.
  \item Final answer: $f(x) = $ \ans{$-(x+1)^2(x-2)$, i.e. $-x^3+3x+2$}
  \item \textbf{Check the arms.} Degree \ans{$3$}, leading coefficient \ans{$-1$}: up on the
        left, down on the right. Does the graph agree? \ans{yes}
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Why the answer is only ``a possible equation.''} A graph cannot show you an imaginary zero
(Lesson 3.5), so $f(x)$ could also carry a factor like $x^2+1$ that no picture would reveal. Say
\emph{least degree} when you want the one answer above.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{5. Reading the Finished Graph: All the Characteristics}
Here is $p(x) = x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 = (x-1)(x+1)(x-2)(x+2)$ --- the quartic you factored in Lesson 3.2.
(Each horizontal gridline is $2$ units.)
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.5]
  \lgrid{-3}{3}{-3}{4}
  \begin{scope}
    \clip (-3,-3) rectangle (3,4);
    \draw[very thick, forest, domain=-2.35:2.35, samples=170, smooth]
      plot (\x,{((\x)*(\x)*(\x)*(\x) - 5*(\x)*(\x) + 4)/2});
  \end{scope}
  \foreach \x in {-2,-1,1,2}
    \draw[charcoal] (\x,-0.15)--(\x,0.15) node[below=1pt, font=\tiny]{$\x$};
  \foreach \p in {-2,-1,1,2} \fill[charcoal] (\p,0) circle (2.5pt);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Domain:} \ans{all reals, $(-\infty,\infty)$} \quad (every polynomial has the same domain --- why?
        \ans{no denominators or radicals to break})
  \item \textbf{Range:} \ans{about $[-2.25,\infty)$} \; The lowest the graph ever goes is about $y=-2.25$, and both
        arms rise forever.
  \item \textbf{Zeros / $x$-intercepts:} \ans{$-2,-1,1,2$} \qquad \textbf{$y$-intercept:} \ans{$(0,4)$}
  \item \textbf{Turning points:} \ans{$3$}, which is the most a degree-\ans{$4$} polynomial
        may have.
  \item \textbf{Relative maximum} at about $($\ans{$0,4$}$)$. It is \emph{relative} --- not
        absolute --- because \ans{the arms climb past $y=4$ farther out}.
  \item \textbf{Absolute minimum} value about \ans{$-2.25$}, reached at two different $x$-values,
        about $x \approx \pm 1.6$. There is \ans{no} absolute maximum, because the arms
        \ans{rise without bound}.
  \item \textbf{Increasing:} about \ans{$(-1.6,0) \cup (1.6,\infty)$} \quad \textbf{Decreasing:} about \ans{$(-\infty,-1.6) \cup (0,1.6)$}
  \item \textbf{Positive} ($p(x)>0$) on \ans{$(-\infty,-2) \cup (-1,1) \cup (2,\infty)$}; \textbf{negative} on \ans{$(-2,-1) \cup (1,2)$}.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Even-degree vs.\ odd-degree, one more time.} A polynomial of odd degree has \ans{no}
absolute extrema at all, because its arms run in opposite directions. Only an \emph{even} degree can
have one --- and then only one of the two.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{6. Modeling: Where a Polynomial Comes From}
A $10$-inch by $8$-inch sheet of cardboard has a square of side $x$ cut from each corner; the flaps
fold up to make an open box.

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X c@{}}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.60\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Build the model.} After the cuts, the base measures
\ans{$10-2x$} by \ans{$8-2x$}, and the height is \ans{$x$}.

\vspace{3pt}
$V(x) = $ \ans{$x(10-2x)(8-2x)$}

\vspace{3pt}
Expanded (Lesson 3.1): $V(x) = 4x^3 - 36x^2 + 80x$. Degree
\ans{$3$}.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Feasible domain.} The zeros of $V$ are $x = $ \ans{$0,\,4,\,5$}.
Which of them could actually be a corner cut? \ans{none}

\vspace{2pt}
A cut must be positive, and it cannot take more than half of the
\ans{$8$-inch} side. So the model only makes sense for
\ans{$0<x<4$} --- the \textbf{feasible domain}.
\end{minipage}
&
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.36\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}\centering
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\small
\begin{tabular}{|C{1.1cm}|C{2.2cm}|}
\hline
\rowcolor{forestbg}
$x$ & $V(x)$ \\ \hline
$0.5$ & $31.5$ \\ \hline
$1$   & \ans{$48$} \\ \hline
$1.5$ & \ans{$52.5$} \\ \hline
$2$   & $48$ \\ \hline
$2.5$ & $37.5$ \\ \hline
$3$   & $24$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{minipage}
\end{tabularx}

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item Fill in the two missing table values. The largest volume in the table is about \ans{$52.5$}
        cubic inches, at $x \approx$ \ans{$1.5$} inches.
  \item That point is a \textbf{relative maximum} of $V$. On the feasible domain it is also the
        \ans{absolute} maximum --- which is why it answers the question ``what is the biggest box?''
  \item \textbf{Interpret (A2.F.2f).} $V(2.5) = 37.5$ means: \ans{cutting $2.5$-inch corner squares
        gives a box holding $37.5$ cubic inches}
  \item Outside the feasible domain the polynomial keeps going --- $V(5) = 0$ and $V(6) = 48$ --- but
        those outputs describe \ans{no real box (negative side lengths)}. The \emph{algebra} does not know about cardboard; you
        do.
\end{itemize}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{practicebox}
\textbf{A. Five steps, one function.} $f(x) = (x+3)(x-2)^2$
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Degree \ans{$3$}; leading coefficient \ans{$1$}; end behavior: down-left and
        \ans{up-right}.
  \item Zeros and behavior: $x=$ \ans{$-3$} (\ans{crosses}), \; $x=$ \ans{$2$}
        (\ans{touches}). \quad $y$-intercept: \ans{$(0,12)$}
  \item Sign of $f$ on $x<-3$: \ans{$-$}; on $-3<x<2$: \ans{$+$}; on $x>2$: \ans{$+$}
  \item So the graph is below the axis only when \ans{$x<-3$}, and it \ans{touches} the axis at
        $x=2$ without ever going below it again.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{B. Backward.} A cubic's graph crosses the $x$-axis at $x=-1$, touches it at $x=3$, and passes
through $(0,-9)$.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em, itemsep=5pt, start=5]
  \item Skeleton with $a$: \ans{$f(x)=a(x+1)(x-3)^2$}
  \item Solve for $a$: \ans{$9a=-9$, so $a=-1$} \quad Final equation: \ans{$f(x)=-(x+1)(x-3)^2$}
\end{enumerate}
\end{practicebox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Pacing.} Sections 1--3 are the core and should take roughly half the period; Section 4 is
short if Lesson 3.5's build-from-zeros work landed; Sections 5--6 can share the remaining time, with
Section 6 protected --- it is the only place A2.F.2f (interpreting $f(x)$ in context) is taught.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{The hook is the lesson in one picture.} Two graphs, identical zeros, completely different
shapes. Do not let students answer ``Graph 1'' by elimination on the $y$-intercept alone --- push
them to say \emph{touch at $1$}. Multiplicity is the feature that separates the two, and it is the
feature most often skipped when students graph.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Errors to expect.} (1) Sign chart done by plugging into the \emph{expanded} form --- slow and
error-prone; insist on the factored form, where only signs matter. (2) The belief that the graph must
alternate above/below at every zero; the anchor's double zero at $x=1$ is the counterexample and it
is on the page. (3) End behavior read off the \emph{constant} term. (4) In Section 4, solving for $a$
with an $x$-intercept instead of the $y$-intercept (which gives $0=0$ and no information). (5) In
Section 6, giving $x=4$ or $x=5$ as an answer because it is a zero of $V$ --- the feasible-domain beat
exists to kill exactly that.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Say this out loud in Section 5.} An odd-degree polynomial has \emph{no} absolute extrema, and
an even-degree one has only an absolute min (positive lead) or only an absolute max (negative lead).
Students who learned ``the vertex is the max'' from Unit 2 need this stated directly, because a
quartic has a relative max that is not the highest point on the graph --- which is exactly what the
$(0,4)$ point demonstrates here.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Bridge to the activity.} Tier R repeats the plan on already-factored polynomials and matches
them to graphs; Tier A makes students factor first and read an equation off a graph; Tier E returns
to the box model with a \emph{target} volume, which turns into a cubic equation solved with the
Rational Root Theorem --- Lessons 3.2--3.4 arriving in a modeling problem.
\end{teachernote}
