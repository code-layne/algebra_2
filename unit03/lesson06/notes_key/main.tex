\begin{notesbox}{Round 1 --- Counting Zeros: the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra}
\small
\textbf{Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.} A polynomial of degree $n\ge1$ has exactly $n$
\textbf{complex zeros}, counted with multiplicity --- the real zeros and the imaginary zeros
together.
\textbf{Conjugate pairs.} If a polynomial has real coefficients and $a+bi$ is a zero, then its
\textbf{conjugate} $a-bi$ is also a zero, so imaginary zeros always come in pairs.
\textbf{Reading a graph.} Each real zero is an $x$-intercept (a touch counts twice); imaginary
zeros never show on the graph, so the number of imaginary zeros is the degree minus the number
of real zeros.

\vspace{6pt}
\noindent
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.63\linewidth}
\textbf{Example.} The graph of $f(x)=x^4-x^3+2x^2-4x-8$ is shown, and $2i$ is one of its zeros.
How many zeros does $f$ have? How many are real, and how many imaginary? Name the imaginary ones.
\begin{work}
  \text{zeros} &= 4 \quad \text{degree } 4 \text{ (FTA)} \\
  \text{real} &= 2 \quad \text{crosses at } -1 \text{ and } 2 \\
  \text{imaginary} &= 4-2=2 \quad \text{degree minus real} \\
  \text{they are} &\ 2i,\ -2i \quad \text{a conjugate pair}
\end{work}
\end{minipage}\hfill

\begin{notesbox}{Round 2 --- Writing a Polynomial From Its Zeros}
\small
\textbf{Factored form.} If a polynomial of degree $n$ has zeros $r_1,r_2,\dots,r_n$ (counted
with multiplicity), then $f(x)=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)\cdots(x-r_n)$, where $a$ is the leading
coefficient. Each zero $r$ gives the factor $(x-r)$, so a zero at $x=3$ gives $(x-3)$ and a zero
at $x=-3$ gives $(x+3)$. With real coefficients, every imaginary zero brings its conjugate, and
the pair multiplies to a real quadratic: $(x-2i)(x+2i)=x^2+4$. To find $a$, substitute one more
point the polynomial passes through.

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{Example.} Write the polynomial of least degree with real coefficients whose zeros
include $3$ and $2i$ and whose graph passes through $(0,24)$. Give it in standard form.
\begin{work}
  f(x) &= a(x-3)(x-2i)(x+2i) && \text{one factor per zero; add the conjugate } -2i \\
       &= a(x-3)(x^2+4) && -4i^2=+4 \\
       &= a(x^3-3x^2+4x-12) && \text{distribute} \\
  24 &= a(0-0+0-12) && \text{substitute } (0,24) \\
  a &= -2 && \text{divide by } -12 \\
  f(x) &= -2x^3+6x^2-8x+24 && \text{distribute the } -2
\end{work}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Your turn.} \textit{Start at \rung{1} and climb.} Standard form each time.

\begin{work}

\end{work}

\end{work}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{4pt}
\rung{3}\; The puzzle board in the dorm common room says: \emph{``I have real coefficients and
degree $3$. My zeros are $-1$ (twice) and $4$, and my graph crosses the $y$-axis at $8$. Who
am I?''} Write the polynomial in standard form.
\begin{work}
  f(x) &= a(x+1)^2(x-4) && \text{three zeros, } -1 \text{ twice} \\
  8 &= a(1)^2(0-4) && \text{substitute } (0,8) \\
  a &= -2 \\
  f(x) &= -2(x^2+2x+1)(x-4) \\
       &= -2(x^3-2x^2-7x-4) \\
       &= -2x^3+4x^2+14x+8
\end{work}

\textbf{Show me.} My answer: \ans{3: $(x-2)(x^2-2x+2)$}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{Round 3 --- Graphs From Zeros and End Behavior}
\small
\textbf{Reading a factored polynomial.} In $f(x)=a(x-r_1)^{m_1}(x-r_2)^{m_2}\cdots$, each factor
$(x-r)$ puts an $x$-intercept at $x=r$ --- the number that makes the factor zero, the
\emph{opposite} of the sign printed inside. An odd power \textbf{crosses} the $x$-axis; an even
power \textbf{touches} it and turns back. The \textbf{end behavior} comes from the degree and
the sign of $a$, and the \textbf{$y$-intercept} is $f(0)$. To \emph{match} an equation to a graph,
check these features one at a time. To \emph{write} an equation from a graph, turn each
intercept into a factor, square the factor at every touch, and use the $y$-intercept to find $a$.

\textbf{Example.} Which graph, A--D, is $f(x)=-(x+2)(x-1)^2$? Defend the match with features.
\begin{work}
  x &= -2 && \text{from } (x+2)\text{; power } 1\text{, so it crosses} \\
  x &= 1 && \text{from } (x-1)^2\text{; power } 2\text{, so it touches} \\
  \text{degree } 3,\ a &= -1 && \text{rises on the left, falls on the right} \\
  f(0) &= -(2)(-1)^2=-2 && y\text{-intercept} \\
  &\Rightarrow \text{Graph A} && \text{the only graph with all four}
\end{work}

\textbf{Bonus (+1).} A clue on the Math Night scavenger hunt reads: \emph{``I have real
coefficients and degree $3$. Two of my zeros are $2$ and $1+i$, and my graph passes through
$(0,8)$.''} Write the polynomial in standard form.
\begin{work}
  f(x) &= a(x-2)(x-1-i)(x-1+i) && \text{add the conjugate } 1-i \\
