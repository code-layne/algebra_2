\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=forestbg, colframe=forest, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students assemble every tool of Unit 3 into one procedure that runs
    in both directions. From an equation they predict the whole picture --- \textbf{end behavior}
    from the degree and leading coefficient, \textbf{$x$-intercepts} from the factors,
    \textbf{cross/touch/flatten} from each zero's multiplicity, the \textbf{$y$-intercept} from
    $f(0)$, the \textbf{turning-point limit} $n-1$, and \textbf{above or below} from a sign chart ---
    and then match that prediction to the correct graph. From a graph they run the same road backward
    to a factored equation, and they read a finished graph for domain, range, increasing/decreasing
    intervals, and relative vs.\ absolute extrema. Finally they build a polynomial \emph{model},
    restrict it to its \textbf{feasible domain}, and answer a real question with the feature that
    matches it.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Standards (2023 VA SOL):} \textbf{A2.F.2a} (domain, range, zeros, intercepts),
    \textbf{A2.F.2b} (contrasting characteristics across function families --- here, even vs.\ odd
    degree), \textbf{A2.F.2c} (increasing/decreasing intervals), \textbf{A2.F.2d} (absolute maxima
    and minima), \textbf{A2.F.2e} (relative maxima and minima), \textbf{A2.F.2f} (determine $f(x)$
    from a graph or equation and explain its meaning in context --- the modeling strand), and
    \textbf{A2.F.2g} (end behavior). \textbf{A2.EI.6a} (a factored form from the $x$-intercepts of a
    graph) drives the ``run it backward'' section. Builds on \textbf{A2.EO.3b} (factor completely,
    Lesson 3.2), \textbf{A2.EO.3c} and \textbf{A2.EI.6c} (division and the Rational Root Theorem,
    Lessons 3.3--3.4), and \textbf{A2.EI.6b} (what a graph can and cannot show, Lesson 3.5).
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Ideas \& Skills} & \textbf{Key Understandings (the ``why'')} \\[2pt]
\hline
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item Run the \textbf{five-step graph plan}: degree/lead $\rightarrow$ arms; factors $\rightarrow$ intercepts; multiplicity $\rightarrow$ cross/touch/flatten; $f(0)$ $\rightarrow$ one exact point; sign chart $\rightarrow$ above/below.
  \item Build a \textbf{sign chart} from the \emph{factored} form and read positive/negative intervals off it.
  \item Use the \textbf{turning-point limit}: at most $n-1$ turns; run backward, $k$ turns forces degree $\ge k+1$.
  \item \textbf{Match} an equation to the correct graph, and defend the match with a feature, not a feeling.
  \item \textbf{Write a possible equation} in factored form from a graph, solving for $a$ with the $y$-intercept (A2.EI.6a).
  \item Read \textbf{domain, range, increasing/decreasing, relative vs.\ absolute extrema} off a polynomial graph (A2.F.2a/c/d/e).
  \item Build a \textbf{model}, state its \textbf{feasible domain}, and interpret $V(x)$ and its maximum in context (A2.F.2f).
\end{itemize}
&
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item Nothing here is new. Every step is a Unit 3 tool; today they run in order and produce a picture.
  \item Zeros alone do not determine a graph. \textbf{Multiplicity} is what separates two graphs with identical intercepts.
  \item The sign chart is fast only in factored form --- you never need the value, only the sign.
  \item Sign changes at odd multiplicities and does \emph{not} change at even ones; the chart makes that automatic instead of memorized.
  \item The $y$-intercept is the only labeled point that is not a zero, which is exactly why it can pin down $a$.
  \item A graph proves a \emph{minimum} degree, never the exact degree: imaginary zeros (3.5) and even factors are invisible.
  \item An odd degree has no absolute extrema. A model's answer lives inside its feasible domain --- the algebra does not know about cardboard.
\end{itemize}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{sky}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\textbf{End behavior rule} & Even degree $\Rightarrow$ arms agree; odd degree $\Rightarrow$ arms oppose. The sign of the leading coefficient sets the right arm (Lesson 3.0). \\
\textbf{Multiplicity behavior} & Odd $\Rightarrow$ crosses (sign changes); even $\Rightarrow$ touches and turns back (no sign change); $\ge 3$ $\Rightarrow$ flattens, then crosses. \\
\textbf{Sign chart} & Test one $x$-value in each interval between consecutive zeros; record only the sign --- it says above or below the $x$-axis. \\
\textbf{Turning point} & Where the graph changes from increasing to decreasing or back. A degree-$n$ polynomial has at most $n-1$. \\
\textbf{Relative (local) extremum} & Highest or lowest \emph{nearby} --- a peak or valley (A2.F.2e). \\
\textbf{Absolute (global) extremum} & Highest or lowest on the \emph{whole} graph (A2.F.2d). Odd degree $\Rightarrow$ none at all. \\
\textbf{Feasible domain} & The inputs a model actually allows (positive lengths, no negative sides) --- usually far smaller than ``all reals.'' \\
\textbf{Possible equation} & What a graph earns you: the factored form of \emph{least} degree consistent with the picture. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{fixedskillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{forestbg}
    The Warm-Up hands students the three pieces this lesson assembles, and each piece is already
    theirs. \textbf{(1)} \emph{End behavior from factored form} (Lessons 3.0 and 3.1): degree and
    leading coefficient for $(x-1)^2(x+2)$, $-2x(x+3)^3$, and $-x^2(x-3)^3$ --- degrees add, leads
    multiply, no expanding. \textbf{(2)} \emph{Zeros and multiplicity} (Lesson 3.0) for the unit
    anchor $g(x)=x^3-3x+2=(x-1)^2(x+2)$, plus the $y$-intercept $g(0)=2$. \textbf{(3)} \emph{A first
    sign chart}, though it is not named yet: test $x=-3, 0, 2$ and record only the sign. The page
    closes on the hinge question --- with the arms, the intercepts, the behavior at each, and the
    sign in every interval, \emph{could you pick this graph out of a lineup?} Yes, except for how
    high the humps go. Debrief by assembling the three results into one description on the board;
    do not confirm the hinge, because the notes will.
\end{fixedskillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{forestbg}
    Put two cubics on the board: $\;(x-1)^2(x+2)\;$ and $\;(x-1)(x+2)^2$. Ask for the zeros of each.
    They are the \emph{same two numbers} --- and both are cubics with a positive leading coefficient,
    so even the arms match. Now show the two graphs (pre-drawn in the notes, side by side) and ask
    the question the lesson answers: \textbf{``The zeros are identical and the arms are identical.
    Why do these graphs look nothing alike?''} Take answers until someone says the double zero is in
    a different place. Name it: \textbf{multiplicity is the feature that separates look-alikes}, and
    it is invisible until you look at the exponents. Then make the promise: by the end of the period
    they will be able to describe a polynomial's entire graph from its equation without plotting a
    point, read an equation off a graph they have never seen, and use both to answer a question about
    a real object --- a box built from a sheet of cardboard.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{forestbg}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. Assemble the plan, do not teach it.} Build the five-step table \emph{from the students}:
what did Lesson 3.0 give us? (arms, and cross/touch). Lessons 3.2--3.4? (the zeros). What have we
never done? (decided above/below \emph{between} the zeros). That last gap is the only new content of
the day, and it takes one minute to fill: test a single $x$-value in each interval and keep the sign.
Insist the test be done in \emph{factored} form --- $g(-3) = (-4)^2(-1)$ is a ten-second calculation
and $(-3)^3-3(-3)+2$ is not.

\textbf{2. Worked Example A --- the anchor, one last time.} Run all five steps on
$g(x)=x^3-3x+2=(x-1)^2(x+2)$, the polynomial students have met in every lesson of this unit, and then
hold the prediction against the pre-drawn graph. The payoff line: \emph{the equation told us the
picture}. Emphasize the row of the sign chart where the sign fails to flip at $x=1$; that is the
even-multiplicity fingerprint the chart catches automatically.

\textbf{3. Worked Example B --- what a triple zero looks like.} $q(x)=-(x+2)(x-1)^3$ adds two new
features at once: an even degree with a negative lead (both arms down) and a multiplicity-3 zero
(flattens, then crosses). Have students predict all five steps \emph{before} the graph is revealed;
the flattening at $x=1$ is the moment worth waiting for.

\columnbreak

\textbf{4. Backward: graph $\rightarrow$ equation (A2.EI.6a).} Same road, other direction. Read the
intercepts, decide which factor is squared from cross vs.\ touch, write $f(x)=a(\cdots)$, and solve
for $a$ with the $y$-intercept. Ask why an $x$-intercept cannot be used ($0=0$, true for every $a$),
and close with the honesty clause from Lesson 3.5: a graph cannot show an imaginary zero, so the
answer is always a \emph{possible} equation of \emph{least} degree.

\textbf{5. Characteristics off the finished graph (A2.F.2a/c/d/e).} Use $p(x)=x^4-5x^2+4$ --- the
quartic factored in Lesson 3.2 --- for domain, range, increasing/decreasing, and the distinction that
matters most: the relative maximum at $(0,4)$ is \emph{not} an absolute maximum, because the arms
climb past it. State the general rule out loud: odd degree $\Rightarrow$ no absolute extrema; even
degree $\Rightarrow$ one or the other, never both. Unit 2 taught ``the vertex is the max''; this is
where that gets corrected.

\textbf{6. Modeling (A2.F.2f).} Build $V(x)=x(10-2x)(8-2x)$ from a $10\times 8$ sheet, expand it to
the cubic from Lesson 3.1, and then do the step students skip: find the \textbf{feasible domain}
$0<x<4$ and say why $x=5$ --- a genuine zero of $V$ --- is nonsense. Read the maximum from the table
($52.5$ in$^3$ near $x=1.5$), and name it: on the feasible domain, that relative maximum \emph{is}
the absolute maximum, which is why it answers the question. Finish by interpreting a single value:
$V(2.5)=37.5$ means a $2.5$-inch cut yields a $37.5$-cubic-inch box.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
Circulate during the notes practice and Tier work. Watch for and correct:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{degree counted as the number of factors} --- $-2(x+2)^3(x-1)$ called a cubic; make them add the exponents;
  \item \textbf{end behavior taken from the constant term} or from the sign of the $y$-intercept rather than the leading term;
  \item \textbf{``crosses'' written at every zero} --- the single most common error of the lesson, and the one the hook exists to prevent;
  \item \textbf{sign charts computed in expanded form} --- slow, arithmetic-heavy, and unnecessary;
  \item \textbf{signs forced to alternate} at every zero, so a double zero gets an invented sign change;
  \item \textbf{$a$ never solved for} when reading an equation off a graph (or solved for with an $x$-intercept, giving $0=0$);
  \item \textbf{relative called absolute} --- a peak on a quartic reported as the maximum of the function;
  \item \textbf{model answers outside the feasible domain} --- $x=5$ offered as a corner cut because it is a zero of $V$.
\end{itemize}
Cold-call prompts: ``Degree and leading coefficient --- which way do the arms point?'' ``Multiplicity
$2$: cross or touch? Show me the sign chart row that proves it.'' ``You tested $x=0$ and got a
negative. What does that tell me about the picture?'' ``How many turning points does the degree
allow? How many does this graph have?'' ``Which labeled point on that graph is \emph{not} a zero, and
why does that make it the useful one?'' ``Your box has a $5$-inch cut. Draw me that box.''
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\textbf{Predict, Then Look} activity (tiered; mirrors the notes).
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate.} Three cousins built from the same two numbers ---
$(x+1)(x-2)^2$, $-(x+1)(x-2)^2$, and $(x+1)^2(x-2)$ --- analyzed in a feature table (degree, lead,
arms, behavior at each zero, $y$-intercept), then \emph{matched} to three pre-drawn graphs. Students
must name the one feature that separates the first from the third. A guided sign chart follows, with
an explicit ``why did the sign not change at $x=2$?'' prompt, and the tier closes on the
turning-point limit for a quintic and why an odd degree must cross.

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching.} Two things Tier R does not require. First, \emph{factor before
analyzing}: $f(x)=x^3-x^2-6x$ needs a GCF (Lesson 3.2) before its three zeros appear, then a
four-interval sign chart, a check against the pre-drawn graph, and the A2.F.2a/c/d/e read --- domain,
range, relative extrema, and \emph{no} absolute extrema because the degree is odd. Second, read an
equation off a pre-drawn cubic with a touch at $x=1$, solving $a=-1$ from the $y$-intercept and
checking the sign against the arms. Closes with error analysis on the two signature mistakes: a touch
called a crossing, and turning points read as the degree.

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension.} \emph{Part 1:} the notes' box, run backward --- a customer wants
exactly $48$ in$^3$, which becomes $x^3-9x^2+20x-12=0$, solved with the Rational Root Theorem (3.4)
to $x=1,2,6$; students reject $6$ and explain how \emph{two} cuts give the same volume (they straddle
the maximum, and both appear in the notes table). \emph{Part 2:} build a graph to order --- touch at
$-1$, crossings at $0$ and $3$, both arms down --- which forces degree $4$ and a negative lead, and
asks why a cubic cannot do it. \emph{Part 3:} a graph with two crossings and three turning points ---
minimum degree $4$, hence two imaginary zeros (3.5) --- and the closing insight that a degree-$6$
polynomial could produce the same picture, so a graph proves only a \emph{minimum} degree.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (independent, no notes): \textbf{(1)} predict the graph of $f(x)=(x-3)(x+1)^2$
--- degree and leading coefficient, end behavior, both zeros with cross/touch, the $y$-intercept
$(0,-3)$, the turning-point limit, and one sign question that requires explaining why the sign does
\emph{not} change at the double zero $x=-1$; \textbf{(2)} read a pre-drawn cubic with intercepts
$-3$ and $2$ (a touch at $2$) and $y$-intercept $-12$, write $f(x)=-(x+3)(x-2)^2$ by solving for $a$,
and say whether the function has an absolute maximum (no --- odd degree); and \textbf{(3)} an
\textbf{SOL-style multiple-choice} item asking which function's graph touches the $x$-axis at $x=2$
and falls to $-\infty$ on both ends, with $-(x-2)^2(x+1)^2$ correct and three distractors that each
break exactly one requirement (odd degree, positive lead, multiplicity $3$ at $x=2$). Sort into ``got
it / end behavior wrong / multiplicity ignored / could not solve for $a$.'' The multiplicity pile is
the one to reteach on the spot: it is the feature that distinguishes graphs with identical intercepts
and it appears on the unit test in both directions.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} a prediction table for three factored polynomials (including a multiplicity-3
flatten and a negative lead of $-2$); a two-graph discrimination between $x^2(x-3)$ and $x(x-3)^2$;
a full five-step analysis with a four-interval sign chart; a factor-first problem ($x^3+2x^2-8x$);
reading an equation off a graph where $a=2$, so the stretch factor cannot be assumed to be $\pm1$;
a complete characteristics read of $p(x)=x^4-4x^2$ (including the subtlety that $p(x)<0$ on
$(-2,0)\cup(0,2)$, with $x=0$ excluded); a $12\times12$ box model with a table, a feasible domain, a
maximum of $128$ in$^3$ at $x=2$, and an interpretation item; and two written items --- fixing a
false ``sign changes at the touch'' claim, and explaining why a graph gives only a minimum degree.
\textbf{Extension:} build a quartic to order; decide whether $4$ intercepts with $2$ turning points is
possible; find a least degree and then count imaginary zeros; and solve $4x^3-48x^2+144x=108$ with the
Rational Root Theorem to find the \emph{second} feasible cut, $x\approx1.15$. \textbf{Preview:} the
Unit 3 test, then Unit 4 --- rational functions, where a polynomial lands in a denominator and
division by zero puts asymptotes on the graph.
\end{skillbox}
