\begin{notesbox}{1. What Division Even Means (the statement behind everything)}
Warm-Up item 1: $247 \div 5$ gave quotient $49$ and remainder $2$, which you wrote as
$247 = 5 \cdot 49 + 2$. Polynomials obey the exact same statement.
\[
  \underbrace{P(x)}_{\text{dividend}} \;=\; \underbrace{D(x)}_{\text{divisor}} \cdot
  \underbrace{Q(x)}_{\text{quotient}} \;+\; \underbrace{R(x)}_{\text{remainder}}
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \frac{P(x)}{D(x)} = Q(x) + \frac{R(x)}{D(x)}
\]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item You stop dividing when the degree of the remainder is \blank{3.2cm} the degree of the divisor.
  \item Every division can be \emph{checked} by \blank{4.6cm}.
  \item And the case we care most about: if $R = 0$, then $P(x) = D(x)\cdot Q(x)$, which says
        $D(x)$ is a \blank{2.0cm} of $P(x)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{2. Dividing by a Monomial: Split the Fraction}
A sum over a single term splits term by term: $\dfrac{a+b}{c} = \dfrac{a}{c} + \dfrac{b}{c}$.

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item $\dfrac{12x^4 - 8x^3 + 6x^2}{2x^2} = $ \blank{5.0cm}
  \item Two variables: \; $\dfrac{15x^3y^2 - 10x^2y^3 + 5x^2y^2}{5x^2y^2} = $ \blank{4.6cm}
\end{itemize}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{The trap.} In that second problem the last term divides out to \blank{1.0cm} --- \emph{not}
zero. A term that cancels completely leaves a \blank{2.6cm} behind.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{3. Long Division: When the Divisor Has More Than One Term}
\textbf{Setup rules.} Write both polynomials in \blank{3.0cm} form (highest power first), and put a
\blank{1.4cm} in for every missing power. Then repeat: divide the leading terms, multiply,
\emph{subtract}, bring down.

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Model --- knock the wall down.} Divide $p(x) = x^3 - 3x + 2$ by $x - 1$. Note the
placeholder $0x^2$.
\[
\begin{array}{r}
  x^2 + x - 2 \phantom{{}+2} \\[2pt]
  x-1\,\overline{\smash{\big)}\,x^3+0x^2-3x+2} \\[2pt]
  \underline{-(x^3-\phantom{1}x^2)}\phantom{{}-3x+2} \\[2pt]
  x^2-3x\phantom{{}+2} \\[2pt]
  \underline{-(x^2-\phantom{1}x)}\phantom{{}+2} \\[2pt]
  -2x+2 \\[2pt]
  \underline{-(-2x+2)} \\[2pt]
  0
\end{array}
\]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item Quotient: \blank{2.6cm} \qquad Remainder: \blank{1.2cm}
  \item Because the remainder is $0$: \; $x^3 - 3x + 2 = (x-1)\cdot($ \blank{2.6cm} $)$
  \item Now finish the job --- the quotient still factors:
        $x^3 - 3x + 2 = $ \blank{5.0cm}
  \item Check it against the graph in the Hook: the zero $x = 1$ has multiplicity \blank{1.0cm},
        so the graph \blank{2.0cm} there, and $x = -2$ has multiplicity \blank{1.0cm}, so the graph
        \blank{2.0cm} there. \textbf{The wall from Lesson 4.2 is down.}
\end{itemize}

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Your turn --- and this one has a remainder.} Divide $2x^3 + 3x^2 - 5$ by $x + 2$. Fill in the
missing power first.
\[
\begin{array}{r}
  \phantom{2x^2 - x + 2} \\[2pt]
  x+2\,\overline{\smash{\big)}\,2x^3+3x^2+\underline{\hspace{0.7cm}}-5}
\end{array}
\]
\vspace{2.0in}

Quotient: \blank{3.0cm} \quad Remainder: \blank{1.2cm} \quad Final answer:
$\dfrac{2x^3+3x^2-5}{x+2} = $ \blank{4.6cm}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{4. Synthetic Division: The Shortcut (and Its One Restriction)}
Synthetic division works \textbf{only} when the divisor looks like $x - r$. It throws away the
variables and keeps the \blank{3.2cm}.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{The sign flip --- the error to guard against.} For $x - 1$ you use $r = $ \blank{0.9cm}. For
$x + 2$, rewrite it as $x - ($\blank{0.9cm}$)$, so $r = $ \blank{0.9cm}.

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Same problem, faster.} $x^3 - 3x + 2 \;\div\; (x-1)$ --- coefficients $1,\;0,\;-3,\;2$
(there is that placeholder again). Bring down, multiply by $r$, add.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{r|C{1.5cm}C{1.5cm}C{1.5cm}C{1.5cm}}
$1$ & $1$ & $0$ & $-3$ & $2$ \\
    &     &     &      &     \\ \cline{2-5}
    &     &     &      &     \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The last number in the bottom row is the \blank{2.4cm}; the others are the coefficients of the
quotient, whose degree is \blank{3.4cm} the dividend's.

\vspace{2pt}
Quotient: \blank{3.0cm} \quad Remainder: \blank{1.2cm} \quad Same as the long division? \blank{1.2cm}

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Now the one with a remainder.} $2x^3 + 3x^2 - 5 \;\div\; (x+2)$, so $r = $ \blank{0.9cm}.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{r|C{1.5cm}C{1.5cm}C{1.5cm}C{1.5cm}}
    & $2$ & $3$ & $0$ & $-5$ \\
    &     &     &     &      \\ \cline{2-5}
    &     &     &     &      \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Quotient: \blank{3.0cm} \quad Remainder: \blank{1.2cm}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{5. The Remainder Theorem}
Look hard at that last remainder, $-9$. Now evaluate the dividend at $r = -2$:
$f(-2) = 2(-2)^3 + 3(-2)^2 - 5 = $ \blank{1.6cm}. \textbf{Not a coincidence.}

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=navy, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=1.5mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
\textbf{Remainder Theorem.} When $f(x)$ is divided by $x - r$, the remainder equals \blank{1.6cm}.
\end{tcolorbox}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Why it works} --- one line from the division statement. Write $f(x) = (x-r)q(x) + R$ and
substitute $x = r$:
\[
  f(r) = (\,\blank{1.0cm}\,)\,q(r) + R = \blank{1.6cm}
\]
So the theorem cuts both ways: it gives a remainder without dividing, \emph{and} it gives a fast way
to evaluate $f$ at a number.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{6. The Factor Theorem --- Five Ways to Say One Thing}
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=navy, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=1.5mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
\textbf{Factor Theorem.} $(x-r)$ is a factor of $f(x)$ \; if and only if \; $f(r) = $ \blank{1.0cm}.
\end{tcolorbox}

\vspace{4pt}
For a polynomial $f$ and a number $r$, all five of these say \emph{exactly} the same thing:
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{|c|p{11.0cm}|}
\hline
1 & $f(r) = 0$ \\ \hline
2 & The remainder on dividing $f(x)$ by $x-r$ is \blank{1.4cm} \\ \hline
3 & $(x-r)$ is a \blank{2.2cm} of $f(x)$ \\ \hline
4 & $r$ is a \blank{2.2cm} of the function $f$ \\ \hline
5 & The point $($\blank{0.8cm}$,\;$\blank{0.8cm}$)$ is an $x$-intercept of the graph of $f$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
Warm-Up item 3 gave you $p(1) = 0$ and $p(-2) = 0$ for $p(x) = x^3-3x+2$. So without dividing
anything, you already knew that both \blank{1.6cm} and \blank{1.6cm} are factors of $p$.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{The workflow for the rest of this unit.} Test a candidate $r$ with the Remainder Theorem
(or a synthetic division). If you get $0$, divide it out. The quotient --- one degree lower --- is
called the \textbf{depressed polynomial}, and it is small enough to finish with Lesson 4.2's tools.
\end{notesbox}
