\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=forestbg, colframe=forest, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students can add and subtract rational expressions --- combining
    numerators directly when the denominators match, and otherwise \textbf{factoring every denominator}
    and building the \textbf{least common denominator} from those factors (each distinct factor to its
    highest power), rewriting each fraction with the \textbf{building factor} it is missing, combining,
    and factoring the result to see whether anything divides out. They put the second numerator in
    \textbf{parentheses} on every subtraction, and they recognize a \textbf{complex fraction} as a
    division in disguise --- simplifying it as a quotient of two simple fractions. Above all they state
    \emph{every} restriction, read off the \emph{original} denominators (equivalently, off the LCD's
    factors) and, for a complex fraction, off the whole bottom as well --- then say which excluded value
    is a \textbf{hole} and which is a \textbf{wall}.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Standards (2023 VA SOL):} \textbf{A2.EO.1a} --- add, subtract, multiply, or divide rational
    algebraic expressions, simplifying the result (today: add and subtract). \textbf{A2.EO.1c} ---
    recognize a complex algebraic fraction and simplify it as a product or quotient of simple algebraic
    fractions. Applied throughout: \textbf{A2.EO.1b} (equivalent rational expressions with monomial and
    binomial factors; expressions limited to linear and quadratic) and \textbf{A2.EO.1d} (equivalence of
    forms --- homework problem 6). Builds on \textbf{A2.EO.3b} (factor completely, Lesson 3.2), Lesson
    4.1's restriction habit, and Lesson 4.2's keep--change--flip, which Section 4 reuses outright; it is
    the prerequisite for \textbf{A2.EI.4b/c} (4.6 --- solving rational equations begins by multiplying
    through by an LCD, and an extraneous solution is one of today's excluded values sneaking back in).
\end{tcolorbox}

\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Ideas \& Skills} & \textbf{Key Understandings (the ``why'')} \\[2pt]
\hline
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item \textbf{Like denominators:} add or subtract the numerators, keep the denominator, then simplify.
  \item \textbf{Parenthesize} the second numerator on every subtraction, then distribute the minus sign.
  \item \textbf{Build the LCD} from the \emph{factored} denominators --- each distinct factor to its highest power.
  \item \textbf{Rewrite} each fraction with the building factor $\frac kk$ it is missing; combine; factor the numerator; divide out.
  \item Handle \textbf{opposite binomials} in denominators ($1-x=-(x-1)$) instead of doubling the LCD.
  \item \textbf{Simplify a complex fraction} as a quotient of two simple fractions (A2.EO.1c).
  \item State every restriction; sort each into \textbf{hole} or \textbf{wall}.
\end{itemize}
&
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item A denominator says how big each piece is. You can only count pieces that are the same size --- which is why adding needs a common denominator and multiplying never did.
  \item You never add denominators. $\frac12+\frac12$ is $1$, not $\frac24$.
  \item The product of the denominators always works; it is just expensive, and it buys you a re-factoring job at the end.
  \item \textbf{The minus sign owns the whole numerator.} The wrong answer often simplifies more prettily than the right one --- which is exactly why it survives.
  \item \textbf{The LCD is the restriction list.} It already contains every factor of every original denominator.
  \item \textbf{The main fraction bar is a division sign}, so whatever sits below it may never equal zero.
  \item An answer with no restriction list is half an answer --- even when the answer is a plain number.
\end{itemize}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{sky}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\textbf{Like denominators} & \TallMath{\frac ac\pm\frac bc=\frac{a\pm b}{c}} for $c\neq0$ --- combine the numerators, keep the denominator. \\
\textbf{Least common denominator} & The smallest expression every denominator divides into: each \emph{distinct} factor, raised to the \emph{highest} power it reaches in any one denominator. \\
\textbf{Building factor} & The form of $1$, written $\frac kk$, that supplies whatever a fraction's denominator is missing from the LCD. \\
\textbf{The parenthesis rule} & \TallMath{\frac ac-\frac bc=\frac{a-(b)}{c}} --- the minus sign changes the sign of \emph{every} term of $b$. \\
\textbf{Opposite binomials} & $b-a=-(a-b)$, so $\frac{k}{b-a}=\frac{-k}{a-b}$; the LCD is $a-b$, not $(a-b)(b-a)$. \\
\textbf{Complex algebraic fraction} & A fraction with fractions inside its numerator, its denominator, or both (A2.EO.1c). \\
\textbf{Main fraction bar} & The long bar of a complex fraction --- a division sign. Whatever sits below it may never be zero. \\
\textbf{Hole vs.\ wall (4.0)} & Factor divides out $\Rightarrow$ removable discontinuity; factor stays $\Rightarrow$ vertical asymptote. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{fixedskillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{forestbg}
    The Warm-Up rehearses three skills, one per leg of the lesson. \textbf{(1)} \emph{The LCD on
    numbers}, where students already trust it: $\frac{5}{12}+\frac{7}{18}$ is done by factoring
    $12=2^2\cdot3$ and $18=2\cdot3^2$ into the LCD $36$, and then \emph{redone} over $12\cdot18=216$ so
    the class can answer honestly why we bother with the \emph{least} common denominator (you get the
    same value, and you buy a reduction at the end). Part (c) keeps keep--change--flip warm --- you need
    it in Section 4. \textbf{(2)} \emph{Signs and factors}: $9-(4-6)$ versus $9-4-6$ plants today's
    signature error numerically before any letters appear, and $x^2-9$, $x^2-4$, $x^2+5x+6$ all reappear
    in the notes. \textbf{(3)} \emph{Simplify and restrict} $\frac{3x-9}{x^2-9}$ with the hole/wall sort,
    carrying 4.1 forward. Debrief all three --- but say nothing about item 3's answer,
    $\frac{3}{x+3}$ with $x\neq3,-3$. The anchor problem in Section 3 lands on exactly that function
    from a completely different starting point, and the recognition is worth more than the hint.
\end{fixedskillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{forestbg}
    Put $D(x)=\dfrac{4x-1}{x+3}-\dfrac{2x-7}{x+3}$ on the board with two students' answers beside it ---
    Dev's $\dfrac{2x-8}{x+3}$ and Elin's plain $2$ --- and have the class fill a table at $x=0,1,-3$.
    Dev's column never matches ($-\tfrac83$ and $-\tfrac32$ against the true $2$ and $2$), and the
    disproof is a number, not a rule. \textbf{``Which one keystroke is Dev missing?''} (The parentheses:
    subtracting the whole numerator gives $-(2x-7)=-2x+7$, so his numerator is off by $14$ every time.)
    Point back at Warm-Up 2(a) rather than at a rule. Then the second beat, which is the one students do
    not see coming: \textbf{``Elin's value is right --- so is her answer finished?''} Look at the last
    column. The original has no value at $x=-3$ and Elin's ``$2$'' happily does, so her answer is
    incomplete: it is $2$ \emph{for every $x$ except $-3$} --- a horizontal line with a hole punched in
    it. Land both sentences now, because they run the whole period: \emph{the minus sign owns the whole
    numerator}, and \emph{even an answer as plain as ``2'' still carries the restrictions of the problem
    it came from}.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{forestbg}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. Like denominators: nothing to build.} State $\frac ac\pm\frac bc=\frac{a\pm b}{c}$ and spend
the time instead on the error it invites: \textbf{you never add the denominators}. Kill it on numbers
first --- $\frac12+\frac12$ is $1$, and adding denominators would turn two halves into a half. Work
$\frac{7}{x^2-4}+\frac{x}{x^2-4}=\frac{x+7}{(x-2)(x+2)}$, factoring the denominator \emph{before}
combining so the restrictions are visible, and end on the habit that never changes: \emph{factor the new
numerator and check whether anything divides out}.

\textbf{2. Subtraction, and the parenthesis.} Post the rule as a physical instruction --- write the
parentheses \emph{before} you distribute. Work
$\frac{6x+5}{x^2-4}-\frac{2x+13}{x^2-4}$: the numerator is $(6x+5)-(2x+13)=4x-8=4(x-2)$, the $(x-2)$
divides out, and the answer is $\frac{4}{x+2}$ with $x\neq2,-2$. Have the class verify at $x=0$ (both
sides give $2$). Then the 4.0 callback: $(x-2)$ cancelled, so $x=2$ is a \textbf{hole}; $(x+2)$ stayed,
so $x=-2$ is a \textbf{wall}.

\textbf{3. Unlike denominators --- the lesson.} Six steps: factor, build the LCD, list the restrictions,
rewrite with building factors, combine, factor-and-divide-out. Do the monomial case
$\frac{5}{6x^2}+\frac{7}{4x}=\frac{21x+10}{12x^2}$ first, then the anchor
$\frac{3}{x-3}-\frac{18}{x^2-9}$, which collapses to $\frac{3}{x+3}$ with $x\neq3,-3$ --- and stop:
\textbf{``where have you seen this answer today?''} It is the Warm-Up. Add the two-trinomial case
$\frac{2}{x^2+5x+6}+\frac{3}{x^2-9}=\frac{5x}{(x+2)(x+3)(x-3)}$ so the shared factor $(x+3)$ appears in
the LCD exactly \emph{once}. Then the payoff sentence: \textbf{the LCD is the restriction list} --- it
already holds every factor of every original denominator, so setting its factors to zero costs nothing
and misses nothing. Close the section with opposite binomials:
$\frac{4}{x-5}+\frac{2}{5-x}=\frac{2}{x-5}$, and the LCD was never $(x-5)(5-x)$.

\columnbreak

\textbf{4. Complex fractions (A2.EO.1c).} Define one, then say the sentence that makes it easy:
\textbf{the main fraction bar is a division sign}. Work
$\dfrac{\frac1x+\frac12}{\frac1x-\frac14}$ twice. \emph{Method 1 --- combine, then divide:} top
$\frac{x+2}{2x}$, bottom $\frac{4-x}{4x}$, keep--change--flip to $\frac{2(x+2)}{4-x}$. This is the
method the standard names, and it is the one that keeps the restrictions readable. \emph{Method 2 ---
multiply top and bottom by $4x$}, the LCD of every inner fraction, and the little fractions vanish at
once. Both give the same answer; the difference is bookkeeping, and that is the discussion. Restrictions
come from two levels: every inner denominator ($x\neq0$) and \emph{the whole bottom} ($4-x\neq0$, so
$x\neq4$) --- which is Lesson 4.2's source 3 wearing a new coat, since a fraction is zero exactly when
its numerator is. Verify at $x=2$ (both give $4$). Then the second example
$\dfrac{\frac1x-\frac13}{x-3}=-\frac{1}{3x}$, $x\neq0,3$, where the opposite binomials return and the
answer's $-1$ is the whole point.

\textbf{5. The picture.} Finish on the graph of the Section 3 anchor so today stays a \emph{function}
lesson: $y=\frac{3}{x+3}$ with a wall at $x=-3$ and a hole at $\left(3,\frac12\right)$. One subtraction
produced one of each, and both came from denominators that no longer appear on the page. Name where this
returns: \textbf{4.5} (every restriction becomes a hole or a wall, read straight off the equation) and
\textbf{4.6} (multiply through by the LCD to solve --- and any solution that lands on one of these values
is extraneous).
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: Building the LCD]{forestbg}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{0.46\linewidth} X@{}}
\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Steps (post these).}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.3em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item \textbf{Factor} every denominator completely.
  \item \textbf{Build the LCD:} every distinct factor, each to the highest power it reaches in \emph{any one} denominator.
  \item \textbf{Write the restrictions now} --- set each factor of the LCD to zero.
  \item \textbf{Rewrite} each fraction times the building factor $\frac kk$ it is missing.
  \item \textbf{Combine} over the LCD --- parentheses on every subtraction --- and simplify the numerator.
  \item \textbf{Factor the numerator}, divide out what you can, attach the restrictions.
\end{enumerate}
&
\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Worked, on the anchor.}
\[ \frac{3}{x-3}-\frac{18}{x^2-9}
   =\frac{3}{x-3}\cdot\frac{x+3}{x+3}-\frac{18}{(x-3)(x+3)} \]
\[ =\frac{(3x+9)-18}{(x-3)(x+3)}=\frac{3(x-3)}{(x-3)(x+3)}=\frac{3}{x+3} \]
\centerline{$x\neq3,\;-3$ \quad (from the LCD, before anything cancelled)}
\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{The two questions students must answer aloud:} \emph{which factor did each fraction have to be
given?} and \emph{which restriction will your answer never remind you of?} Here the answer displays
$x\neq-3$ and stays silent about $x=3$ --- the hole.
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
Circulate during the guided practice and Tier work. Watch for and correct:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{the lost parenthesis} on a subtraction --- the signature error, and the one whose wrong answer often \emph{simplifies more prettily} than the right one, so it survives unchallenged;
  \item \textbf{adding the denominators} ($\frac1x+\frac13=\frac{2}{x+3}$) --- do not assert, hand the student $x=1$;
  \item \textbf{the LCD built as a product} of the denominators --- not wrong, only expensive; make them re-factor the result in front of you;
  \item \textbf{a doubled LCD from opposite binomials}, $(x-1)(1-x)$ --- ask what that product actually equals;
  \item \textbf{multiplying only the denominator} by the building factor and forgetting the numerator;
  \item \textbf{cancelling out of a sum} before it is combined --- Lesson 4.1's ghost;
  \item \textbf{restrictions read off the final answer}, still costing points at its third appearance, and now worse: a cancelled factor is invisible \emph{and} the LCD would have given it away for free;
  \item \textbf{no restriction at all on a constant or polynomial answer};
  \item \textbf{a complex fraction attacked by cancelling across the main bar} instead of combining each level first.
\end{itemize}
Cold-call prompts: ``What is the LCD, and which factor came from which denominator?'' ``Read me the
numerator with its parentheses still in it.'' ``Which building factor did this fraction need, and why
that one?'' ``Your answer's denominator is $x+3$ --- so where did $x\neq3$ go?'' ``The main bar is a
division sign; what does that make the bottom?''
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\textbf{Build the Denominator, Guard the Sign} activity (tiered; mirrors the notes).
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate.} Five scaffolded items: a like-denominator sum
($\frac{2x}{x-6}+\frac{5}{x-6}$), a like-denominator \emph{difference} with the parentheses pre-printed
($\frac{4x+1}{x+2}-\frac{x-8}{x+2}=\frac{3(x+3)}{x+2}$), a monomial LCD ($\frac3x+\frac{5}{2x}$), a
first binomial LCD ($\frac{1}{x-1}+\frac{2}{x+3}$), and then item 5, which is the point of the tier:
redo item 2 \emph{wrongly on purpose}, dropping the parentheses, and test all three expressions at
$x=0$ ($\frac92$, $\frac92$, $-\frac72$). Do not let anyone skip item 5 --- it is where the error stops
being abstract.

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching.} A four-row table (denominators factored / LCD \& restrictions /
simplest form) over $\frac{7}{4x^2}-\frac{3}{10x}$, $\frac{x}{x^2-4}+\frac{1}{x+2}$,
$\frac{2}{x-4}-\frac{16}{x^2-16}$ (the row that cancels: answer $\frac{2}{x+4}$, so $x=4$ is a hole),
and $\frac{5}{x-2}+\frac{3}{2-x}$ (\emph{opposite binomials} --- the LCD is $x-2$). Then an
\textbf{error analysis}: a student adds tops and bottoms, $\frac1x+\frac13=\frac{2}{x+3}$; groups
disprove it at $x=1$ ($\frac43$ vs.\ $\frac12$), do it correctly, and explain in one sentence why
multiplying \emph{may} work straight across and adding may not.

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension.} \emph{Part 1:} one complex fraction,
$\frac{\frac1x-\frac14}{\frac{1}{x^2}-\frac{1}{16}}=\frac{4x}{4+x}$, done \emph{both} ways, with the
full restriction list $x\neq0,4,-4$ and the conceptual question: Method 2 (multiply through by $16x^2$)
erases the evidence for $x\neq0$, because clearing the inner denominators is exactly what it does.
\emph{Part 2:} build a \emph{difference} that simplifies to $\frac{2}{x+1}$ with restrictions exactly
$x\neq-1,4$ (e.g.\ $\frac{2}{x-4}-\frac{10}{x^2-3x-4}$), then sort the two values into hole and wall
without graphing. \emph{Part 3:} two paint crews, $x$ and $x+3$ hours --- combined rate
$\frac{2x+3}{x(x+3)}$ per hour, together-time $\frac{x(x+3)}{2x+3}$ hours (a complex fraction),
sanity-checked at $x=6$ ($3.6$ hours, faster than both), with the algebraic exclusions separated from
the contextual domain $x>0$.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (independent, no notes): \textbf{(1)} combine
$\frac{5}{x-2}-\frac{20}{x^2-4}$, showing the factored denominators, the LCD, and the combined
numerator $5(x-2)$; the answer $\frac{5}{x+2}$ with $x\neq2,-2$, then the sort --- hole at $x=2$ of
height $\frac54$, wall at $x=-2$. \textbf{(2)} an \textbf{error item}: a classmate wrote
$\frac{4x+1}{x-6}-\frac{x-5}{x-6}=\frac{3x-4}{x-6}$; full credit requires naming the lost parenthesis,
giving $\frac{3(x+2)}{x-6}$ with $x\neq6$, \emph{and} settling it at $x=0$ ($-1$ against $\frac23$).
\textbf{(3)} an \textbf{SOL-style multiple-choice} item on the complex fraction
$\frac{\frac1x-\frac15}{x-5}$, answer $-\frac{1}{5x}$ with $x\neq0,5$, whose distractors each break
exactly one idea (the lost $-1$ from opposite binomials / the main bar's restriction forgotten /
never simplified, restrictions read off its own denominator). Collect and sort into ``got it / sign
error / LCD built as a product / restrictions incomplete.'' The sign pile is the one to re-teach
immediately; the restrictions pile is the one to remember, because 4.6 is where an excluded value
becomes an \emph{extraneous solution} and those students will meet it again.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} three like-denominator items, two of which have answers with no denominator at all
(a constant $3$ and the line $x-5$) so the restriction is the only trace left; three monomial LCDs
including a two-variable case; five binomial/quadratic items spanning a plain two-linear LCD, a
non-factoring numerator that is nonetheless finished ($\frac{x^2+x+6}{x^2-9}$), a cancelling case
($\frac{4}{x-6}-\frac{48}{x^2-36}=\frac{4}{x+6}$), an \emph{opposite-binomial} pair
($\frac{6}{x-1}+\frac{3}{1-x}$), and a three-factor LCD; three \textbf{complex fractions}, the last of
which draws its $x\neq0$ from the main bar rather than from any visible denominator; a \textbf{round-trip
model} ($30$ miles out at $x$, back at $x+5$) whose total time is $\frac{30(2x+5)}{x(x+5)}$ and whose
\emph{average speed} $\frac{2x(x+5)}{2x+5}$ is $12$ mi/h --- not the $12.5$ everyone predicts, because
the slower leg takes longer and therefore counts for more; and an \textbf{A2.EO.1d justification} pairing
one genuinely equivalent pair with one that only looks equivalent
($\frac{3}{x-3}-\frac{18}{x^2-9}$ versus $\frac{3}{x+3}$, which accepts $x=3$). \textbf{Extension:} an
error gallery on $\frac{5}{x-1}-\frac{2}{x+1}$ --- three students, three answers (added tops and
bottoms / lost the parenthesis, and got an answer that cancels \emph{beautifully} / correct expression
with no restrictions) --- plus a \emph{design} task: build a \emph{sum} equal to $\frac{5}{x-1}$ with
restrictions exactly $x\neq1,-2$, which forces one denominator to be the quadratic $(x+2)(x-1)$.
\textbf{Preview:} Lesson 4.4, \emph{The Rational Parent Function \& Transformations} --- these
expressions go back on the grid as relatives of $y=\frac1x$, and shifting one \emph{moves its
asymptotes} off the axes.
\end{skillbox}
