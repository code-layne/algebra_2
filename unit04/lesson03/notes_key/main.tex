\begin{objectivebox}
\textbf{By the end of this lesson, I will be able to\dots}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item divide a polynomial by a \emph{monomial}, and by a \emph{binomial} or \emph{factorable trinomial} using long division;
  \item use \emph{synthetic division} when the divisor has the form $x - r$, and write the answer as quotient plus remainder over divisor;
  \item use the \emph{Remainder Theorem} to find a remainder without dividing, and the \emph{Factor Theorem} to decide whether $(x-r)$ is a factor;
  \item divide out a known factor to \emph{finish} a factorization that Lesson 4.2's tools could not reach.
\end{itemize}
\end{objectivebox}

\vspace{0.05in}

\begin{vocabbox}
\small
Fill in each term as we build it together.
\vspace{2pt}

\termblanklong{Division statement (dividend $=$ divisor $\times$ quotient $+$ remainder)}
\ansline{$P(x) = D(x)\,Q(x) + R(x)$, where the degree of $R$ is less than the degree of $D$.}
\termblanklong{Synthetic division}
\ansline{A shortcut for dividing by $x-r$ that uses only the coefficients: bring down, multiply by $r$, add.}
\termblanklong{Remainder Theorem}
\ansline{Dividing $f(x)$ by $x-r$ leaves the remainder $f(r)$.}
\termblanklong{Factor Theorem}
\ansline{$(x-r)$ is a factor of $f(x)$ exactly when $f(r)=0$.}
\termblanklong{Depressed polynomial}
\ansline{The quotient left after dividing a known factor out --- one degree lower, and easier to factor.}
\end{vocabbox}

\begin{hookbox}
Lesson 4.2 ended at a wall. The unit's anchor, $p(x) = x^3 - 3x + 2$, has no GCF, is not a
difference of squares or cubes, is not in quadratic form, and has only three terms --- so it cannot
be grouped. \emph{And yet it factors:} in Lesson 4.0 you read its graph and saw a touch at $x = 1$
and a crossing at $x = -2$.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.45]
  \lgrid{-3}{3}{-2}{6}
  \begin{scope}
    \clip (-3,-2) rectangle (3,6);
    \draw[very thick, forest, domain=-2.6:2.1, samples=120, smooth]
      plot (\x,{(\x)*(\x)*(\x) - 3*(\x) + 2});
  \end{scope}
  \fill[charcoal] (-2,0) circle (3pt) node[below left, font=\scriptsize]{$(-2,0)$};
  \fill[charcoal] (1,0)  circle (3pt) node[below right, font=\scriptsize]{$(1,0)$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item In the Warm-Up you found $p(1) = $ \ans{$0$} and $p(-2) = $ \ans{$0$}.
  \item You also multiplied $(x-1)(x^2+x-2)$ and got \ans{$x^3-3x+2$}, so $(x-1)$ \emph{is}
        a factor of $p$.
  \item Here is the honest question: nobody handed you $x^2 + x - 2$ today. If you know $(x-1)$ is a
        factor, how would you \emph{find} the other factor yourself? \ansline{Divide $p(x)$ by $(x-1)$ --- the quotient is the other factor.}
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Answer: divide.} That is the whole lesson.
\end{hookbox}

\begin{notesbox}{1. What Division Even Means (the statement behind everything)}
Warm-Up item 1: $247 \div 5$ gave quotient $49$ and remainder $2$, which you wrote as
$247 = 5 \cdot 49 + 2$. Polynomials obey the exact same statement.
\[
  \underbrace{P(x)}_{\text{dividend}} \;=\; \underbrace{D(x)}_{\text{divisor}} \cdot
  \underbrace{Q(x)}_{\text{quotient}} \;+\; \underbrace{R(x)}_{\text{remainder}}
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \frac{P(x)}{D(x)} = Q(x) + \frac{R(x)}{D(x)}
\]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item You stop dividing when the degree of the remainder is \ans{less than} the degree of the divisor.
  \item Every division can be \emph{checked} by \ans{multiplying $D\cdot Q$ and adding $R$}.
  \item And the case we care most about: if $R = 0$, then $P(x) = D(x)\cdot Q(x)$, which says
        $D(x)$ is a \ans{factor} of $P(x)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{2. Dividing by a Monomial: Split the Fraction}
A sum over a single term splits term by term: $\dfrac{a+b}{c} = \dfrac{a}{c} + \dfrac{b}{c}$.

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item $\dfrac{12x^4 - 8x^3 + 6x^2}{2x^2} = $ \ans{$6x^2 - 4x + 3$}
  \item Two variables: \; $\dfrac{15x^3y^2 - 10x^2y^3 + 5x^2y^2}{5x^2y^2} = $ \ans{$3x - 2y + 1$}
\end{itemize}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{The trap.} In that second problem the last term divides out to \ans{$1$} --- \emph{not}
zero. A term that cancels completely leaves a \ans{$1$} behind.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{3. Long Division: When the Divisor Has More Than One Term}
\textbf{Setup rules.} Write both polynomials in \ans{standard} form (highest power first), and put a
\ans{$0$} in for every missing power. Then repeat: divide the leading terms, multiply,
\emph{subtract}, bring down.

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Model --- knock the wall down.} Divide $p(x) = x^3 - 3x + 2$ by $x - 1$. Note the
placeholder $0x^2$.
\[
\begin{array}{r}
  x^2 + x - 2 \phantom{{}+2} \\[2pt]
  x-1\,\overline{\smash{\big)}\,x^3+0x^2-3x+2} \\[2pt]
  \underline{-(x^3-\phantom{1}x^2)}\phantom{{}-3x+2} \\[2pt]
  x^2-3x\phantom{{}+2} \\[2pt]
  \underline{-(x^2-\phantom{1}x)}\phantom{{}+2} \\[2pt]
  -2x+2 \\[2pt]
  \underline{-(-2x+2)} \\[2pt]
  0
\end{array}
\]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item Quotient: \ans{$x^2+x-2$} \qquad Remainder: \ans{$0$}
  \item Because the remainder is $0$: \; $x^3 - 3x + 2 = (x-1)\cdot($ \ans{$x^2+x-2$} $)$
  \item Now finish the job --- the quotient still factors:
        $x^3 - 3x + 2 = $ \ans{$(x-1)(x-1)(x+2) = (x-1)^2(x+2)$}
  \item Check it against the graph in the Hook: the zero $x = 1$ has multiplicity \ans{$2$},
        so the graph \ans{touches} there, and $x = -2$ has multiplicity \ans{$1$}, so the graph
        \ans{crosses} there. \textbf{The wall from Lesson 4.2 is down.}
\end{itemize}

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Your turn --- and this one has a remainder.} Divide $2x^3 + 3x^2 - 5$ by $x + 2$. Fill in the
missing power first.
\[
\begin{array}{r}
  \mans{2x^2 - x + 2} \phantom{{}-5} \\[2pt]
  x+2\,\overline{\smash{\big)}\,2x^3+3x^2+\mans{0x}-5} \\[2pt]
  \underline{-(2x^3+4x^2)}\phantom{{}+0x-5} \\[2pt]
  -x^2+0x\phantom{{}-5} \\[2pt]
  \underline{-(-x^2-2x)}\phantom{{}-5} \\[2pt]
  2x-5 \\[2pt]
  \underline{-(2x+4)} \\[2pt]
  -9
\end{array}
\]
\vspace{4pt}

Quotient: \ans{$2x^2-x+2$} \quad Remainder: \ans{$-9$} \quad Final answer:
$\dfrac{2x^3+3x^2-5}{x+2} = $ \ans{$2x^2-x+2-\dfrac{9}{x+2}$}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{4. Synthetic Division: The Shortcut (and Its One Restriction)}
Synthetic division works \textbf{only} when the divisor looks like $x - r$. It throws away the
variables and keeps the \ans{coefficients}.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{The sign flip --- the error to guard against.} For $x - 1$ you use $r = $ \ans{$1$}. For
$x + 2$, rewrite it as $x - ($\ans{$-2$}$)$, so $r = $ \ans{$-2$}.

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Same problem, faster.} $x^3 - 3x + 2 \;\div\; (x-1)$ --- coefficients $1,\;0,\;-3,\;2$
(there is that placeholder again). Bring down, multiply by $r$, add.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{r|C{1.5cm}C{1.5cm}C{1.5cm}C{1.5cm}}
$1$ & $1$ & $0$ & $-3$ & $2$ \\
    &     & \ans{$1$} & \ans{$1$} & \ans{$-2$} \\ \cline{2-5}
    & \ans{$1$} & \ans{$1$} & \ans{$-2$} & \ans{$0$} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The last number in the bottom row is the \ans{remainder}; the others are the coefficients of the
quotient, whose degree is \ans{one less than} the dividend's.

\vspace{2pt}
Quotient: \ans{$x^2+x-2$} \quad Remainder: \ans{$0$} \quad Same as the long division? \ans{Yes}

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Now the one with a remainder.} $2x^3 + 3x^2 - 5 \;\div\; (x+2)$, so $r = $ \ans{$-2$}.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{r|C{1.5cm}C{1.5cm}C{1.5cm}C{1.5cm}}
\ans{$-2$} & $2$ & $3$ & $0$ & $-5$ \\
    &     & \ans{$-4$} & \ans{$2$} & \ans{$-4$} \\ \cline{2-5}
    & \ans{$2$} & \ans{$-1$} & \ans{$2$} & \ans{$-9$} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Quotient: \ans{$2x^2-x+2$} \quad Remainder: \ans{$-9$}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{5. The Remainder Theorem}
Look hard at that last remainder, $-9$. Now evaluate the dividend at $r = -2$:
$f(-2) = 2(-2)^3 + 3(-2)^2 - 5 = $ \ans{$-9$}. \textbf{Not a coincidence.}

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=navy, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=1.5mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
\textbf{Remainder Theorem.} When $f(x)$ is divided by $x - r$, the remainder equals \ans{$f(r)$}.
\end{tcolorbox}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Why it works} --- one line from the division statement. Write $f(x) = (x-r)q(x) + R$ and
substitute $x = r$:
\[
  f(r) = (\,\mans{0}\,)\,q(r) + R = \mans{R}
\]
So the theorem cuts both ways: it gives a remainder without dividing, \emph{and} it gives a fast way
to evaluate $f$ at a number.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{6. The Factor Theorem --- Five Ways to Say One Thing}
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=navy, boxrule=0.7pt, arc=1.5mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
\textbf{Factor Theorem.} $(x-r)$ is a factor of $f(x)$ \; if and only if \; $f(r) = $ \ans{$0$}.
\end{tcolorbox}

\vspace{4pt}
For a polynomial $f$ and a number $r$, all five of these say \emph{exactly} the same thing:
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{|c|p{11.0cm}|}
\hline
1 & $f(r) = 0$ \\ \hline
2 & The remainder on dividing $f(x)$ by $x-r$ is \ans{$0$} \\ \hline
3 & $(x-r)$ is a \ans{factor} of $f(x)$ \\ \hline
4 & $r$ is a \ans{zero} of the function $f$ \\ \hline
5 & The point $($\ans{$r$}$,\;$\ans{$0$}$)$ is an $x$-intercept of the graph of $f$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
Warm-Up item 3 gave you $p(1) = 0$ and $p(-2) = 0$ for $p(x) = x^3-3x+2$. So without dividing
anything, you already knew that both \ans{$(x-1)$} and \ans{$(x+2)$} are factors of $p$.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{The workflow for the rest of this unit.} Test a candidate $r$ with the Remainder Theorem
(or a synthetic division). If you get $0$, divide it out. The quotient --- one degree lower --- is
called the \textbf{depressed polynomial}, and it is small enough to finish with Lesson 4.2's tools.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{practicebox}
\textbf{Crack one on your own.} Let $g(x) = x^3 + 4x^2 + x - 6$.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em, itemsep=6pt]
  \item Test $r = 1$ with the Remainder Theorem: $g(1) = $ \ans{$0$}
  \item So $(x - 1)$ \ans{is} a factor of $g$. (is / is not)
  \item Divide it out synthetically:
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
    \begin{tabular}{r|C{1.4cm}C{1.4cm}C{1.4cm}C{1.4cm}}
    $1$ & $1$ & $4$ & $1$ & $-6$ \\
        &     & \ans{$1$} & \ans{$5$} & \ans{$6$} \\ \cline{2-5}
        & \ans{$1$} & \ans{$5$} & \ans{$6$} & \ans{$0$} \\
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    Depressed polynomial: \ans{$x^2+5x+6$}
  \item Factor that quotient (Lesson 4.2 tools): \ans{$(x+2)(x+3)$}
  \item Write $g$ completely factored: \; $g(x) = $ \ans{$(x-1)(x+2)(x+3)$}
  \item List the three zeros of $g$: \ans{$1,\ -2,\ -3$}
  \item \textbf{The next wall.} We tried $r = 1$ only because the problem pointed us there. If you
        were handed $2x^3 - 3x^2 - 11x + 6$ with no hint, which numbers would you even test ---
        and how many are there? \ansline{Guessing is hopeless: there are infinitely many numbers. We need a rule that narrows the candidates to a short list --- that is Lesson 4.4.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{practicebox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Pacing.} Sections 1--2 take about eight minutes; Section 2 is mostly Unit 1 exponent rules
and exists to satisfy the ``monomial divisor'' half of A2.EO.3c and to kill the
$\frac{5x^2y^2}{5x^2y^2}=0$ error. Spend the period in Sections 3--6.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{The three errors to pre-empt.} (1) \emph{Missing placeholders} --- both worked problems have
a gap on purpose ($x^3-3x+2$ has no $x^2$; $2x^3+3x^2-5$ has no $x$). (2) \emph{Subtraction} --- in
long division the sign change is written out as $-(\;\cdots)$ in the model for a reason; students who
skip it get the right first term and wrong everything after. (3) \emph{The synthetic sign flip} ---
$x+2$ means $r=-2$. Make them say the rewritten form $x-(-2)$ out loud.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{The moment that matters} is the end of Section 3: dividing $x^3-3x+2$ by $x-1$ produces
$x^2+x-2$, which factors to $(x+2)(x-1)$ --- so $p(x)=(x-1)^2(x+2)$, exactly the multiplicity-2 touch
at $x=1$ and the crossing at $x=-2$ that students read off the graph back in Lesson 4.0. Say plainly
that the wall Lesson 4.2 hit is now down. Section 5's derivation is two lines and worth doing live:
substituting $x=r$ annihilates the $(x-r)$ term, so $f(r)=R$.

\end{teachernote}
