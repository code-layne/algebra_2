\begin{notesbox}{Practice}
Every answer needs \emph{two} parts: the simplest form \textbf{and} the restrictions. Factor every
denominator before you build an LCD, and put the second numerator in \textbf{parentheses} every single
time you subtract.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=11pt]
  \item \textbf{Like denominators.} Combine, simplify, restrict.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item $\dfrac{3x}{x+7}+\dfrac{21}{x+7}=\;$\ans{$3$} \quad $x\neq$ \ans{$-7$}
      \item $\dfrac{7x-1}{x^2-1}-\dfrac{3x+3}{x^2-1}=\;$\ans{$\frac{4}{x+1}$} \quad $x\neq$ \ans{$1,\ -1$}
      \item $\dfrac{x^2}{x+5}-\dfrac{25}{x+5}=\;$\ans{$x-5$} \quad $x\neq$ \ans{$-5$}
    \end{enumerate}
    \vspace{2pt}
    Your answer to (a) is a number and your answer to (c) is a polynomial. Neither has a denominator
    left. So what does each restriction do to the graph? \ans{it punches a hole: (a) is the horizontal line $y=3$ missing $(-7,3)$, and (c) is the line $y=x-5$ missing $(-5,-10)$}

  \item \textbf{Monomial LCDs.}
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item $\dfrac{3}{4x}+\dfrac{5}{6x}=\;$\ans{$\frac{19}{12x}$} \quad LCD used: \ans{$12x$}
      \item $\dfrac{7}{x^2}-\dfrac{2}{5x}=\;$\ans{$\frac{35-2x}{5x^2}$} \quad LCD used: \ans{$5x^2$}
      \item $\dfrac{4}{3a}+\dfrac{1}{a^2b}=\;$\ans{$\frac{4ab+3}{3a^2b}$} \quad restrictions: \ans{$a\neq0,\ b\neq0$}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Binomial and quadratic LCDs.} Factor first. One of these has \emph{opposite}
        binomials for denominators, and one of them cancels at the end.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=7pt]
      \item $\dfrac{2}{x+1}+\dfrac{3}{x-2}=\;$\ans{$\frac{5x-1}{(x+1)(x-2)}$} \quad $x\neq$ \ans{$-1,\ 2$}
      \item $\dfrac{x}{x-3}-\dfrac{2}{x+3}=\;$\ans{$\frac{x^2+x+6}{(x-3)(x+3)}$} \quad $x\neq$ \ans{$3,\ -3$}
      \item $\dfrac{4}{x-6}-\dfrac{48}{x^2-36}=\;$\ans{$\frac{4}{x+6}$} \quad $x\neq$ \ans{$6,\ -6$}
      \item $\dfrac{6}{x-1}+\dfrac{3}{1-x}=\;$\ans{$\frac{3}{x-1}$} \quad $x\neq$ \ans{1}
      \item $\dfrac{5}{x^2+4x+3}+\dfrac{2}{x^2-9}=\;$\ans{$\frac{7x-13}{(x+1)(x+3)(x-3)}$} \quad $x\neq$ \ans{$-1,\ -3,\ 3$}
    \end{enumerate}
    \vspace{2pt}
    {\small (c) is the one that cancels: $4(x+6)-48=4x-24=4(x-6)$, so $x=6$ is a hole and $x=-6$ is a
    wall. (d) is the opposite-binomial pair: $1-x=-(x-1)$, so $\frac{3}{1-x}=\frac{-3}{x-1}$ and the LCD
    is just $x-1$. In (b) the numerator $x^2+x+6$ is prime --- nothing cancels, and that is a finished
    answer.}

  \item \textbf{Complex fractions.} Simplify each as a quotient of two simple fractions, then state
        \emph{all} restrictions --- the inner denominators \emph{and} the main fraction bar.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=7pt]
      \item \TallMath{\frac{\;\dfrac{1}{x}+\dfrac{1}{6}\;}{\;\dfrac{1}{x}-\dfrac{1}{6}\;}}
            $=\;$\ans{$\frac{6+x}{6-x}$} \quad $x\neq$ \ans{$0,\ 6$}
      \item \TallMath{\frac{\;\dfrac{2}{x}-\dfrac{2}{5}\;}{\;x-5\;}}
            $=\;$\ans{$-\frac{2}{5x}$} \quad $x\neq$ \ans{$0,\ 5$}
      \item \TallMath{\frac{\;\dfrac{x}{x+2}\;}{\;\dfrac{x^2}{x^2-4}\;}}
            $=\;$\ans{$\frac{x-2}{x}$} \quad $x\neq$ \ans{$0,\ 2,\ -2$}
    \end{enumerate}
    \vspace{2pt}
    {\small In (a) the main bar contributes $x\neq6$ (the bottom is zero when $\frac1x=\frac16$). In (b)
    the top is $\frac{10-2x}{5x}=\frac{-2(x-5)}{5x}$, and the opposite binomials leave the $-1$. In (c)
    the main bar contributes $x\neq0$, because a fraction equals zero exactly when its numerator does
    --- Lesson 4.2's source 3, word for word.}

  \item \textbf{A round trip.} A cyclist rides $30$ miles out to a lake at $x$ mi/h and rides the same
        $30$ miles home at $x+5$ mi/h.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=5pt]
      \item Total time $T(x)=$ \ans{$\frac{30(2x+5)}{x(x+5)}$} hours (one fraction, simplified).
      \item Check your formula: if $x=10$, part (a) gives \ans{5} hours, and adding the two legs
            by hand gives \ans{5} hours.
      \item The algebra excludes two values of $x$. Which two? \ans{$0$ and $-5$} \; Which values of $x$
            make sense for a bike ride? \ans{$x>0$}
      \item \textbf{Average speed} for the whole trip is total distance divided by total time --- a
            complex fraction. Simplify $\dfrac{60}{T(x)}=$ \ans{$\frac{2x(x+5)}{2x+5}$} mi/h. \\[3pt]
            At $x=10$ that gives \ans{12} mi/h. Is that the average of $10$ and $15$?
            \ans{no} \; Explain in one sentence. \ans{you spend more \emph{time} at the slower speed, so it counts for more}
    \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Justify.} Are $\dfrac{1}{x-2}+\dfrac{1}{x+2}$ and $\dfrac{2x}{x^2-4}$ the same
        expression? Then answer the same question for $\dfrac{3}{x-3}-\dfrac{18}{x^2-9}$ and
        $\dfrac{3}{x+3}$. Answer both in terms of \emph{inputs}: which inputs does each form accept,
        and where do they agree?
        \ansline{First pair: yes, fully equivalent. Combining gives exactly $\frac{2x}{x^2-4}$, and both forms accept}
        \ansline{every input except $x=2,-2$, so they agree everywhere either one is defined. Second pair: \emph{not}}
        \ansline{interchangeable. They agree at every input both accept, but $\frac{3}{x+3}$ also accepts $x=3$ (giving $\frac12$)}
        \ansline{while the difference does not --- so as functions one has a hole at $\left(3,\frac12\right)$ and the other does not.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{extensionbox}
\textbf{Part 1 --- Three students, three answers.} Each student combined $\dfrac{5}{x-1}-\dfrac{2}{x+1}$
and got something different. Decide who is right, and name each error.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Student} & \textbf{Answer} & \textbf{Right or wrong --- and why?} \\
\hline
Gia   & $\dfrac{3}{2x}$ & \ans{wrong --- subtracted tops and bottoms; no LCD at all} \\
Hana  & $\dfrac{3}{x-1}$, $x\neq1,-1$ & \ans{wrong --- lost the parentheses; restrictions are right} \\
Ivo   & $\dfrac{3x+7}{x^2-1}$, no restrictions & \ans{correct expression, half an answer --- needs $x\neq1,-1$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\vspace{2pt}
Hana's answer is the dangerous one: her mistake produced a numerator that \emph{cancels beautifully}.
Show her work, find the exact step that went wrong, and then test her answer and the correct one at
$x=0$.
\ansline{Hana wrote $5(x+1)-2(x-1)$ as $5x+5-2x-2=3x+3=3(x+1)$, which divides out to $\frac{3}{x-1}$. The bad step is}
\ansline{$-2(x-1)=-2x-2$; it should be $-2x+2$, giving $3x+7$, which is prime. At $x=0$: original $=-5-2=-7$; correct $\frac{7}{-1}=-7$; Hana $\frac{3}{-1}=-3$.}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Part 2 --- Design one.} Write a \emph{sum} of two rational expressions whose simplified value is
$\dfrac{5}{x-1}$ and whose restrictions are exactly $x\neq1$ and $x\neq-2$.
\begin{center}
\ans{$\frac{5}{x+2}$} $+$ \ans{$\frac{15}{x^2+x-2}$}
\end{center}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Which of your denominators had to be a quadratic, and why could they not both be linear?
  \item Which excluded value is a hole on the graph of your expression, and which is a wall?
\end{enumerate}
\ansline{(a) $x^2+x-2=(x+2)(x-1)$ --- the LCD is $(x+2)(x-1)$, and combining gives $\frac{5(x-1)+15}{(x+2)(x-1)}=\frac{5(x+2)}{(x+2)(x-1)}=\frac{5}{x-1}$.}
\ansline{With two \emph{constant} numerators over $x+2$ and $x-1$, the combined numerator can never contain the factor}
\ansline{$(x+2)$, so nothing would cancel; one denominator has to carry both factors. (b) $x=-2$ is the hole, $x=1$ is the wall.}
\end{extensionbox}

\begin{spiralbox}
\textbf{Coming next --- Lesson 4.4: The Rational Parent Function \& Transformations.} For four lessons
the rational functions you have met have been \emph{expressions} to simplify. Now they go back on the
grid. Every one of them is a relative of a single parent, $y=\dfrac{1}{x}$ --- the hyperbola with a wall
at $x=0$ and a floor at $y=0$ --- and the same four transformations you used on lines, parabolas, and
absolute values ($f(x)+k$, $f(x+k)$, $kf(x)$, $f(kx)$) apply to it. The new twist: shifting a rational
function \emph{moves its asymptotes}, so the walls and floors you learned to read in 4.0 stop sitting on
the axes.
\end{spiralbox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Grading focus.} Problem 1 and problem 3(c) are the two items that decide whether the lesson
landed: 1(a) and 1(c) have answers with no denominator at all, so a missing restriction there is not a
technicality --- it is a student who thinks the restriction lives in the answer rather than in the
problem. 3(c) is the only item where the numerator cancels, and it is the one to put on the board.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Expect} the lost parenthesis in 1(b) and 3(b), an LCD built as a product in 3(a)/3(e), and
$(x-1)(1-x)$ offered as the LCD in 3(d). Problem 4(c) is worth its own minute: the restriction $x\neq0$
comes from the \emph{main bar}, not from any visible denominator, and it is Lesson 4.2's source~3 in a
new outfit.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Problem 5(d)} is the most interesting result on the page and the one students argue about: the
average speed of the round trip is $12$ mi/h, not $12.5$. The reason is a genuine idea, not a trick ---
the slower leg takes longer, so it gets more weight. If a student insists on $12.5$, have them compute
$60\div5$ and watch the arithmetic settle it.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Problem 6 is the A2.EO.1d item} and the one to discuss out loud. The first pair is fully
equivalent; the second is the pair that looks equivalent and is not, because equivalence of
\emph{expressions} allows the two forms to accept different inputs, while equality of \emph{functions}
does not. That distinction is the whole reason Lesson 4.6 will call a solution extraneous.
\end{teachernote}
