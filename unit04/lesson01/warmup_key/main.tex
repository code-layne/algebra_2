\begin{notesbox}{Getting Ready: Skills We'll Use Today}
\small Three skills, one per idea in today's lesson. Work quickly.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{1. Factor completely} (the Unit~3 toolkit --- GCF first, then count the terms).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item $x^2-9=$ \ans{$(x-3)(x+3)$}
  \item $x^2-x-6=$ \ans{$(x-3)(x+2)$}
  \item $3x^2-12=$ \ans{$3(x-2)(x+2)$} \quad (GCF \emph{first})
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{2. What are you allowed to cancel?} Reduce each numerical fraction, then settle an argument.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item Write top and bottom as products of $6$ and something: $18=6\cdot$ \ans{3} \; and \;
        $24=6\cdot$ \ans{4}, \; so $\dfrac{18}{24}=$ \ans{$\frac34$}
  \item Kai looks at $\dfrac{3+6}{3}$, crosses out both $3$s, and writes $6$. Compute the
        \emph{true} value: $\dfrac{3+6}{3}=$ \ans{3}. \; So Kai is \ans{wrong}.
  \item Complete the rule: you may divide out a common \ans{factor}, but never a
        \ans{term}.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{3. Where is the expression undefined?} (From Lesson~4.0.) For
$\dfrac{x+1}{x^2-4}$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item To find the excluded values, set the \ans{denominator} equal to \ans{0} and solve:
        $x=$ \ans{$2,\ -2$}
  \item Domain, as a union: \ans{$(-\infty,-2)\cup(-2,2)\cup(2,\infty)$}
  \item The numerator is zero at $x=-1$. Is $x=-1$ an excluded value? \ans{No} \;
        Why? \ans{a zero numerator just makes the value $0$}
\end{enumerate}

\end{notesbox}

\begin{teachernote}
Each item is one leg of today's lesson. \textbf{Item 1} is the engine: nothing can be simplified
until both parts are \emph{factored}, and (c) is the reminder that the GCF comes out first.
\textbf{Item 2} is the whole lesson in miniature --- (a) shows that ``cancelling'' is really
dividing out a common \emph{factor} (the $6/6=1$), and (b) kills the year's most stubborn error
numerically before any variables appear: $3$ and $6$ are \emph{terms} of the numerator, so nothing
cancels. Keep (b) on the board; you will point at it again when a student writes
$\frac{x^2+9}{x+3}=x+3$. \textbf{Item 3} carries Lesson~4.0's restriction procedure forward
unchanged --- restrictions come from the \emph{denominator}, and a zero numerator is harmless.
Do \emph{not} yet say that the restrictions survive cancelling; that is today's punch line.
\end{teachernote}
