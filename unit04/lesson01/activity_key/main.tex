\begin{headlinebox}{forestbg}
\small\textbf{Building Polynomials.} Yesterday you \emph{read} polynomials off their graphs; today you
\emph{build} them. Two rules carry everything: \emph{like terms combine, unlike terms do not}, and
\emph{every term times every term}. Write every answer in \textbf{standard form}, and be ready to
justify a sign to your partner.
\end{headlinebox}

\vspace{0.06in}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em, itemsep=9pt]
  \item \textbf{Add.} $(2x^2 + 5x - 3) + (4x^2 - x + 8) = $ \ans{$6x^2 + 4x + 5$}

  \item \textbf{Subtract.} $(7x^2 - 4x + 1) - (3x^2 + 6x - 5)$

    \emph{Step 1 --- rewrite the second polynomial with every sign changed:}

    \vspace{2pt}
    $7x^2 - 4x + 1$ \; \ans{$-\,3x^2 - 6x + 5$}

    \vspace{3pt}
    \emph{Step 2 --- combine like terms:} \ans{$4x^2 - 10x + 6$}

  \item \textbf{Distribute.} $4x(x^2 - 3x + 2) = $ \ans{$4x^3 - 12x^2 + 8x$}

  \item \textbf{Multiply two binomials.} $(x + 6)(x - 2)$. Fill in the box, then collect.
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.9}
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
    \hline
    $\times$ & $x$ & $-2$ \\ \hline
    $x$  & \ans{$x^2$} & \ans{$-2x$} \\ \hline
    $+6$ & \ans{$6x$} & \ans{$-12$} \\ \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    Answer in standard form: \ans{$x^2 + 4x - 12$}
\end{enumerate}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching Proficiency},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em, itemsep=10pt]
  \item \textbf{A vanishing degree.} $(5x^3 - 2x + 9) - (5x^3 + x^2 - 4x + 9) = $ \ans{$-x^2 + 2x$}

    \vspace{2pt}
    Both polynomials have degree $3$, but the answer does not. What is its degree? \ans{2}
    \; Explain what happened.
    \ansline{The $5x^3$ terms are identical, so subtracting cancels them; the constants $9$ and $9$ cancel too. A difference has degree \emph{at most} the larger degree --- never more, sometimes less.}

  \item \textbf{Binomial $\times$ trinomial.} $(3x - 2)(2x^2 - 5x + 4)$
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.9}
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|}
    \hline
    $\times$ & $2x^2$ & $-5x$ & $+4$ \\ \hline
    $3x$ & \ans{$6x^3$} & \ans{$-15x^2$} & \ans{$12x$} \\ \hline
    $-2$ & \ans{$-4x^2$} & \ans{$10x$} & \ans{$-8$} \\ \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    Answer in standard form: \ans{$6x^3 - 19x^2 + 22x - 8$}

  \item \textbf{Special products, two variables.}

    \vspace{2pt}
    (a) $(4x + 3y)(4x - 3y) = $ \ans{$16x^2 - 9y^2$} \qquad (b) $(2x - 5y)^2 = $ \ans{$4x^2 - 20xy + 25y^2$}

  \item \textbf{Predict, don't expand.} For the product $(2x^3 - 1)(-3x^4 + x)$:

    \vspace{2pt}
    Degree of the product: \ans{7} \quad Leading coefficient: \ans{$-6$}

    \vspace{2pt}
    So its left arm points \ans{up} and its right arm points \ans{down}. How did you know
    without multiplying everything out?
    \ansline{Only the two leading terms can produce the highest power: $2x^3\cdot(-3x^4)=-6x^7$. Degrees add ($3+4=7$) and leading coefficients multiply ($2\cdot(-3)=-6$). Odd degree with a negative lead means the arms oppose, right one down.}

  \item \textbf{Error analysis.} A classmate writes $(x + 4)^2 = x^2 + 16$.

    \vspace{2pt}
    (a) Test both sides at $x = 1$: \; left side $=$ \ans{25}, \; right side $=$ \ans{17}.

    \vspace{2pt}
    (b) Write the correct expansion: \ans{$x^2 + 8x + 16$}

    \vspace{2pt}
    (c) In one sentence, tell your classmate exactly what they left out and why.
    \ansline{Squaring a binomial means multiplying it by itself, which gives \emph{four} products, not two --- the two middle ones ($4x$ and $4x$) combine into the middle term $8x$ that was dropped.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier E --- Extension},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{Part 1 --- The open box, expanded.} In Lesson 4.0 you read a table for the open-box volume
$V(x) = x(10-2x)(8-2x)$ but never saw its standard form. Build it now.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=6pt]
  \item Multiply the two binomials first: $(10-2x)(8-2x) = $ \ans{$4x^2 - 36x + 80$}
  \item Now multiply by $x$: \; $V(x) = $ \ans{$4x^3 - 36x^2 + 80x$}
  \item Check against Lesson 4.0's table, which gave $V(1) = 48$. Substitute $x=1$ into your standard
        form: \ans{$4 - 36 + 80 = 48$} \; Does it match? \ans{Yes}
  \item Degree \ans{3}, leading coefficient \ans{4}. The polynomial itself is defined for
        \emph{every} real $x$, but the \emph{box} only exists for $0 < x < 4$. Why?
        \ansline{$x$ is a cut length, so $x>0$; and the shorter flap $8-2x$ must be positive, so $x<4$. Outside that window the ``box'' would need a negative side.}
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Part 2 --- Profit is a subtraction.} A small print shop sells $x$ hundred posters. Its revenue
and cost, in dollars, are
\[
  R(x) = 20x^2 + 150x \qquad\text{and}\qquad C(x) = 12x^2 + 90x + 400 .
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=6pt]
  \item Profit is $P(x) = R(x) - C(x)$. Find $P(x)$ in standard form --- watch every sign.
        \ans{$P(x) = 8x^2 + 60x - 400$}
  \item Evaluate $P(10)$: \ans{$800 + 600 - 400 = 1000$} \; What does that number mean in context?
        \ansline{Selling $x=10$ hundred (that is, 1000) posters produces a profit of $\$1{,}000$.}
  \item The $-400$ in $P(x)$ came from the cost polynomial. What does it represent for the shop?
        \ansline{A fixed cost of $\$400$ that is owed no matter how many posters sell --- at $x=0$ the profit is $-\$400$, so the shop starts in the hole by that amount.}
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Part 3 --- Justify.} Two claims about degree. Decide whether each is \emph{always} true, and
support your decision with an example.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=6pt]
  \item The \emph{product} of a degree-3 polynomial and a degree-4 polynomial always has degree 7.
        \ansline{Always true. Only one pair of terms --- the two leading terms --- can make an $x^7$ term, so there is nothing for it to cancel against, and its coefficient is a product of two nonzero numbers. Example: $(x^3+5)(2x^4-x)=2x^7-x^4+10x^4-5x$, degree 7.}
  \item The \emph{sum} of two degree-4 polynomials always has degree 4.
        \ansline{Not always. The leading terms can cancel: $(x^4+2x)+(-x^4+3)=2x+3$, which has degree 1. A sum or difference has degree \emph{at most} 4.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{teachernote}
Tier~A item~1 and Tier~E Part~3 are the same idea seen twice --- addition can lose degree, multiplication
never can. If a group finishes Tier~A early, send them straight to Part~3 before Part~1; it is the
higher-value reasoning. In Tier~E Part~1(c), insist the check be done in the \emph{expanded} form
(not the factored one) --- substituting into the factored form checks nothing about the expansion.
Part~2(a) is the sign trap in disguise: watch for $8x^2+60x+400$, which means the $-400$ was never
distributed.
\end{teachernote}
