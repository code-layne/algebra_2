\begin{learningtargetbox}
\small
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item \ldots{} predict a polynomial's \textbf{end behavior} from its degree and leading coefficient, and its \textbf{$x$-intercepts} from its factored form.
  \item \ldots{} use the \textbf{multiplicity} of each zero to say whether the graph \emph{crosses}, \emph{touches}, or \emph{flattens and crosses} there.
  \item \ldots{} build a \textbf{sign chart} to decide where a graph lies above or below the $x$-axis, and use the $y$-intercept and the turning-point limit to complete the picture.
  \item \ldots{} \textbf{match} a polynomial equation to the correct graph, and run it backward --- write a possible equation in factored form from a graph's intercepts and $y$-intercept.
  \item \ldots{} state a polynomial's \textbf{domain, range, increasing/decreasing intervals, and relative vs.\ absolute extrema} from its graph.
  \item \ldots{} build a \textbf{polynomial model}, restrict it to its \textbf{feasible domain}, and interpret its values and its maximum in context.
\end{enumerate}
\end{learningtargetbox}

\begin{remindbox}
\small Every tool this unit built was really a piece of one picture. The \textbf{degree and leading
coefficient} decide which way both arms point (4.0). The \textbf{factors} decide where the graph
meets the $x$-axis (4.2--4.4). The \textbf{multiplicity} of each zero decides whether it crosses,
touches, or flattens through (4.0). $f(0)$ gives one exact point, the \textbf{turning-point limit}
$n-1$ caps how many times the graph can change direction, and one test value per interval says
whether the graph is above or below the axis. Put those together and you can recognize a polynomial's
graph before plotting a single point --- or read an equation off a graph you have never seen. The
last step is the point of it all: a polynomial that came from a real situation has a
\textbf{feasible domain}, and only the part of the picture inside it means anything.
\end{remindbox}
