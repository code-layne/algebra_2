\begin{notesbox}{Getting Ready: Skills We'll Use Today}
\small Three skills. The first two you already own. The third is a fact about \emph{equations} that
explains everything strange that happens today.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{1. Restrictions, off the original denominator} (Lesson 4.1). List every excluded value.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item $\dfrac{5}{x-4}$: \; $x\neq$ \ans{$4$}
  \item $\dfrac{x}{2x+6}$: \; $x\neq$ \ans{$-3$}
  \item $\dfrac{7}{x^2-9}$: \; $x\neq$ \ans{$3,\;-3$}
  \item $\dfrac{x+1}{x^2-2x-8}$: \; $x\neq$ \ans{$4,\;-2$}
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{2. The LCD, from factored denominators} (Lesson 4.3). Write the least common denominator.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item $\dfrac{1}{x}$ and $\dfrac{3}{x-2}$: \quad LCD $=$ \ans{$x(x-2)$}
  \item $\dfrac{2}{x+3}$ and $\dfrac{5}{x^2-9}$: \quad LCD $=$ \ans{$(x-3)(x+3)$}
  \item $\dfrac{4}{x}$, \; $\dfrac{1}{x+1}$, \; and the number $3$: \quad LCD $=$ \ans{$x(x+1)$}
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{3. A fact about equations.} Start with a statement that is plainly \textbf{false}:
\[ 5 = 2 \]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item Multiply both sides by $3$. New statement: \ans{$15=6$} \; Is it true or false?
        \ans{false} \\[2pt]
        Could you get back to $5=2$? Yes --- \ans{divide} both sides by $3$.
  \item Now multiply both sides of $5=2$ by $\mathbf{0}$. New statement: \ans{$0=0$} \;
        True or false? \ans{true}
  \item Can you get back to $5=2$ from that? \ans{no} \; Why not? \ans{you would have to divide both
        sides by $0$, which is not allowed}
  \item Finish the rule: multiplying both sides of an equation by a \emph{nonzero} number can always be
        undone, so it never changes which values make the equation true. But multiplying both sides by
        \ans{zero} can turn a \ans{false} statement into a \ans{true} one.
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Item 1} is 4.1, and it is the list students will check every answer against today. Insist on
``off the \emph{original} denominator.'' All unit long, reading restrictions off the simplified form has
been a mislabeled graph feature; starting today it is a \emph{wrong answer}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Item 2} is 4.3's LCD, used in the opposite direction: there it \emph{built} a common denominator
to combine an expression, here it \emph{destroys} every denominator in an equation. Same object,
opposite job --- and 2(c) makes the point that a term with no denominator (the number $3$) is still a
term the LCD has to multiply.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Item 3} is the justification for extraneous solutions (A2.EI.4d), and it costs nothing to
believe because there is no algebra in it. Land one sentence out loud: \emph{multiplying both sides by
something that might be zero can create solutions that were never there.} The LCD is an expression, and
at each excluded value it \emph{is} zero --- which is why the extra answers appear, and why they can
only be the values from item 1. Extraneous solutions are not a mystery or a penalty for sloppiness;
they are the predictable price of a step that is not reversible.
\end{teachernote}
