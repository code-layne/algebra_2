\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=forestbg, colframe=forest, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students can solve a quadratic equation \emph{even when it will not factor
    over the integers}, using two new tools. They apply the \emph{Square Root Property} --- if $x^2=k$ (with
    $k\ge0$) then $x=\pm\sqrt{k}$ --- to any equation they can write as ``(a square) $=$ (a number),''
    remembering the $\pm$ and writing radicals in simplest form. When a linear term is in the way, they
    \emph{complete the square}: add $(b/2)^2$ to manufacture a perfect-square trinomial, rewrite as
    $(x-h)^2=k$, and take square roots. They connect completing the square back to Lesson~2.1's
    \emph{vertex form} $(x-h)^2+k$ (the same move reveals the vertex) and choose sensibly among factoring,
    square roots, and completing the square. Real solutions only here; the negative-radicand case
    ($x^2=-4$) is previewed and deferred to Lesson~2.4 (complex numbers).
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Standards (2023 VA SOL):} \textbf{A2.EI.2b} (solve a quadratic equation in one variable
    algebraically --- here the square-root and completing-the-square methods, restricted to real
    solutions), \textbf{A2.EI.2a} (create a quadratic equation to model a context), and \textbf{A2.EI.2d}
    (verify solutions and interpret them --- the roots are the parabola's $x$-intercepts). Builds on
    \textbf{A2.EO.3d} (the perfect-square-trinomial identity $x^2\pm2kx+k^2=(x\pm k)^2$, run \emph{forward}
    to complete the square) and \textbf{A2.F.2} vertex-form characteristics from Lesson~2.1.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Ideas \& Skills} & \textbf{Key Understandings (the ``why'')} \\[2pt]
\hline
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item Solve $x^2=k$ with the \textbf{Square Root Property}: $x=\pm\sqrt{k}$ (never drop the $\pm$).
  \item Isolate the square first, then root; write answers in \textbf{simplest radical form}.
  \item Solve $(x-h)^2=k$ by taking square roots and undoing the shift.
  \item \textbf{Complete the square}: add $\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2$ to build a perfect-square trinomial.
  \item Rewrite $x^2+bx+c$ as \textbf{vertex form} $(x-h)^2+k$ --- the same move that finds the vertex.
  \item \textbf{Choose a method}: factor if it factors; square roots if there's no $bx$ term; otherwise complete the square.
\end{itemize}
&
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item A number has \emph{two} square roots, so a squared equation has two solutions --- the $\pm$ keeps both.
  \item ``Taking the square root'' is the inverse of squaring, so isolate the square before undoing it.
  \item Completing the square forces the perfect-square pattern from 2.2 to appear \emph{on purpose}.
  \item Half-the-$b$-squared works because $(x+\tfrac{b}{2})^2=x^2+bx+\tfrac{b^2}{4}$.
  \item Every quadratic can be put in vertex form, so \emph{every} quadratic can be solved this way --- factoring is just the lucky shortcut.
  \item The roots are still the parabola's $x$-intercepts, even when they're irrational.
\end{itemize}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{sky}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\textbf{Square Root Property} & If $x^2=k$ and $k\ge0$, then $x=\pm\sqrt{k}$. The engine of the square-root method. \\
\textbf{$\pm$ (plus or minus)} & Records \emph{both} square roots of a number; forgetting it loses a root. \\
\textbf{Simplest radical form} & A radical with no perfect-square factor left inside, e.g. $\sqrt{12}=2\sqrt{3}$. \\
\textbf{Completing the square} & Adding $\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2$ to $x^2+bx$ to make the perfect-square trinomial $(x+\tfrac{b}{2})^2$. \\
\textbf{Perfect-square trinomial} & $x^2\pm2kx+k^2=(x\pm k)^2$; the pattern completing the square manufactures. \\
\textbf{Vertex form} & $a(x-h)^2+k$ with vertex $(h,k)$; completing the square converts standard form into it. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{fixedskillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{forestbg}
    The Warm-Up rehearses exactly what today needs: \textbf{(1)} \emph{simplifying radicals}
    ($\sqrt{12}=2\sqrt{3}$) so square-root answers land in simplest form; \textbf{(2)} \emph{squaring a
    binomial} $(x+5)^2=x^2+10x+25$, noticing the constant is (half the middle coefficient) squared --- the
    pattern we will run forward; and \textbf{(3)} \emph{completing the square} numerically: ``what constant
    makes $x^2+8x+\underline{\ \ }$ a perfect square?'' plus solving $x^2=16$ with \emph{both} roots.
    Debrief item~2 next to item~3: completing the square is item~2 read as a recipe.
\end{fixedskillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{forestbg}
    Put two quadratics side by side:
    \[ x^2-6=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x^2+6x+1=0. \]
    In Lesson~2.2 the tool was factoring --- but neither of these factors over the integers (there are no
    two integers with product $-6$ and sum $0$, nor product $1$ and sum $6$). \textbf{Are they unsolvable?}
    No: the parabolas clearly cross the $x$-axis, so the roots exist --- they're just irrational. Today we
    get them exactly. The first only needs ``undo the square'': $x=\pm\sqrt{6}$. The second has a pesky
    $6x$ in the way, so we \emph{complete the square} to force a perfect square, then undo it. Land the
    theme: \emph{factoring is the lucky shortcut; square roots and completing the square solve the rest.}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{forestbg}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. The Square Root Property.} Anchor the idea: $x^2=9$ asks ``what squares to $9$?'' --- both $3$
\emph{and} $-3$, so $x=\pm3$. In general, if $x^2=k$ ($k\ge0$) then $x=\pm\sqrt{k}$. Stress the $\pm$: a
squared equation has \emph{two} solutions and dropping the sign throws one away.

\textbf{2. Isolate, then root; simplify.} The square must be alone first. $2x^2-50=0\Rightarrow x^2=25
\Rightarrow x=\pm5$. Irrational case: $x^2-6=0\Rightarrow x=\pm\sqrt6$. And $3x^2=36\Rightarrow x^2=12
\Rightarrow x=\pm2\sqrt3$. Reinforce simplest radical form from the warm-up.

\textbf{3. Squares of binomials.} If the square is a \emph{binomial}, the same rule applies:
$(x-3)^2=16\Rightarrow x-3=\pm4\Rightarrow x=7$ or $-1$; $(x+2)^2=12\Rightarrow x+2=\pm2\sqrt3\Rightarrow
x=-2\pm2\sqrt3$. This is the form completing the square will create.

\textbf{4. Preview the wall.} $x^2=-4$ has \emph{no real} solution --- nothing real squares to a negative.
Flag it and defer: Lesson~2.4 builds the numbers that fix this. Today everything stays real.

\columnbreak

\textbf{5. Completing the square (why).} When a $bx$ term blocks the square-root move, manufacture a
perfect square. From the warm-up, $x^2+bx+\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2=\left(x+\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2$. So
add $\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2$ to \emph{both} sides.

\textbf{6. The routine ($a=1$).} Solve $x^2+6x+1=0$: (i) move the constant, $x^2+6x=-1$; (ii) half of $6$
is $3$, $3^2=9$, add to both sides: $x^2+6x+9=8$; (iii) factor the perfect square: $(x+3)^2=8$; (iv) root:
$x+3=\pm2\sqrt2$, so $x=-3\pm2\sqrt2$. Verify against the graph's two $x$-intercepts.

\textbf{7. Completing the square $=$ vertex form.} The same move rewrites the unit anchor:
$x^2-2x-3=(x-1)^2-4$, vertex $(1,-4)$ --- exactly the vertex form from 2.0--2.1. So completing the square
both \emph{solves} and \emph{reveals the vertex}.

\textbf{8. Choose a method \& handle $a\ne1$.} Decide: factors $\Rightarrow$ factor; no $bx$ $\Rightarrow$
square roots; otherwise complete the square (dividing through by $a$ first when $a\ne1$). Preview: 2.5
packages completing the square into the quadratic formula.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: The Completing-the-Square Routine ($a=1$)]{forestbg}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{0.46\linewidth} X@{}}
\textbf{Steps (post and refer back):}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, nosep, itemsep=2pt]
  \item Get $x^2+bx$ alone: move the constant to the right side.
  \item Compute $\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2$ and add it to \emph{both} sides.
  \item Factor the left side as $\left(x+\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2$.
  \item Take $\pm\sqrt{\ }$ of both sides (simplify the radical).
  \item Solve for $x$; write both roots.
  \item Verify: the roots are the parabola's $x$-intercepts.
\end{enumerate}
&
\textbf{Worked example.} Solve $x^2+4x-6=0$.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep, itemsep=1pt]
  \item Move the constant: $x^2+4x=6$.
  \item Half of $4$ is $2$; $2^2=4$. Add $4$: $x^2+4x+4=10$.
  \item Factor: $(x+2)^2=10$.
  \item Root: $x+2=\pm\sqrt{10}$.
  \item $x=-2\pm\sqrt{10}$ ($\approx1.16$ and $-5.16$).
  \item Check: those are the two $x$-intercepts of $y=x^2+4x-6$.
\end{itemize}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
Circulate during the notes practice and Tier work. Watch for and correct:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{dropping the $\pm$}: writing $x=\sqrt{k}$ only and losing the negative root;
  \item taking the square root \emph{before} isolating the square (e.g.\ rooting $2x^2=50$ without dividing by $2$ first);
  \item leaving radicals unsimplified ($\sqrt{12}$ instead of $2\sqrt3$) or mis-simplifying;
  \item in completing the square, adding $\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2$ to \emph{one} side only, or forgetting to move the constant first;
  \item using $b$ instead of $\tfrac{b}{2}$, or squaring the sign wrong when $b$ is negative;
  \item declaring ``no solution'' for an \emph{irrational} root (that's fine --- ``no \emph{real} solution'' is only for a negative under the root, coming in 2.4).
\end{itemize}
Cold-call prompts: ``Why does $x^2=9$ have \emph{two} answers?'' ``What number completes the square for
$x^2-10x$?'' ``$2x^2=50$: what do you do \emph{before} taking a square root?''
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\textbf{Undo the Square} activity (tiered; mirrors the notes).
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate.} Apply the Square Root Property: solve $x^2=k$ and $(x-h)^2=k$ (perfect-square
$k$), keeping both roots, and match the roots to a pre-drawn parabola.

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching.} Isolate-then-root with irrational answers, complete the square on $a=1$
quadratics, and convert a standard-form quadratic to \emph{vertex form} to name the vertex.

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension.} Complete the square when $a\ne1$ (divide first), \emph{choose} the efficient
method with justification, and \emph{model}: a dropped-object height solved by the square-root method.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (independent, no notes): solve $(x-2)^2=18$ by square roots (simplest radical form,
both roots); complete the square to solve $x^2+4x-6=0$ and say what the roots mean on the graph; and an
\textbf{SOL-style multiple-choice} item asking which constant completes the square for $x^2-12x$
(answer $36$). Collect and sort into ``got it / dropped-$\pm$ slip / didn't-isolate slip / used-$b$-not-$b/2$
slip'' to decide whether Lesson~2.4 opens with a re-teach.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} solve by square roots (isolate, simplify radicals, both roots), complete the square on
$a=1$ quadratics, convert to vertex form and name the vertex, choose the smartest method for a mixed set,
and match irrational roots to a pre-drawn graph. \textbf{Extension:} a dropped-object / falling-height
\emph{model} solved by the square-root method (reject the negative time). \textbf{Preview:} Lesson~2.4,
\emph{Complex Numbers} --- what to do when the square-root step hits a \emph{negative} radicand
($x^2=-4$) and there is no real solution.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Every item is a direct prerequisite. Item~1 keeps square-root answers in simplest form ($\sqrt{72}=6\sqrt2$
is the sign-trap). Item~2 is the perfect-square pattern read \emph{forward}; the ``half the middle, then
square'' observation \emph{is} the completing-the-square recipe. Item~3 runs that recipe numerically
(constant $=(b/2)^2$: $16$ and $25$). Item~4 drills the $\pm$ --- the single most common lost point today.
Keep $(x+4)^2$ and $(x-5)^2$ on the board for the notes.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
The two engines: the Square Root Property (\S1--2, always with $\pm$ and simplest radical form) and
completing the square (\S3, the $(b/2)^2$ recipe from the warm-up). \S4 is the payoff --- the \emph{same}
move gives vertex form, so it ties back to the anchor $x^2-2x-3=(x-1)^2-4$, vertex $(1,-4)$, roots $-1,3$.
Pre-empt the four recurring slips: dropping the $\pm$; rooting before isolating (\S1 ``isolate first'');
using $b$ instead of $b/2$; and unsimplified radicals ($\sqrt{20}$ vs.\ $2\sqrt5$ in practice~1). The
$x^2=-4$ ``wall'' is the deliberate bridge to Lesson~2.4 --- name it as ``no \emph{real} solution,'' not
``no solution.''
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
Tier~R keeps radicands to perfect squares so the focus is the $\pm$ and undoing a shift (item~2), plus the
graph link (item~3: $x^2=9\to$B, $(x+2)^2=9\to$C with roots $1,-5$, $x^2=4\to$A). Tier~A item~1 is
``isolate first, then simplify'' ($3x^2-24=0\Rightarrow x^2=8\Rightarrow\pm2\sqrt2$); watch students who
root before dividing. Item~3 is the vertex-form payoff --- verify by expanding. In Tier~E, the $a\ne1$ case
must \emph{divide by $a$ first}; the choose-a-method table rewards recognizing structure (no $bx\Rightarrow$
roots; nice factors $\Rightarrow$ factor; otherwise complete the square). In the model, insist on rejecting
$t=-2.5$ \emph{with a reason}.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
Sort responses into ``got it / dropped-$\pm$ slip / didn't-isolate slip / used-$b$-not-$b/2$ slip.'' The
telltales: item~1 answered $x=2+3\sqrt2$ only (lost the $\pm$) or left $\sqrt{18}$ unsimplified; item~2
adding $4$ to one side only; item~3 choosing \textbf{D} ($144=b^2$, forgot to halve) or \textbf{A}
($6=b/2$, forgot to square). If the $b/2$-vs-$b$ error is widespread, reopen Lesson~2.4 with a $30$-second
completing-the-square recap before introducing $i$.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
Item~1 mixes ``isolate first'' ($4x^2-100=0\Rightarrow x^2=25$) with simplest-radical answers
($(x+3)^2=8\Rightarrow-3\pm2\sqrt2$). Item~2 is pure completing-the-square; the bracketed completed squares
show the checkpoint. Item~3 is the vertex-form tie-back --- verify by expanding. Item~4 rewards method
choice: (a) factors, (b) has no $bx$, (c) needs completing the square. Item~5 is the graph$\leftrightarrow$
algebra check with \emph{irrational} intercepts ($1\pm\sqrt5$). Item~6 is the year's most common slip
(dropping the $\pm$). In the Extension, insist the negative root is rejected \emph{with a reason} and the
$121$ check is shown. The spiral box deliberately sets up Lesson~2.4's $\sqrt{-9}$.
\end{teachernote}
