\begin{headlinebox}{forestbg}
\small\textbf{Making It Rational.} Irrational numbers are stubborn --- but multiply the right two
of them together and the radicals vanish completely. With your partner, hunt for the products that
come out \emph{rational}, and figure out what those problems have in common. Every final answer
must be in simplest radical form with \textbf{no radical in a denominator}. Assume all variables
represent \textbf{positive} real numbers.
\end{headlinebox}

\vspace{0.06in}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{1. Multiply, then simplify.} Insides times insides, outsides times outsides. The first row
is done for you --- notice that the \emph{last} column is where the real work is.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.7}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Problem} & \textbf{One radical} & \textbf{Simplified} \\
\hline
$\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{6}$      & $\sqrt{12}$ & $2\sqrt3$ \\
$\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{8}$      & \ans{$\sqrt{16}$} & \ans{$4$} \\
$\sqrt{3}\cdot\sqrt{6}$      & \ans{$\sqrt{18}$} & \ans{$3\sqrt2$} \\
$\sqrt{5}\cdot\sqrt{5}$      & \ans{$\sqrt{25}$} & \ans{$5$} \\
$2\sqrt{3}\cdot4\sqrt{2}$    & \ans{$8\sqrt{6}$} & \ans{$8\sqrt6$ (already simplest)} \\
$\sqrt[3]{2}\cdot\sqrt[3]{4}$ & \ans{$\sqrt[3]{8}$} & \ans{$2$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Three rows above finished with \emph{no radical at all}. Which three, and what did the three
products have in common? \ansline{$\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt8=4$, \; $\sqrt5\cdot\sqrt5=5$, \; and
$\sqrt[3]2\cdot\sqrt[3]4=2$. In each one the two radicands multiplied to a \emph{perfect power
matching the index} --- $16$ and $25$ are perfect squares under a square root, and $8$ is a perfect
cube under a cube root --- so the entire radicand escaped and nothing was left inside.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{2. Distribute.} Multiply the outside radical by \emph{each} term inside, then simplify each
piece.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{2.0}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X X@{}}
(a) \; $\sqrt3\left(\sqrt3+\sqrt5\right)=$ \ans{$3+\sqrt{15}$} &
(b) \; $\sqrt2\left(\sqrt6-4\right)=$ \ans{$2\sqrt3-4\sqrt2$} &
(c) \; $\sqrt5\left(2\sqrt5+\sqrt{10}\right)=$ \ans{$10+5\sqrt2$} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
{\small\emph{Work:} (a) $\sqrt3\cdot\sqrt3=3$ and $\sqrt3\cdot\sqrt5=\sqrt{15}$. \;
(b) $\sqrt{12}=2\sqrt3$. \; (c) $2\cdot5=10$ and $\sqrt{50}=5\sqrt2$.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{3. What do I multiply by?} Fill in the form of $1$ you need, then finish.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{2.2}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l c l c l@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Start} & & \textbf{Multiply by} & & \textbf{Answer} \\
\hline
$\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$ & $\times$ & $\dfrac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3}$ & $=$ & $\dfrac{\sqrt3}{3}$ \\
$\dfrac{2}{\sqrt7}$ & $\times$ & \ans{$\dfrac{\sqrt7}{\sqrt7}$} & $=$ & \ans{$\dfrac{2\sqrt7}{7}$} \\
$\dfrac{5}{\sqrt5}$ & $\times$ & \ans{$\dfrac{\sqrt5}{\sqrt5}$} & $=$ & \ans{$\sqrt5$} \\
$\dfrac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt2}$ & $\times$ & \ans{$\dfrac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}$} & $=$ & \ans{$\dfrac{\sqrt6}{2}$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
The third row simplifies further than you might expect --- check it.
{\small \emph{(It becomes $\frac{5\sqrt5}{5}=\sqrt5$: the $5$ in front cancels the $5$ underneath.)}}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{4. Conjugates.} Write each conjugate (same terms, opposite middle sign), then multiply the
pair.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.7}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Expression} & \textbf{Conjugate} & \textbf{Product} \\
\hline
$2+\sqrt{7}$          & \ans{$2-\sqrt{7}$} & \ans{$4-7=-3$} \\
$\sqrt{5}-3$          & \ans{$\sqrt{5}+3$} & \ans{$5-9=-4$} \\
$\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{2}$   & \ans{$\sqrt{6}-\sqrt{2}$} & \ans{$6-2=4$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Every product came out with no radical in it. Explain why that always happens. \ansline{The two
middle products are always the same size with opposite signs, so they cancel --- and the two
remaining terms are each a radical multiplied by itself, which erases the radical. All that is left
is (first term)$^{2}$ minus (second term)$^{2}$, and squaring is exactly what undoes a square root.}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching Proficiency},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{1. Multiply and simplify completely.}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{2.0}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
(a) \; $\sqrt6\cdot\sqrt{15}=$ \ans{$3\sqrt{10}$} &
(b) \; $3\sqrt2\cdot5\sqrt{10}=$ \ans{$30\sqrt5$} \\
(c) \; $\sqrt{2x}\cdot\sqrt{8x^{3}}=$ \ans{$4x^{2}$} &
(d) \; $\sqrt[3]{9}\cdot\sqrt[3]{6}=$ \ans{$3\sqrt[3]{2}$} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
{\small\emph{Work:} (a) $\sqrt{90}=\sqrt{9\cdot10}$. \; (b) $15\sqrt{20}=15\cdot2\sqrt5$. \;
(c) $\sqrt{16x^{4}}$ --- the whole radicand escapes. \; (d) $\sqrt[3]{54}=\sqrt[3]{27\cdot2}$.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{2. Expand and simplify.}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{2.2}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
(a) \; $\sqrt3\left(\sqrt{12}-\sqrt6\right)=$ \ans{$6-3\sqrt2$} &
(b) \; $\left(4+\sqrt2\right)\left(3-\sqrt2\right)=$ \ans{$10-\sqrt2$} \\
(c) \; $\left(\sqrt5+\sqrt3\right)^{2}=$ \ans{$8+2\sqrt{15}$} &
(d) \; $\left(2\sqrt7-3\right)\left(2\sqrt7+3\right)=$ \ans{$19$} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
Only one of (c) and (d) came out rational. Circle it, and say in a few words why the other did not.
\ansline{\textbf{(d)} is the rational one. Its two factors are \emph{conjugates}, so the middle
products $+6\sqrt7$ and $-6\sqrt7$ cancel and only $28-9=19$ survives. In (c) both signs are plus,
so the middle products \emph{add} to $2\sqrt{15}$ instead of cancelling, and the radical stays.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{3. Rationalize each denominator.}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{2.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X X@{}}
(a) \; $\dfrac{8}{\sqrt6}=$ \ans{$\dfrac{4\sqrt6}{3}$} &
(b) \; $\dfrac{\sqrt5}{\sqrt{18}}=$ \ans{$\dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{6}$} \\
(c) \; $\dfrac{3}{5+\sqrt2}=$ \ans{$\dfrac{15-3\sqrt2}{23}$} &
(d) \; $\dfrac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt7-\sqrt3}=$ \ans{$\dfrac{\sqrt{21}+3}{4}$} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
{\small\emph{Work:} (a) $\frac{8\sqrt6}{6}$, then reduce by $2$. \; (b) simplify
$\sqrt{18}=3\sqrt2$ first, then $\frac{\sqrt5}{3\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}
=\frac{\sqrt{10}}{6}$. \; (c) conjugate $5-\sqrt2$; denominator $25-2=23$, which shares no factor
with the numerator. \; (d) conjugate $\sqrt7+\sqrt3$; denominator $7-3=4$, numerator
$\sqrt{21}+3$ (note $\sqrt3\cdot\sqrt3=3$).}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{4. Find the mistake.} Each student made exactly one error. Name it and give the correct
answer.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.9}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X X@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Student work} & \textbf{What went wrong} & \textbf{Correct answer} \\
\hline
$\left(\sqrt5+\sqrt2\right)^{2}=5+2=7$ & \ans{Squared each term and dropped the two middle
products, $2\sqrt{10}$ --- the same error as $(x+4)^{2}=x^{2}+16$.} & \ans{$7+2\sqrt{10}$} \\
$\dfrac{2}{\sqrt3}=\dfrac{2\sqrt3}{\sqrt3\cdot\sqrt3}=\dfrac{2\sqrt3}{\sqrt9}=\dfrac{2\sqrt3}{9}$
  & \ans{The method was right; they then evaluated $\sqrt9$ as $9$ instead of $3$.}
  & \ans{$\dfrac{2\sqrt3}{3}$} \\
$\dfrac{5}{2+\sqrt3}=\dfrac{5\left(2+\sqrt3\right)}{\left(2+\sqrt3\right)\left(2+\sqrt3\right)}
  =\dfrac{10+5\sqrt3}{7+4\sqrt3}$ & \ans{Multiplied by the \emph{same} binomial instead of the
  conjugate, so the denominator is still irrational --- nothing was accomplished.}
  & \ans{$10-5\sqrt3$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
{\small\emph{Note on the third row:} the conjugate $2-\sqrt3$ gives a denominator of $4-3=1$, so
$\frac{5\left(2-\sqrt3\right)}{1}=10-5\sqrt3$.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{5. A rectangle with irrational sides.} A rectangle measures $\left(3+\sqrt5\right)$ cm by
$\left(3-\sqrt5\right)$ cm.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item Find its \textbf{area}: \ans{$4$ cm$^{2}$} \quad Is that number rational or irrational?
        \ans{rational}
  \item Find its \textbf{perimeter}: \ans{$12$ cm} \quad Rational or irrational? \ans{rational}
  \item Both sides are irrational, yet neither answer has a radical in it. Explain what happened
        in each case --- they are two \emph{different} reasons. \ansline{\textbf{Area:} the two
        sides are conjugates, so multiplying them cancels the middle terms \emph{and} squares the
        radical away: $9-5=4$. \\[3pt]
        \textbf{Perimeter:} nothing was multiplied at all. Adding gives
        $6+2\sqrt5+6-2\sqrt5$, and $+2\sqrt5$ and $-2\sqrt5$ are \emph{like radicals} that cancel
        as like terms (Lesson 5.2). One reason is about multiplying, the other about adding.}
  \item Now find the area of a \textbf{square} whose side is $\left(3+\sqrt5\right)$ cm:
        \ans{$14+6\sqrt5$ cm$^{2}$} \\
        This one \emph{is} irrational. What is different about it? \ansline{Both factors are
        $3+\sqrt5$ --- the same sign, not opposite signs. The middle products $+3\sqrt5$ and
        $+3\sqrt5$ now \emph{add} to $6\sqrt5$ instead of cancelling, so the radical survives.
        Conjugates are the only pair that clears it.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white, colframe=black!40, title=\textbf{Tier E --- Extension},
    fonttitle=\bfseries, arc=1.5mm, left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm, breakable]
\textbf{Part 1 --- Why the conjugate, and \emph{only} the conjugate.} You are going to rationalize
$\dfrac{1}{3-\sqrt5}$ three times, multiplying top and bottom by three different things. Only one
works.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Multiply by $\dfrac{\sqrt5}{\sqrt5}$. New denominator: \ans{$3\sqrt5-5$} \; Rational?
        \ans{no}
  \item Multiply by $\dfrac{3-\sqrt5}{3-\sqrt5}$. New denominator: \ans{$14-6\sqrt5$} \; Rational?
        \ans{no}
  \item Multiply by $\dfrac{3+\sqrt5}{3+\sqrt5}$. New denominator: \ans{$4$} \; Rational?
        \ans{yes} \; Finish the problem: \ans{$\dfrac{3+\sqrt5}{4}$}
  \item Now prove it in general. Expand $\left(a+\sqrt b\right)\left(a-\sqrt b\right)$ term by term
        and explain why the answer must be rational whenever $a$ and $b$ are rational.
        \ansline{$\left(a+\sqrt b\right)\left(a-\sqrt b\right)
        =a^{2}-a\sqrt b+a\sqrt b-\left(\sqrt b\right)^{2}=a^{2}-b$. The two middle terms are exact
        opposites and cancel, and the last term loses its radical because a square root times
        itself is the radicand. What survives, $a^{2}-b$, is built only from the rational numbers
        $a$ and $b$ using multiplication and subtraction, so it is rational.}
  \item \textbf{Lesson 2.4, revisited.} Back in the quadratics unit you multiplied $a+bi$ by
        $a-bi$ and got $a^{2}+b^{2}$. Today $\left(a+\sqrt b\right)\left(a-\sqrt b\right)=a^{2}-b$.
        Why is one a \emph{plus} and the other a \emph{minus}? \ansline{Both come from
        $a^{2}-(\text{second term})^{2}$. Squaring $bi$ gives $b^{2}i^{2}=-b^{2}$, and subtracting
        a negative makes it $+b^{2}$; squaring $\sqrt b$ gives $+b$, and subtracting that leaves
        $-b$. The sign difference is entirely $i^{2}=-1$ versus $\left(\sqrt b\right)^{2}=+b$.}
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Part 2 --- Rationalizing the \emph{numerator} (this is a calculus problem).} Consider
\[
  E(x)=\frac{\sqrt{x+9}-3}{x}.
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item Try to evaluate $E(0)$. What goes wrong? \ans{$\frac{3-3}{0}=\frac{0}{0}$ --- undefined}
  \item Multiply the top and bottom by $\sqrt{x+9}+3$ --- the conjugate of the \emph{numerator}.
        Show that the top becomes just $x$, and then that the whole expression reduces to
        \[
          E(x)=\frac{1}{\mans{\sqrt{x+9}+3}}.
        \]
        {\small \ans{Top: $\left(\sqrt{x+9}-3\right)\left(\sqrt{x+9}+3\right)=(x+9)-9=x$. \;
        Bottom: $x\left(\sqrt{x+9}+3\right)$. \; The common factor $x$ divides out (legal for every
        $x\ne0$).}}
  \item Fill in the table using \emph{either} form (they are equal for every $x\ne0$). Round to
        four decimal places.
        \begin{center}
        \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
        \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        $x$ & $7$ & $1$ & $0.1$ & $0.01$ \\ \hline
        $E(x)$ & \ans{$0.1429$} & \ans{$0.1623$} & \ans{$0.1662$} & \ans{$0.1666$} \\ \hline
        \end{tabular}
        \end{center}
  \item The values are closing in on a number. Which one? \ans{$\frac16\approx0.1667$} \; Now put
        $x=0$ into \emph{your rationalized form} from (b): \ans{$\frac{1}{3+3}=\frac16$}
  \item The original expression is undefined at $x=0$; the rewritten one is not, and it lands
        exactly on the number the table was approaching. Explain what rationalizing accomplished
        here. \ansline{The original form had a factor of $x$ hidden in the numerator that only
        appeared after the radical was cleared. Once it cancelled the $x$ in the denominator, the
        $\frac00$ problem disappeared --- the two forms agree everywhere except at $x=0$, and the
        new one simply fills in the missing value. Rationalizing did not change the expression; it
        revealed the value the expression had been heading toward all along. This is exactly the
        move calculus uses to evaluate a limit.}
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{Part 3 --- Rationalizing when the index is not $2$.} Yesterday's rule --- \emph{the index
sets the group size} --- decides what you must multiply by.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=5pt]
  \item $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{2}}$: you need \ans{$3$} copies of $2$ under the cube root, and you
        already have $1$, so multiply by $\dfrac{\mans{\sqrt[3]{4}}}{\mans{\sqrt[3]{4}}}$. Answer:
        \ans{$\dfrac{\sqrt[3]{4}}{2}$}
  \item $\dfrac{5}{\sqrt[3]{9}}$: \; multiply by \ans{$\dfrac{\sqrt[3]{3}}{\sqrt[3]{3}}$} \;
        Answer: \ans{$\dfrac{5\sqrt[3]{3}}{3}$}
  \item $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[4]{8}}$: \; multiply by \ans{$\dfrac{\sqrt[4]{2}}{\sqrt[4]{2}}$} \;
        Answer: \ans{$\dfrac{\sqrt[4]{2}}{2}$}
  \item State the rule in one sentence: to rationalize $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[n]{a^{m}}}$, multiply by
        whatever \ansline{brings the exponent under the radical up to the index --- that is,
        multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt[n]{a^{\,n-m}}$, so the radicand becomes $a^{n}$ and the
        whole thing escapes as $a$.}
  \item \textbf{The same thing in exponent notation (Lesson 5.1).} Show that
        $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{2}}=2^{-1/3}$, and that $2^{-1/3}=\dfrac{2^{2/3}}{2}$. Does that agree
        with your answer to (a)? \ansline{$\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]2}=\dfrac{1}{2^{1/3}}=2^{-1/3}$. And
        $\dfrac{2^{2/3}}{2^{1}}=2^{2/3-1}=2^{-1/3}$, so the two are equal. Since
        $2^{2/3}=\sqrt[3]{2^{2}}=\sqrt[3]{4}$, this says $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]2}=\dfrac{\sqrt[3]4}{2}$
        --- the same answer as (a). Rationalizing is just moving a negative exponent up to a
        positive one.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Tier R} (about twelve minutes). Item 1's payoff is the follow-up question, not the table:
students should notice that a product comes out rational exactly when the two radicands multiply to
a perfect power \emph{matching the index}. Item 3 row three ($\frac{5}{\sqrt5}=\sqrt5$) is worth a
share --- students who stop at $\frac{5\sqrt5}{5}$ have the right answer but have not finished, and
that is yesterday's habit again. Item 4 is the first time most groups will say the conjugate
sentence in their own words; harvest one of those sentences for the board.

\textbf{Tier A} item 4 is the highest-value work on the page --- have three groups present. Row 1 is
the middle-term error (point at the Warm-Up). Row 2 is a \emph{correct method} ruined by
arithmetic, which is a useful thing for students to see labelled, since it is not a conceptual
failure. Row 3 is the important one: the student did everything ``in the right shape'' and
accomplished nothing, because only the \emph{conjugate} makes a denominator rational. Item 5 is the
best synthesis question in the activity --- the area and the perimeter both come out rational for
\emph{different} reasons (conjugate multiplication in one, like-radical addition in the other), and
part (d) closes it by showing that changing one sign destroys the effect.

\textbf{Tier E} Part 1 is a genuine ``rule out the alternatives'' argument, and (a) and (b) matter
as much as (c): a student who has watched two reasonable-looking multipliers fail understands why
the conjugate is not arbitrary. Part 2 is the most ambitious thing in the unit so far and is worth
assigning to any group that finishes early --- rationalizing the \emph{numerator} looks backwards
until the $x$ appears on top and cancels, and the table makes the payoff visible before the algebra
explains it. (If a group tries $x=0.000001$ on a calculator and gets a wrong answer, that is real:
subtracting two nearly equal decimals destroys precision, and the rationalized form does not. Worth
mentioning if it comes up.) Part 3 connects directly to the 5.2 exit ticket: any student who
believed $\sqrt[3]{9}$ was unfinished will pick the wrong multiplier here, because it is the same
misconception about group size.
\end{teachernote}
