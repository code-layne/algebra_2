\begin{remindbox}
\small Yesterday two of your functions did something strange: composed in \emph{either} order, they
handed back the $x$ you started with. That was not a coincidence. A pair like that are
\textbf{inverses} --- each one undoes the other --- and $f^{-1}(x)$ is the name of the undoing
function. Read that $-1$ carefully: it is a \emph{label}, not an exponent, and $f^{-1}(x)$ is never
$\frac{1}{f(x)}$. Today you learn to \emph{build} the partner instead of waiting to be handed one.
The method is short --- write $y=f(x)$, swap $x$ and $y$, solve for $y$ --- and the trap is the one
you have been circling all week: to undo a process you reverse the operations \textbf{and the
order}. You do not undo ``double, then add $5$'' by halving first, and you do not undo
$\frac95c+32$ with $\frac59f-32$. Geometrically all of this is one picture: swapping $x$ and $y$
reflects the graph over the line $y=x$, so domain and range trade places and the intercepts swap
too. And then the question this unit opened with finally gets its answer. $y=x^{2}$ sends two
different inputs to the same output, so the swap would give one input two outputs --- which is why
it has to have its domain \textbf{restricted} to $x\ge0$ before $\sqrt{x}$ is allowed to be its
inverse, and why $\sqrt{x}$ gets only half the number line. $y=x^{3}$ never repeats an output, so
$\sqrt[3]{x}$ gets all of it. One difference; every contrast in Lesson 5.0 follows from it.
\end{remindbox}
