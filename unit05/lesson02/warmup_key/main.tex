\begin{notesbox}{Getting Ready: Skills We'll Use Today}
\small Three skills, one per leg of today's lesson. Work quickly.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{1. Fractions --- the fast road.} Do \emph{not} multiply the big numbers out.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item Write every part as a product of primes, divide out what matches, \emph{then} multiply:
        $\dfrac{6}{35}\cdot\dfrac{25}{9}=\dfrac{2\cdot3}{5\cdot7}\cdot\dfrac{5\cdot5}{3\cdot3}=$
        \ans{$\frac{10}{21}$}
  \item To \emph{divide} by a fraction, multiply by its \ans{reciprocal}:
        $\dfrac{4}{9}\div\dfrac{8}{15}=\dfrac{4}{9}\cdot$ \ans{$\frac{15}{8}$} $=$ \ans{$\frac56$}
  \item You may never divide by \ans{0}. So in $\dfrac{4}{9}\div\dfrac{n}{15}$, the one value
        $n$ is not allowed to have is \ans{0}.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{2. Factor completely} (the Unit~4 toolkit --- nothing simplifies until this is done).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item $x^2-9=$ \ans{$(x-3)(x+3)$}
  \item $x^2+2x-8=$ \ans{$(x+4)(x-2)$}
  \item $x^2+3x=$ \ans{$x(x+3)$} \quad (GCF)
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{3. Simplify and restrict} (Lesson~5.1). For $\dfrac{x^2+3x}{x^2-9}$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2.1em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item Factored form: \ans{$\frac{x(x+3)}{(x-3)(x+3)}$} \quad Restrictions: $x\neq$ \ans{$3,\ -3$}
  \item Simplest form: \ans{$\frac{x}{x-3}$}
  \item Which excluded value gives a \emph{hole}? \ans{$x=-3$} \; Why? \ans{its factor cancelled}
\end{enumerate}

\end{notesbox}

\begin{teachernote}
Each item is one leg of today's lesson. \textbf{Item 1} rehearses both operations on numbers, where
students already trust them: (a) is the whole discipline of the day --- \emph{factor, cancel, then
multiply}, never multiply out first --- and (b) names the reciprocal. Item \textbf{1(c)} is the seed of
today's genuinely new idea, planted numerically before it is named: the \emph{numerator} of the divisor
is not allowed to be zero, because a zero divisor is a zero divisor no matter how it is dressed.
Keep 1(c) on the board; you will point back at it when $x\neq2$ appears out of nowhere in the Hook.
\textbf{Item 2} is the engine --- all three factorizations reappear in today's anchor problem.
\textbf{Item 3} carries yesterday's four-step habit forward unchanged; today it becomes step one of
everything. Do \emph{not} yet explain where the divisor's restriction comes from --- that is the Hook's
job.
\end{teachernote}
