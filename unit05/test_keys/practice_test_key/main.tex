\parthead{Part A --- Vocabulary (8 pts)}
Write the letter of the best description next to each term.

\vspace{4pt}
\noindent
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.36\textwidth}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em, itemsep=7pt, label=\arabic*.]
  \item Index \ans{C}
  \item Radicand \ans{A}
  \item Principal root \ans{F}
  \item Conjugate \ans{H}
  \item Extraneous solution \ans{E}
  \item Composition \ans{B}
  \item Inverse function \ans{G}
  \item Anchor point \ans{D}
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.61\textwidth}
\small
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item[(A)] Everything written underneath the radical symbol; when the index is even, it may not be negative.
  \item[(B)] The operation that feeds one function's output into another function's input, written $(f\circ g)(x)$.
  \item[(C)] The small number in the crook of the radical symbol that says which power is being undone.
  \item[(D)] The point $(h,k)$ that positions a transformed radical graph --- an endpoint when the index is even, a turning point when it is odd.
  \item[(E)] A candidate that satisfies the powered-up equation but fails when it is tested in the original.
  \item[(F)] The non-negative value the radical symbol by itself returns; a $\pm$ must be written in by hand.
  \item[(G)] The function that reverses another's inputs and outputs; its graph is the mirror image over the line $y=x$.
  \item[(H)] A binomial with the same two terms but the opposite middle sign; multiplying by it turns an irrational denominator rational.
\end{description}
\end{minipage}

\parthead{Part B --- Multiple Choice (12 pts)}
Circle the best answer.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=9pt]
  \item Written with a rational exponent, $\sqrt[3]{x^5}$ is
    \hfill \textbf{(A) $x^{5/3}$} \ans{$\leftarrow$} \quad (B) $x^{3/5}$ \quad (C) $x^{15}$ \quad (D) $x^{-3/5}$
  \item The domain of $f(x)=\sqrt{7-2x}$ is
    \hfill (A) $x\ge \frac72$ \quad (B) $x\ge -\frac72$ \quad \textbf{(C) $x\le \frac72$} \ans{$\leftarrow$} \quad (D) all real numbers
  \item The graph of $y=\sqrt{3x-12}+5$ begins at the point
    \hfill (A) $(12,5)$ \quad (B) $(4,-5)$ \quad (C) $(-4,5)$ \quad \textbf{(D) $(4,5)$} \ans{$\leftarrow$}
  \item In simplest form, $\sqrt{75}+\sqrt{12}$ is
    \hfill (A) $\sqrt{87}$ \quad \textbf{(B) $7\sqrt3$} \ans{$\leftarrow$} \quad (C) $7\sqrt6$ \quad (D) $10\sqrt3$
  \item Which equation has \emph{no real solution}?
    \\[3pt]\hspace*{1.2em} (A) $\sqrt[3]{x+1}=-4$
    \hspace{1.0cm} (B) $\sqrt{x-1}=7$
    \hspace{1.0cm} \textbf{(C) $\sqrt{x+6}=-2$} \ans{$\leftarrow$}
    \hspace{1.0cm} (D) $\sqrt{x}=0$
  \item If $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ and $g(x)=x+9$, then $(f\circ g)(x)=$
    \hfill (A) $\sqrt{x}+9$ \quad (B) $\sqrt{9x}$ \quad (C) $x\sqrt{x}+9\sqrt{x}$ \quad \textbf{(D) $\sqrt{x+9}$} \ans{$\leftarrow$}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{teachernote}
Item 1: \textbf{denominator = index, numerator = power}. (B) is the signature Unit 5 error --- the
flipped fraction --- and (C) multiplies index by power. Item 2: set the \emph{radicand}
$\ge 0$: $7-2x\ge0$ gives $-2x\ge-7$, and dividing by $-2$ \textbf{flips} the inequality. (A) is the
un-flipped answer. Item 3: factor first --- $\sqrt{3x-12}=\sqrt3\sqrt{x-4}$, so the graph starts at
$x=4$, not $x=12$; (A) is the students who never factored. Item 4: $5\sqrt3+2\sqrt3$; (A) adds
radicands, (C) multiplies them, (D) multiplies the coefficients. Item 5: an isolated \emph{even}
root can never equal a negative number. Distractor (A) is the one to sort for --- a student who
learned ``roots can't be negative'' instead of ``\emph{even} roots can't'' picks it, and that is a
whole-unit failure, not a slip; $\sqrt[3]{x+1}=-4$ solves fine ($x=-65$). Item 6: the function next
to the $x$ runs first. (A) is the order error, (C) reads $\circ$ as multiplication.
\end{teachernote}

\parthead{Part C --- Short Answer \& Computation (40 pts)}
Show your work. Assume every variable is non-negative unless told otherwise.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=10pt]
  \item \textbf{Read the graph.} The radical function $f$ is graphed below; each gridline is one unit
        and the solid dot is the endpoint.
        \begin{center}
        \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.45]
          \lgrid{-3}{10}{-4}{4}
          \begin{scope}
            \clip (-3,-4) rectangle (10,4);
            \draw[very thick, forest, domain=-1:10, samples=120, smooth]
              plot (\x,{sqrt((\x)+1)-2});
          \end{scope}
          \fill[forest] (-1,-2) circle (4pt);
          \foreach \x in {-2,2,4,6,8}
            \draw[charcoal] (\x,-0.15)--(\x,0.15) node[below=1pt, font=\tiny]{$\x$};
          \dpt{0}{-1}
          \dpt{3}{0}
          \dpt{8}{1}
        \end{tikzpicture}
        \end{center}
        (a) Coordinates of the endpoint: \ans{$(-1,-2)$}
        \\[4pt]
        (b) Domain (interval notation): \ans{$[-1,\infty)$}; \quad range: \ans{$[-2,\infty)$}
        \\[4pt]
        (c) $x$-intercept: \ans{$(3,0)$}; \quad $y$-intercept: \ans{$(0,-1)$}
        \\[4pt]
        (d) Interval(s) where $f$ is increasing: \ans{$[-1,\infty)$ --- the whole graph}
        \\[4pt]
        (e) Does $f$ have an absolute maximum or an absolute minimum? Give it and say where it occurs.
        \\[2pt] \ans{An absolute \textbf{minimum} of $-2$, at the endpoint $x=-1$. There is no maximum: the arm climbs forever.}
        \\[4pt]
        (f) End behavior: as $x\to\infty$, $f(x)\to$ \ans{$\infty$};
            \ as $x\to-\infty$, \ans{nothing --- the graph \textbf{stops} at $x=-1$}
        \\[4pt]
        (g) Interval(s) where $f(x)<0$: \ans{$[-1,3)$}

  \item \textbf{Radicals and rational exponents.} Rewrite or evaluate. Leave no negative exponents.
        \\[4pt]
        (a) Write $\sqrt[4]{x^3}$ with a rational exponent. \ans{$x^{3/4}$}
        \\[4pt]
        (b) Write $y^{5/2}$ as a radical. \ans{$\sqrt{y^5}$, or $\left(\sqrt{y}\right)^5$}
        \\[4pt]
        (c) $27^{2/3}=$ \ans{$9$} \hspace{1.2cm}
        (d) $16^{-1/2}=$ \ans{$\frac14$}
        \\[4pt]
        (e) Write $x^{1/2}\cdot x^{1/3}$ as a single radical.
        \ans{Add the exponents: $x^{1/2+1/3}=x^{3/6+2/6}=x^{5/6}=\sqrt[6]{x^5}$. As radicals the two
        factors could not be combined at all --- mismatched indices --- which is exactly why the
        rational-exponent notation exists.}

  \item \textbf{Simplify. Add or subtract where you can.}
        \\[6pt]
        (a) $\sqrt{72}$ \hspace{2.4cm} (b) $\sqrt[3]{54}$
        \\[4pt]
        \ans{(a) Largest perfect square: $\sqrt{36\cdot2}=6\sqrt2$. (Stopping at $2\sqrt{18}$ is true
        but unfinished --- always check the leftover.)\\[3pt]
        (b) The index sets the group size, so hunt for a perfect \emph{cube}:
        $\sqrt[3]{27\cdot2}=3\sqrt[3]{2}$.}
        \\
        (c) $\sqrt{20x^3}$ \hspace{2.1cm} (d) $5\sqrt{12}-\sqrt{27}$
        \\[4pt]
        \ans{(c) $\sqrt{4x^2\cdot 5x}=2x\sqrt{5x}$ --- divide the exponent by the index: $x^3$ gives
        one $x$ out and one $x$ left in.\\[3pt]
        (d) Simplify first, decide second: $5\cdot2\sqrt3-3\sqrt3=10\sqrt3-3\sqrt3=7\sqrt3$. Before
        simplifying these look like unlike radicals.}

  \item \textbf{Multiply, then rationalize.} Every answer must be in simplest form.
        \\[6pt]
        (a) $\sqrt{6}\cdot\sqrt{15}$ \hspace{2.0cm} (b) $\left(2+\sqrt5\right)\left(2-\sqrt5\right)$
        \\[4pt]
        \ans{(a) $\sqrt{90}=\sqrt{9\cdot10}=3\sqrt{10}$. Neither factor simplifies on its own --- the
        \emph{product} does, so the last step is the graded one.\\[3pt]
        (b) Conjugates: $4-5=-1$, a rational number. Compare
        $\left(2+\sqrt5\right)^2=9+4\sqrt5$, where the radical survives.}
        \\
        (c) $\dfrac{6}{\sqrt3}$ \hspace{3.0cm} (d) $\dfrac{4}{3-\sqrt5}$
        \\[4pt]
        \ans{(c) Multiply by $\frac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3}=1$: $\dfrac{6\sqrt3}{3}=2\sqrt3$.\\[3pt]
        (d) Multiply by the \emph{conjugate} $\frac{3+\sqrt5}{3+\sqrt5}$:
        $\dfrac{4\left(3+\sqrt5\right)}{9-5}=\dfrac{4\left(3+\sqrt5\right)}{4}=3+\sqrt5$. Using
        $3-\sqrt5$ again would only produce a new radical.}

  \item \textbf{Transformations.} Let $g(x)=-2\sqrt{x+3}+4$.
        \\[4pt]
        (a) List every transformation that takes $y=\sqrt{x}$ to $g$.
        \ans{left $3$, vertical stretch by $2$, reflection over the $x$-axis, up $4$}
        \\[4pt]
        (b) Anchor point: \ans{$(-3,4)$}
        \\[4pt]
        (c) Domain: \ans{$[-3,\infty)$}; \quad range: \ans{$(-\infty,4]$}
        \\[4pt]
        (d) Is the anchor an absolute maximum or an absolute minimum? \ans{maximum ($a<0$ turns the arm over)}
        \\[4pt]
        (e) A different graph of the form $y=\sqrt{x-h}+k$ begins at $(5,-2)$. Write its equation.
            \ans{$y=\sqrt{x-5}-2$}
        \\[4pt]
        (f) For $p(x)=\sqrt[3]{x-1}+2$, give the anchor point \ans{$(1,2)$} and the domain
            \ans{$(-\infty,\infty)$}. In one sentence, say why that domain is not like $g$'s.
        \\[2pt] \ans{The index $3$ is odd, and cubing keeps the sign, so a cube root forbids no input --- there is nothing to solve, and the anchor is an inflection point rather than an endpoint.}

  \item \textbf{Solve. Test every candidate in the original equation.}
        \\[6pt]
        (a) $\sqrt{2x+3}=x$
        \ans{Square: $2x+3=x^2$, so $x^2-2x-3=0$ and $(x-3)(x+1)=0$, giving candidates $x=3$ and
        $x=-1$. Check $x=3$: $\sqrt9=3$ \checkmark. Check $x=-1$: $\sqrt1=1$, but the right side is
        $-1$, so $x=-1$ is \textbf{extraneous}. Solution: $x=3$ only. (Note the tell --- the
        non-radical side is negative at the rejected value.)}
        \\
        (b) $\sqrt[3]{x-4}=-2$
        \ans{Cube both sides --- legal in both directions, since every real number has exactly one
        real cube root: $x-4=-8$, so $x=-4$. Check: $\sqrt[3]{-8}=-2$ \checkmark. No extraneous risk
        with an odd index.}
        \\
        (c) $\sqrt{3x-2}+4=1$
        \ans{Isolate first: $\sqrt{3x-2}=-3$. An isolated \emph{even} root is never negative, so
        \textbf{no real solution} --- and no algebra was needed. (Squaring anyway gives $x=\frac{11}{3}$,
        a phantom that fails the check.)}
        \\
        (d) $x^{2/3}=25$
        \ans{Raise both sides to the reciprocal power $\frac32$: $x=\pm 25^{3/2}=\pm125$. The
        \emph{numerator} $2$ is even --- it is a square in disguise --- so write the $\pm$ yourself.
        Both check: $(\pm125)^{2/3}=(\pm5)^2=25$ \checkmark}

  \item \textbf{Composition.} Let $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ and $g(x)=x-6$.
        \\[4pt]
        (a) $(f\circ g)(x)=$ \ans{$\sqrt{x-6}$}, \quad domain: \ans{$x\ge6$}
        \\[4pt]
        (b) $(g\circ f)(x)=$ \ans{$\sqrt{x}-6$}, \quad domain: \ans{$x\ge0$}
        \\[4pt]
        (c) Are these the same function? Answer using \emph{both} the rule and the domain.
        \\[2pt] \ans{No. The rules differ --- one shifts the graph right $6$, the other drops it down $6$ --- and so do the domains: $x\ge6$ against $x\ge0$. Order is part of the answer.}
        \\[4pt]
        (d) $(f\circ g)(10)=$ \ans{$\sqrt{4}=2$}
        \\[4pt]
        (e) For $p(x)=2x+1$ and $q(x)=x^2-3$, find $(p\circ q)(-2)$. \ans{$q(-2)=1$, then $p(1)=3$}

  \item \textbf{Inverses.}
        \\[4pt]
        (a) $f(x)=4x-7$. Find $f^{-1}(x)$. \ans{$f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{x+7}{4}$}
        \\[4pt]
        (b) Verify by composition --- \emph{both} orders --- that your answer really is the inverse.
        \ans{$f\!\left(f^{-1}(x)\right)=4\cdot\dfrac{x+7}{4}-7=x+7-7=x$, and
        $f^{-1}\!\left(f(x)\right)=\dfrac{(4x-7)+7}{4}=\dfrac{4x}{4}=x$. Both orders return $x$, so
        they are inverses. One order alone is not a verdict.}
        \\
        (c) $g(x)=\sqrt{x+2}$. Find $g^{-1}(x)$ and state its domain.
        \ans{Swap and solve: $x=\sqrt{y+2}$, so $x^2=y+2$ and $y=x^2-2$. Domain: $x\ge0$, because
        that is the \emph{range} of $g$ --- read it off, never guess it.}
        \\
        (d) The point $(7,3)$ lies on the graph of $g$. What point must lie on the graph of
            $g^{-1}$? \ans{$(3,7)$}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{teachernote}
Item 1(f) is the Unit 5 habit-breaker: ``the graph stops'' is a \emph{complete} answer to the
left-end question, not a missing one --- do not accept ``$-\infty$'' or a blank. Item 1(g): the
endpoint is included, so $[-1,3)$; a student writing $(-1,3)$ has forgotten that $\sqrt0$ is real.
Item 2(e): $x^{5/6}$ alone earns most of the credit, but the item asked for a radical. Item 3: an
answer that is simplified but not \emph{fully} simplified (e.g. $2\sqrt{18}$) is half an answer.
Item 4(d): watch for the $a^2-b^2$ sign flip in the denominator. Item 5(d) is the most-missed --- a
negative $a$ makes the anchor a \textbf{maximum}, and the range flips with it; 5(f) must name the
\emph{index}, not just assert ``all reals.'' Item 6(a) is the graded check: ``$x=3$ and $x=-1$'' as a
final answer earns no credit, because the check is the step being assessed --- and 6(c) should be
answered \emph{before} any squaring. Item 7(c) must cite the domain, not only the rule; ``they look
different'' is not the argument. Item 8(b) is A2.F.2i's justification clause --- both orders, or it
is not a verification.
\end{teachernote}

\parthead{Part D --- Extended Response (12 pts)}
Explain your reasoning in full sentences.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=16pt]
  \item \textbf{The full analysis.} Consider $f(x)=\sqrt{x-1}+2$.
        \textbf{(a)} Give the anchor point, the domain, and the range, both in interval notation.
        \textbf{(b)} Find the $x$-intercept and the $y$-intercept, or explain why one does not exist.
        \textbf{(c)} List the transformations that take $y=\sqrt{x}$ to $f$.
        \textbf{(d)} Find $f^{-1}(x)$ and state its domain, saying exactly where that restriction
        comes from.
        \textbf{(e)} Justify with composition --- both orders --- that $f$ and $f^{-1}$ are inverses.
        \textbf{(f)} A classmate writes $y=(x-2)^2+1$ with \emph{no} restriction and calls it the
        inverse. Explain why that is not the inverse of $f$.
        \ans{(a) Anchor $(1,2)$; domain $[1,\infty)$; range $[2,\infty)$. Both start at the anchor.\\[3pt]
        (b) \textbf{Neither exists.} An $x$-intercept would need $\sqrt{x-1}+2=0$, i.e.
        $\sqrt{x-1}=-2$, and an even root is never negative. A $y$-intercept would need $f(0)$, but
        $0$ is not in the domain --- the graph does not reach the $y$-axis.\\[3pt]
        (c) Right $1$, up $2$. ($a=1$: no stretch, no reflection.)\\[3pt]
        (d) Swap and solve: $x=\sqrt{y-1}+2$, so $x-2=\sqrt{y-1}$, $(x-2)^2=y-1$, and
        $f^{-1}(x)=(x-2)^2+1$ for $x\ge2$. The restriction is the \textbf{range of $f$}, written
        down: whatever $f$ can output is exactly what $f^{-1}$ can accept.\\[3pt]
        (e) $f\!\left(f^{-1}(x)\right)=\sqrt{(x-2)^2+1-1}+2=\sqrt{(x-2)^2}+2=|x-2|+2$, and since
        $x\ge2$ that is $(x-2)+2=x$. Going the other way,
        $f^{-1}\!\left(f(x)\right)=\left(\sqrt{x-1}+2-2\right)^2+1=\left(\sqrt{x-1}\right)^2+1=(x-1)+1=x$
        for $x\ge1$. Both orders return $x$, so the two functions undo each other.\\[3pt]
        (f) Without the restriction it is the \emph{whole} parabola --- two arms --- and it fails the
        horizontal line test, so it cannot be the inverse of anything: it sends both $x=0$ and $x=4$
        to $5$. It also accepts inputs $f$ never produces; $f$ never outputs a number below $2$, so
        $x=0$ has no business being fed to $f^{-1}$. Only the right arm, $x\ge2$, is the inverse.}

  \item \textbf{Modeling.} Ignoring friction, a roller-coaster car that starts from rest and drops
        $h$ feet reaches the bottom at a speed of
        \[ v(h)=8\sqrt{h}\ \text{ feet per second.} \]
        \textbf{(a)} State the domain that makes sense here, and connect it to the rule about what an
        even index will accept.
        \textbf{(b)} Find $v(9)$ and $v(36)$, with units. What does quadrupling the drop do to the
        speed, and which feature of $\sqrt{\,\cdot\,}$ is responsible?
        \textbf{(c)} A car reaches $56$ feet per second at the bottom. Find the height of the drop
        algebraically, and check your answer.
        \textbf{(d)} Write the inverse, $h$ as a function of $v$, and say what it tells an engineer
        that $v(h)$ does not.
        \textbf{(e)} Part (c) required squaring both sides. Explain why there is no danger of an
        extraneous solution in this problem.
        \ans{(a) $h\ge0$, i.e. $[0,\infty)$. The index is even, so the radicand may not be negative ---
        and the context says the same thing twice over, since a drop of negative height is not a
        thing. Here the algebra and the situation agree; that is not always true.\\[3pt]
        (b) $v(9)=8\sqrt9=8\cdot3=24$ feet per second; $v(36)=8\sqrt{36}=8\cdot6=48$ feet per second.
        Quadrupling the drop only \textbf{doubles} the speed, because $\sqrt{4h}=2\sqrt{h}$ --- an
        exponent of $\frac12$ flattens growth, which is the shape of the square root graph.\\[3pt]
        (c) $8\sqrt{h}=56$, so $\sqrt{h}=7$ and $h=49$ feet. Check: $v(49)=8\sqrt{49}=8\cdot7=56$
        \checkmark\\[3pt]
        (d) Solve for $h$: $\sqrt{h}=\frac{v}{8}$, so $h=\dfrac{v^2}{64}$ for $v\ge0$. The original
        answers ``how fast will this drop make the car go?''; the inverse answers the question an
        engineer actually asks --- ``how tall must I build the drop to hit a target speed?''\\[3pt]
        (e) An extraneous root only appears when squaring hides a \emph{sign} disagreement --- the
        step $a^2=b^2$ forgets whether $a=b$ or $a=-b$. Here both sides are non-negative:
        $\sqrt{h}\ge0$ and a speed of $56$ is positive, so there is no hidden sign to lose and the
        single candidate must be genuine. Checking it is still good practice.}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{Scoring Part D (6 pts each).} D1: 1 pt (a) anchor with \emph{both} interval-notation answers;
1 pt (b) \emph{both} non-existences with reasons --- ``no $x$-intercept'' with no reason earns half;
1 pt (c) both transformations; 1 pt (d) the rule \emph{and} $x\ge2$ \emph{and} the sentence that it is
the range of $f$ (no credit for the restriction if it is guessed as $x\ge0$, the reflex answer);
1 pt (e) both compositions --- one order is not a verification, and this is the standard's own
``justify'' clause; 1 pt (f) the horizontal line test or the two-arms argument. A student who writes
$(x-2)^2+1$ with no restriction in (d) and then cannot explain (f) has missed the unit's closing
idea --- score both accordingly. D2: 1 pt (a) $h\ge0$ tied to the even index; 1 pt (b) both values
with units \emph{and} ``doubles'' with $\sqrt{4h}=2\sqrt{h}$ (half credit for ``it doubles'' with no
reason); 1 pt (c) $h=49$ ft; 1 pt the check; 1 pt (d) $h=\frac{v^2}{64}$; 1 pt (e) the sign argument.
Do not accept ``because I checked it'' for (e) --- the question asks why the danger was never there.
\end{teachernote}
