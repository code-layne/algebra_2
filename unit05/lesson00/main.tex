\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=forestbg, colframe=forest, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students can read the graphs of the two radical parents ---
    $y=\sqrt{x}$ and $y=\sqrt[3]{x}$, taught side by side --- and name every characteristic: the
    \emph{domain}, which for an \emph{even} index must be computed by solving \emph{radicand}
    $\ge 0$; the \emph{endpoint} where a square root graph begins and the \emph{absolute extremum}
    it always produces; and the \emph{range}, \emph{intercepts}, \emph{increasing/decreasing} and
    \emph{positive/negative} intervals, and \emph{end behavior} --- including the honest answer that
    a square root graph has \emph{no} second end, it simply stops. Students also explain why the two
    parents look so different (the index), and where each came from: $y=\sqrt{x}$ is $y=x^2$
    restricted to $x\ge0$ and reflected over $y=x$, while $y=\sqrt[3]{x}$ is \emph{all} of $y=x^3$
    reflected the same way --- the \emph{inverse relationship} that Lessons~5.6--5.7 will formalize.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Standards (2023 VA SOL):} \textbf{A2.F.2} --- investigate and analyze the characteristics
    of square root and cube root functions graphically and algebraically. This Lesson~0
    \emph{introduces} the two rows the \S3 spine marks new for Unit~5: the \textbf{restricted domain
    from the radicand} and the \textbf{inverse relationship of families}. It covers
    \textbf{A2.F.2a} (domain, range, zeros, intercepts), \textbf{A2.F.2c} (increasing/decreasing
    intervals), \textbf{A2.F.2d} (absolute maxima and minima --- the endpoint is one), \textbf{A2.F.2f}
    (evaluate $f(x)$ and interpret it in context --- the falling-object and skid-mark models), and
    \textbf{A2.F.2g} (end behavior). \textbf{A2.F.2b} (compare and contrast the characteristics of
    function families) drives the square-root-vs.-cube-root table and the contrast with Units~4--5.
    \textbf{A2.F.1a} (distinguish between the graphs of the parent functions) is the lesson's
    backbone, and \textbf{A2.F.1e} (compare and contrast graphs, tables, and equations) is assessed in
    the Activity Tier~A table. The \emph{inverse} strand previews \textbf{A2.F.2i/j} without yet
    requiring the algebraic method.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|X@{}}
\textbf{Priority Ideas \& Skills} & \textbf{Key Understandings (the ``why'')} \\[2pt]
\hline
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item Identify the \textbf{index} and \textbf{radicand}, and use the index to decide whether the domain is restricted.
  \item Find the \textbf{domain} of an even-index radical by solving \textbf{radicand} $\ge 0$ --- including the sign-flip case that opens the graph to the \textbf{left}.
  \item Locate the \textbf{endpoint} and name the \textbf{absolute extremum} it produces (min or max, depending on direction).
  \item Read \textbf{range}, \textbf{intercepts}/\textbf{zeros}, \textbf{increasing/decreasing} and \textbf{positive/negative} intervals off both radical parents.
  \item State \textbf{end behavior} --- and say plainly when there is no second end.
  \item Explain the \textbf{inverse relationship}: $\sqrt{x}$ with $x^2$ (restricted), $\sqrt[3]{x}$ with $x^3$; reflection over $y=x$; domain/range swap.
  \item \textbf{Compare and contrast} square root with cube root, and both with polynomial and rational.
\end{itemize}
&
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item \emph{The index runs the show.} Even refuses negatives; odd accepts everything. Every difference between the two parents traces back to that one fact.
  \item A polynomial was defined everywhere; a rational function lost scattered inputs. A square root loses an entire \textbf{half} of the number line at once.
  \item Domain is no longer something you \emph{read} --- it is something you \emph{solve for}. That is new this unit.
  \item An endpoint is included in the domain (solid dot), and since the graph leaves it in only one direction, it is automatically the highest or lowest point.
  \item ``End behavior'' has had two answers all year. Today one of them is \emph{the graph stops} --- and that is a complete answer, not a missing one.
  \item Square rooting is undoing squaring. That is why $\sqrt{x}$ accepts exactly the outputs a square can produce, and why $y=x^2$ must be cut in half to be undone at all.
\end{itemize}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{sky}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\textbf{Radical function} & A function with its variable under a radical, $f(x)=\sqrt[n]{\text{expression in }x}$. \\
\textbf{Index} & The small $n$ in the notch of the radical --- which root is being taken. A plain $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ has index $2$. \\
\textbf{Radicand} & Everything underneath the radical symbol. \\
\textbf{Even-index rule} & The radicand may not be negative, so the domain is the solution set of \emph{radicand} $\ge 0$. No real number squares to a negative. \\
\textbf{Odd-index rule} & No restriction: cubing preserves sign, so $\sqrt[3]{-8}=-2$ and the domain is all reals. \\
\textbf{Endpoint} & The single point where a square root graph begins; included in the domain, drawn as a solid dot, and always an absolute maximum or minimum. \\
\textbf{Square root parent} & $y=\sqrt{x}$: domain and range $[0,\infty)$, endpoint $(0,0)$, increasing, absolute min $0$, one arm, no symmetry. \\
\textbf{Cube root parent} & $y=\sqrt[3]{x}$: domain and range all reals, no endpoint, increasing everywhere, no extrema, two arms, origin (odd) symmetry. \\
\textbf{Inverse relationship} & Two functions that undo each other; inputs and outputs trade places, graphs reflect over $y=x$, and domain and range swap. \\
\textbf{Restricted domain (for an inverse)} & $y=x^2$ sends two inputs to one output, so half of it must be discarded ($x\ge0$) before it can be undone by $\sqrt{x}$. $y=x^3$ needs no such cut. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{fixedskillbox}[Activate Prior Knowledge \& Spiral Review]{forestbg}
    The Warm-Up rehearses the three skills this lesson stands on, one per new idea: \textbf{(1)}
    evaluating $\sqrt{36}$, $\sqrt{-36}$, $\sqrt[3]{8}$, and $\sqrt[3]{-8}$ --- so students meet the
    even/odd index split \emph{numerically}, and discover for themselves that exactly one of the four
    has no real answer; \textbf{(2)} solving $x-3\ge0$, $2x+8\ge0$, and $5-x\ge0$ --- the Unit~1
    inequality toolkit, reused as this unit's domain engine, with the third item planting the
    sign-flip that will produce a graph opening to the \emph{left}; and \textbf{(3)} completing
    tables for $y=x^2$ (from $x=0$) and $y=\sqrt{x}$ and noticing that the two are the same table with
    the rows traded. Debrief all three aloud, but do \emph{not} supply the words ``index,''
    ``radicand,'' or ``inverse'' --- let the Hook graphs name them.
\end{fixedskillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{forestbg}
    Put the two parent graphs on the board side by side: $y=\sqrt{x}$ and $y=\sqrt[3]{x}$. Open with
    the observation that reframes the unit: \textbf{``These are both radical functions. One of them
    covers the entire plane from left to right. The other stops dead at the origin and never appears
    to the left of it. What is different about them?''} Let students hunt --- most will say ``the
    little 3'' before they can say why. Then pull the answer out of the Warm-Up: \textbf{``Item 1
    already told you. Why did $\sqrt{-36}$ fail when $\sqrt[3]{-8}$ was fine?''} (Nothing real squares
    to a negative; cubing keeps the sign.) Press once more: \textbf{``So which inputs are missing from
    the square root graph, and how many are we losing?''} (Every negative --- half the number line at
    once.) Contrast that deliberately with Unit~4: \emph{a rational function lost a few scattered
    inputs where the denominator was zero; this loses an entire half-line, and the graph does not
    break --- it simply begins somewhere.} Land the point: \emph{for every family so far we asked what
    the graph does. Radical functions make us ask something new first --- where is the graph allowed to
    exist at all? --- and the answer is written under the radical.}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{forestbg}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. Index, radicand, and where the graph is allowed to exist.} Name the two parts
($\sqrt[n]{\ }$: the \textbf{index} $n$, the \textbf{radicand} underneath) and build the two-row rule
table from the Warm-Up: even index $\Rightarrow$ radicand cannot be negative; odd index $\Rightarrow$
anything goes, because $(-2)^3=-8$. Then state the procedure the whole unit rests on: \emph{if the
index is even, set the radicand $\ge0$ and solve}. Work $\sqrt{x-3}$, $\sqrt{2x+8}$, $\sqrt{5-x}$
(flip! --- and flag that this one runs \emph{left}), and $\sqrt[3]{x-3}$ (no work needed). Make the
comparison explicit: domain used to be something students \emph{read}; now it is something they
\emph{solve for}.

\textbf{2. The square root parent.} Build $y=\sqrt{x}$ from the table $(0,0),(1,1),(4,2),(9,3)$ and
read every feature: domain and range $[0,\infty)$, the \textbf{endpoint} $(0,0)$ drawn \emph{solid}
(that input \emph{is} in the domain), increasing on all of $[0,\infty)$, an \textbf{absolute minimum}
of $0$, no absolute maximum, no symmetry. Spend real time on end behavior: as $x\to+\infty$ it climbs
forever but \emph{slowly}; as $x\to-\infty$ --- \textbf{nothing}. The graph stops. Say out loud that
this is the first family of the year for which one of the two end-behavior questions has no answer,
and that ``it stops'' is the complete, correct answer.

\columnbreak

\textbf{3. The cube root parent.} Same treatment for $y=\sqrt[3]{x}$ from
$(-8,-2),(-1,-1),(0,0),(1,1),(8,2)$, and let every answer come out different: domain \emph{and} range
all reals, \emph{no} endpoint, increasing everywhere, \textbf{no extrema at all}, and \textbf{origin
symmetry} --- verified algebraically ($\sqrt[3]{-8}=-2=-\sqrt[3]{8}$, so $f(-x)=-f(x)$, the odd
function test from Unit~3). Two arms, like an odd-degree polynomial, but \emph{flattening} rather than
steepening.

\textbf{4. Where the parents come from --- the inverse relationship.} Return to Warm-Up item~3: the
two tables were the same table with the rows swapped. Name it: squaring and square-rooting
\textbf{undo} each other, and on a graph undoing is a \textbf{reflection over $y=x$}. Draw both pairs.
Then get the payoff: the domain of $\sqrt{x}$ is $[0,\infty)$ \emph{because that is the range of}
$x^2$. Ask why only half the parabola is drawn --- $(-3)^2=3^2=9$, so reflecting all of it fails the
vertical line test, and $y=x^2$ needs a \textbf{restricted domain} to be undone at all. Contrast
$y=x^3$, which never repeats an output and so needs no cut. Say plainly that Lessons 5.6--5.7 turn
this into a method.

\textbf{5. The full feature list, and the contrast.} Read every characteristic off the anchor
$f(x)=\sqrt{x+4}-1$ (endpoint $(-4,-1)$, domain $[-4,\infty)$, range $[-1,\infty)$, zero $(-3,0)$,
$y$-int $(0,1)$, increasing throughout, negative on $[-4,-3)$, absolute min $-1$, one arm). Finish
with the \textbf{A2.F.2b} table comparing the two radical families row by row, and one sentence
against the earlier units: a polynomial was defined everywhere with no edges; a rational function lost
scattered inputs and gained asymptotes; a radical function is the first whose graph can simply
\emph{begin somewhere and stop}. Preview Lesson~5.1, \emph{$n$th roots \& rational exponents}.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
Circulate during the notes practice and Tier work. Watch for and correct:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep]
  \item writing a restricted domain for a \emph{cube} root --- the student has learned ``radical $\Rightarrow$ restriction'' instead of ``\emph{even index} $\Rightarrow$ restriction''; this is the signature error of the lesson;
  \item solving $5-x\ge0$ as $x\ge5$ --- forgetting the flip, and therefore drawing the graph opening the wrong way (send them back to Warm-Up item~2c);
  \item inventing a left arm: answering ``$f\to-\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$'' on a square root graph instead of ``the graph stops'';
  \item hunting for a $y$-intercept on a graph whose domain excludes $0$ (e.g.\ $\sqrt{x-1}$), and being unwilling to write ``none'';
  \item calling every endpoint a minimum out of reflex --- on $y=-\sqrt{x}+3$ it is a \emph{maximum}; the direction of the arm decides;
  \item writing an interval that leaks outside the domain (``increasing on $(-\infty,\infty)$'' for $\sqrt{x+4}-1$);
  \item drawing the endpoint as an open circle --- it is included, because the radicand is $0$ there and $\sqrt{0}=0$ is perfectly real.
\end{itemize}
Cold-call prompts: ``What is the index, and does it restrict anything?'' ``What did you set greater
than or equal to zero, and what did you get?'' ``Which direction does the arm leave the endpoint --- so
is that a max or a min?'' ``What happens on the left side of this graph? Say it exactly.''
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\textbf{Reading Radical Graphs} activity (tiered; mirrors the notes).
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate.} From one pre-drawn graph of $g(x)=\sqrt{x-1}$: identify the radicand
and solve for the domain, name the endpoint and explain why its dot is solid, give the range and
$x$-intercept, then \emph{attempt} $g(0)=\sqrt{-1}$ and conclude there is \textbf{no} $y$-intercept.
Closes on end behavior in both directions --- with the left one required to be answered ``the graph
stops.''

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching.} An eight-row feature table for two functions chosen so that
\emph{every row differs}: $p(x)=2-\sqrt{x}$ (endpoint $(0,2)$, decreasing, absolute \emph{max}) beside
$q(x)=\sqrt[3]{x}-1$ (all reals, no endpoint, no extrema). Then two \emph{justifications}: point to
the feature of each equation that causes the domain difference, and explain in one breath why $p$ has
a max, $y=\sqrt{x}$ has a min, and $q$ has neither.

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension.} Equation-only work: a four-row table (index / radicand condition /
domain) over $\sqrt{x-7}$, $\sqrt[3]{x+5}$, $\sqrt{12-3x}$, and $\sqrt{2x+1}$, plus ``which one opens
left, and how do you know before graphing?'' Then a falling-object model $t(d)=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{4}$:
domain from algebra \emph{and} context, the endpoint's meaning, why quadrupling the height only doubles
the time, solving for $d=16t^2$, and recognizing it as the \textbf{inverse} --- then explaining why
only $t\ge0$ survives, which is the same cut $y=x^2$ needed in the notes.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit Ticket} (independent, no notes): from one graphed radical function
$f(x)=\sqrt{x+1}-2$, give the domain and range in interval notation, the endpoint, both intercepts,
the increasing interval, and the absolute extremum (naming which kind and noting that the other does
not exist); then \textbf{explain} why $f$ has a restricted domain while $\sqrt[3]{x+1}-2$ would not,
\emph{and} what happens as $x\to-\infty$; then an \textbf{SOL-style multiple-choice} item on the domain
of $h(x)=\sqrt{5-2x}$ (answer $x\le\frac52$) whose distractors each break exactly one idea (forget the
inequality flip / mishandle the signs when isolating $x$ / treat an even index as unrestricted).
Collect and sort into ``got it / sign-flip slip / invented-a-left-arm / even-vs-odd-index confusion''
to decide whether Lesson~5.1 opens with a re-teach --- the last pile matters most, since 5.1 is built
entirely on the even/odd index distinction.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework:} a four-row equation-only table (index / radicand condition / domain / which way it
opens) over $\sqrt{x+6}$, $\sqrt[3]{x-4}$, $\sqrt{3x-9}$, and $\sqrt{8-4x}$; a full feature read off
$m(x)=-\sqrt{x}+3$, deliberately a \emph{falling} square root whose endpoint is an absolute
\textbf{maximum}; a matched full feature read off the cube root $n(x)=\sqrt[3]{x}+1$, closing with
whether $n$ still has origin symmetry (it does not --- the shift moved the center to $(0,1)$); and one
justification item on why a square root graph always has exactly one absolute extremum while a cube
root graph has none. \textbf{Extension:} interpret a skid-mark model $s(d)=\sqrt{24d}$ --- domain from
the radicand and from context, the endpoint at $(0,0)$ together with the model's honest limitation
(anti-lock brakes leave no marks), why quadrupling the skid only doubles the speed estimate, solving
for $d=\frac{s^2}{24}$ and naming it the \textbf{inverse}, and why the negative-speed half is discarded
--- the same restriction $y=x^2$ required in class. \textbf{Preview:} Lesson~5.1, \emph{$n$th Roots \&
Rational Exponents} --- what an index really is ($\sqrt[n]{a}=a^{1/n}$, so $a^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}$),
which hands every exponent rule to radicals and gives ``why does an even index refuse a negative?''
its full algebraic answer.
\end{skillbox}
