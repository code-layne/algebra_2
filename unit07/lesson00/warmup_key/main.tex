\begin{notesbox}{Getting Ready: Skills We'll Use Today}
\small These three skills power today's lesson on reading exponential graphs. Work quickly.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{1. Powers, including the strange exponents.} Evaluate each one. Give exact fractions.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l l@{}}
(a) \; $2^{3}=$ \ans{8} & (b) \; $2^{1}=$ \ans{2} &
(c) \; $2^{0}=$ \ans{1} & (d) \; $2^{-1}=$ \ans{$\frac12$} \\
(e) \; $2^{-2}=$ \ans{$\frac14$} & (f) \; $2^{-4}=$ \ans{$\frac{1}{16}$} &
(g) \; $2^{-8}=$ \ans{$\frac{1}{256}$} & (h) \; $5^{0}=$ \ans{1} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Look at (d) through (g). As the exponent gets more and more negative, the answers get
\ans{smaller and smaller --- but stay positive}. Could $2^{x}$ ever be \emph{exactly} $0$? Explain.
\ansline{No. A negative exponent makes a \emph{fraction}, not a negative number: $2^{-n}=1/2^{n}$.
That fraction can be made as small as you like, but a fraction with $1$ on top is never $0$.}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{2. Two tables, two different patterns.} Fill in the last entry of each, then say what you
\emph{do} to one $y$-value to get the next one.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
\multicolumn{6}{|c|}{\small Table A} \\ \hline
$x$ & $0$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$ & $4$ \\ \hline
$y$ & $2$ & $5$ & $8$ & $11$ & \ans{14} \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\hspace{0.2in}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
\multicolumn{6}{|c|}{\small Table B} \\ \hline
$x$ & $0$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$ & $4$ \\ \hline
$y$ & $2$ & $6$ & $18$ & $54$ & \ans{162} \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Table A: each $y$ is the one before it \ans{plus $3$ (add)}. \quad
Table B: each $y$ is the one before it \ans{times $3$ (multiply)}.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{3. A rumor.} Three people hear a rumor at hour $0$. Every hour after that, the number of
people who have heard it \textbf{doubles}. Complete the table.
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
Hour $t$ & $0$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$ & $4$ \\ \hline
People & $3$ & \ans{6} & \ans{12} & \ans{24} & \ans{48} \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
How many people had heard it at hour $0$? \ans{3} \quad What are you multiplying by each
hour? \ans{2} \\
Which of the two tables in item 2 does this rumor behave like? \ans{Table B}

\end{notesbox}

\begin{teachernote}
Each item seeds one of today's new ideas, so debrief all three aloud --- but do \emph{not} yet supply
the words ``exponential,'' ``growth factor,'' or ``asymptote.'' Let the Hook graphs earn them.

\textbf{Item 1} is the entire asymptote story in miniature. Students often expect a negative exponent
to produce a negative answer; (d)--(g) kill that on the spot and show the outputs shrinking toward
zero while staying \emph{positive}. That is exactly why today's graphs have a floor they never reach,
and why the range is $(0,\infty)$ rather than $[0,\infty)$. Item (c) and (h) plant the $y$-intercept:
\emph{every} base to the zero power is $1$.

\textbf{Item 2} is the family fingerprint. Table A adds a constant (linear --- Lesson 2.0); Table B
multiplies by a constant. Push for the word \emph{multiply}, not ``it grows fast'' --- the whole point
is that the operation, not the speed, identifies the family. Section 1 of the notes completes the
three-way test by adding the quadratic case (constant \emph{second} difference, Lesson 3.0).

\textbf{Item 3} plants $y=ab^{x}$ without the notation: the value at hour $0$ is the initial amount
$a=3$ and the doubling is the constant ratio $b=2$. Ask ``what would hour $-1$ have been?'' if you
want an early laugh at $1.5$ people --- the model runs left too, which is what makes the left arm of
the graph legal.
\end{teachernote}
