\begin{objectivebox}
\textbf{By the end of this lesson, I will be able to\dots}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item solve an exponential equation by rewriting both sides as powers of \emph{one} base and then setting the exponents equal;
  \item explain \emph{why} that move is legal --- an exponential function never produces the same output twice, so matching bases forces the exponents to match;
  \item read a solution off a graph as the point where a horizontal line crosses the curve, and decide from the \emph{range} when an exponential equation has no solution at all.
\end{itemize}
\end{objectivebox}

\vspace{0.05in}

\begin{vocabbox}
\small
Fill in each term as we build it together.
\par\vspace{2pt}   % \par is required: \vocabans's \noindent is a no-op mid-paragraph
\vocabans{Exponential equation}{an equation with the variable in an exponent, such as $2^{x}=32$ or $9^{\,x-2}=27^{x}$}
\vocabans{Common base}{one base that \emph{both} sides of the equation can be rewritten as a power of; finding it is Step 1}
\vocabans{One-to-one property of exponents}{if $b^{m}=b^{n}$ with $b>0$ and $b\neq1$, then $m=n$ --- true because an exponential never repeats an output}
\vocabans{Power of a power, $\left(b^{m}\right)^{n}=b^{mn}$}{the Unit 6 rule that turns $\left(2^{3}\right)^{x}$ into $2^{3x}$; the exponents \emph{multiply}, they do not add}
\vocabans{Solution as an intersection}{the solution of $b^{x}=c$ is the $x$-coordinate where the curve $y=b^{x}$ meets the horizontal line $y=c$}
\end{vocabbox}

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item Solve $x^{2}=4$: \; $x=$ \ans{$\pm2$} \qquad Solve $2^{x}=4$: \; $x=$ \ans{2}
  \item The gold line is at the same height in both pictures. Why does it hit one curve twice and the other only once? \ansline{The parabola turns around, so it uses the output $4$ on the way up and again on the way down; the exponential only ever climbs.}
  \item Which fact about the \emph{shape} of an exponential graph (Lesson 7.0) guarantees the right-hand picture can never look like the left-hand one? \ansline{It has no turning points --- it is always increasing or always decreasing, so no output is ever repeated.}
\end{itemize}
Everything today rests on that last answer: \textbf{an exponential function never uses the same output
twice, so if the bases match, the exponents have no choice but to match too.}

\begin{notesbox}{1. Why You Are Allowed to Drop the Base}
An \textbf{exponential equation} is an equation with the variable in an \ans{exponent}.

\vspace{4pt}
Lesson 7.0 established that an exponential graph has \textbf{no turning points} --- it is always
increasing or always decreasing, never both. A function like that can never come back to an output it
has already used, so a horizontal line crosses it \ans{one} time(s) at most.

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{center}
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=goldbg, colframe=goldacc, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=1.5mm, width=0.90\linewidth,
    left=3mm, right=3mm, top=2mm, bottom=2mm]
\centering
\textbf{One-to-One Property of Exponents} \\[3pt]
If \; $b^{\,m} = b^{\,n}$ \; and \; $b>0$, $b\neq1$, \qquad then \; $m=n$.
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{center}

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{The fine print is not decoration.} Lesson 7.1 told you an exponential model needs $b>0$ and
$b\neq1$. Here is what goes wrong when $b=1$: \; $1^{5}=$ \ans{1} \; and \; $1^{9}=$
\ans{1}, \; so $1^{5}=1^{9}$ even though $5\neq9$. With $b=1$ the graph is a flat line, which
repeats \emph{every} output --- matching exponents would be nonsense. The restriction you first met as
a rule about \emph{models} turns out to be exactly the condition that makes today's \emph{solving}
legal.

\vspace{3pt}
\emph{And $b>0$?} A negative base breaks at fractional exponents: $(-4)^{1/2}=\sqrt{-4}$ is not a real
number (Unit 6).
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{2. Getting the Bases to Match}
The property applies only when the two sides are powers of the \textbf{same base}, so most of the work
in this lesson is the rewriting you did in the Warm-Up. Fill in every exponent.

\vspace{3pt}
\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
\textbf{Powers of $2$} & $4$ & $8$ & $16$ & $32$ & $64$ & $\sqrt{2}$ \\
\textbf{exponent on $2$} & \ans{2} & \ans{3} & \ans{4} & \ans{5} & \ans{6} & \ans{$\frac12$} \\
\hline
\textbf{Powers of $3$} & $9$ & $27$ & $81$ & $\frac13$ & $\frac19$ & $\frac{1}{27}$ \\
\textbf{exponent on $3$} & \ans{2} & \ans{3} & \ans{4} & \ans{$-1$} & \ans{$-2$} & \ans{$-3$} \\
\hline
\textbf{Powers of $5$} & $25$ & $125$ & $\frac15$ & $\frac{1}{25}$ & $\sqrt{5}$ & $\sqrt[3]{5}$ \\
\textbf{exponent on $5$} & \ans{2} & \ans{3} & \ans{$-1$} & \ans{$-2$} & \ans{$\frac12$} & \ans{$\frac13$} \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=redbg, colframe=redacc, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=1.5mm,
    left=2.5mm, right=2.5mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
\textbf{The two mistakes this lesson is made of.} Both are Unit 6 exponent rules, not new ideas.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Power of a power multiplies.} $\left(2^{3}\right)^{x}$ is $2$ to the power
        \ans{$3x$} --- \emph{not} $2^{\,3+x}$. You checked this numerically in the Warm-Up:
        $\left(2^{3}\right)^{4}=4096=2^{12}$, while $2^{3}\cdot2^{4}=128=2^{7}$.
  \item \textbf{Distribute across the \emph{whole} exponent.} $\left(3^{2}\right)^{x+4}=3^{\,2(x+4)}$,
        and $2(x+4)=$ \ans{$2x+8$}. Writing $3^{\,2x+4}$ multiplies the $x$ and forgets the $4$.
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{3. The Method: Three Steps}
\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{0.94\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\textbf{Step 1} & Rewrite \emph{both} sides as powers of one common base. \\
\textbf{Step 2} & The bases match, so set the \ans{exponents} equal to each other. \\
\textbf{Step 3} & Solve what is left --- it is usually a linear equation --- and check. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X X c@{}}
\hline
\textbf{Equation} & \textbf{Step 1: one common base} & \textbf{Step 2: exponents equal} & \textbf{Step 3: $x=$} \\
\hline
\textbf{A.} $2^{\,x+1}=32$ & \ans{$2^{\,x+1}=2^{5}$} & \ans{$x+1=5$} & \ans{4} \\
\textbf{B.} $8^{x}=4$ & \ans{$2^{\,3x}=2^{2}$} & \ans{$3x=2$} & \ans{$\frac23$} \\
\textbf{C.} $3^{x}=\frac{1}{27}$ & \ans{$3^{x}=3^{-3}$} & \ans{$x=-3$} & \ans{$-3$} \\
\textbf{D.} $9^{\,x-2}=27^{x}$ & \ans{$3^{\,2(x-2)}=3^{\,3x}$} & \ans{$2x-4=3x$} & \ans{$-4$} \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Checking B is worth doing out loud.} Its answer is a fraction, which always feels wrong the
first time. Substitute it back with Lesson 6.1's rational exponents:
$8^{\,2/3}=\left(\sqrt[3]{8}\right)^{2}=$ \ans{$2^{2}=4$}, and the right side of B was
\ans{4}. \checkmark

\vspace{2pt}
A non-integer answer is not a red flag. The exponent is a \emph{location on the $x$-axis}, and nothing
requires it to land on a whole number.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{4. The Same Answer, Read Off a Graph}
Every equation above is really a question about where two graphs meet: the curve $y=b^{x}$ and a
horizontal line.
\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.42\linewidth}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.42]
  \lgrid{-3}{4}{-4}{9}
  \begin{scope}
    \clip (-3,-4) rectangle (4,9);
    \expgraph{1}{0.693147}{0}{0}{-3}{4}
  \end{scope}
  \solline{-3}{4}{4}
  \draw[redacc, dashed, very thick] (-3,-3)--(4,-3);
  \node[goldacc, font=\scriptsize, right] at (2.3,4.7) {$y=4$};
  \node[redacc, font=\scriptsize, right] at (2.3,-3.6) {$y=-3$};
  \fill[charcoal] (2,4) circle (3pt) node[above left, font=\scriptsize]{$(2,4)$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}[c]{0.54\linewidth}
\small
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item The gold line meets the curve at $x=$ \ans{2}, the solution of $2^{x}=4$ --- the same
        answer the algebra gave.
  \item This is also the point where $y=2^{x}-4$ crossed the $x$-axis in Lesson 7.2. Then you
        \emph{read} it off a picture; now you can \emph{solve} for it.
  \item The red line $y=-3$ never meets the curve, so $2^{x}=-3$ has \ans{no solution}.
  \item That is not an algebra failure --- it is the \textbf{range}. The range of $y=2^{x}$ is
        \ans{$(0,\infty)$}, and $-3$ is not in it.
\end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
So for $b^{x}=c$: if $c>0$ there is exactly \ans{one} solution; if $c\le0$ there are
\ans{none}. An equation of this shape never has two answers --- that $\pm$ belongs to the parabola
in the Hook, not here.

\vspace{5pt}
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=sky, colframe=navy, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=1.5mm,
    left=2.5mm, right=2.5mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
\textbf{A wall, on purpose.} Look at $2^{x}=5$. The line $y=5$ sits between $y=4$ and $y=8$, so it
\emph{does} cross the curve --- between $x=$ \ans{2} and $x=$ \ans{3}. The solution
exists. But $5$ is not a power of $2$, so Step 1 is impossible and today's method cannot reach it.

\vspace{2pt}
Keep these three. Every one of them has a solution; not one of them has a common base:
\[
  2^{x}=5 \qquad 132(0.85)^{t}=32 \;\;\text{(the coffee)} \qquad (0.9)^{t}=2.118 \;\;\text{(the soda)}
\]
That gap is what Lesson 7.4 walks straight up to, and what Unit 8 finally closes.
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{practicebox}
Solve each equation. Show the common-base step --- the answer alone is not the work.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em, itemsep=9pt]
  \item $5^{x}=125$ \hfill $x=$ \ans{3} \quad \emph{($5^{x}=5^{3}$)}

  \item $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x}=16$ \hfill $x=$ \ans{$-4$} \quad \emph{($2^{-x}=2^{4}$, so $-x=4$)}

  \item $4^{\,x+1}=8$ \hfill $x=$ \ans{$\frac12$} \quad \emph{($2^{\,2x+2}=2^{3}$, so $2x+2=3$)}

  \item Explain why $3^{x}=0$ has no solution. Use the range and the asymptote, not algebra.
        \ansline{The range of $y=3^{x}$ is $(0,\infty)$, so $0$ is not an output the function ever takes.}
        \ansline{The line $y=0$ is the horizontal asymptote: the curve gets closer to it forever and never touches it.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{practicebox}

\begin{teachernote}
\textbf{The Hook is the lesson, and it should take longer than it looks.} Students can solve $2^{x}=4$
by inspection in two seconds; the point is not the answer but the \emph{count}. Do not accept ``because
that's how exponentials are.'' Push to \emph{no turning points} (Lesson 7.0) $\Rightarrow$ no output is
ever repeated $\Rightarrow$ $b^{m}=b^{n}$ forces $m=n$. Every later step in the lesson is licensed by
that sentence, and Unit 8 will re-use it verbatim. Watch for the $\pm$ reflex on item 2 of the practice
box --- it is the Unit 3 habit arriving one family too late.

\textbf{Section 1's $b=1$ line is the payoff for a rule students accepted on faith in 7.1.} They were
told $b\neq1$ because the graph would be flat. Now the restriction earns its keep: $1^{5}=1^{9}$ is a
counterexample to the whole method, and it is the only case where matching bases proves nothing. Write
it on the board; Tier E Part 2 makes students construct it themselves.

\textbf{Rows B and D of the Step table are where the period is won or lost.} B produces $x=\frac23$ ---
run the rational-exponent check in full, because a fractional answer reads as ``I did it wrong'' to most
students, and they will erase correct work. D is the distribution trap: $9^{\,x-2}=3^{\,2(x-2)}=3^{\,2x-4}$,
and a student who writes $3^{\,2x-2}$ gets $x=-2$ and will never see why. Insist that the parentheses
be written before they are removed.

\textbf{Section 4 separates two things that both look like failure.} $2^{x}=-3$ has \emph{no solution}
--- a range fact, settled without algebra. $2^{x}=5$ \emph{has} a solution that this method cannot
reach --- a method fact. Students who blur them will tell you in Unit 8 that logarithms ``fix'' negative
outputs. Say the difference out loud, and leave the three unsolved equations posted; Lesson 7.4 opens
by pointing at them.
\end{teachernote}
